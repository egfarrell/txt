Yes, there is a relationship between the length and amplitude of a pendulum,
although it is somewhat nuanced.

1. _Small-angle approximation_: In the case of a simple pendulum under the
small-angle approximation (where the amplitude, or the maximum angular
displacement, is small), the period of the pendulum is primarily dependent on
the length and not on the amplitude. This approximation assumes that the sine of
the angle is approximately equal to the angle itself when measured in radians
(sin(theta) ≈ theta).

2. _Beyond the small-angle approximation_: When the amplitude is not small,
the period does become dependent on the amplitude. For larger amplitudes, the
pendulum takes longer to complete one full swing. This is because the
approximation that sin(theta) ≈ theta is no longer valid, and the actual path
traveled by the pendulum bob is longer and more complex.

To summarize:

### Under small-angle approximation: 
    - The period \( T \) of a pendulum is given by \( T = 2 \pi
      \sqrt{L / g} \). Here, the period depends only on the
      length \( L \) and the acceleration due to gravity \( g \),
      and is independent of the amplitude.

### For larger amplitudes: 
    - The period \( T \) increases slightly with the amplitude.
      The exact relationship can be derived using elliptic
      integrals, but a rough approximation for small but not
      negligible amplitudes is: \[ T \approx 2 \pi
      \sqrt{\frac{L}{g}} \left(1 + \frac{\theta_0^2}{16}\right)
      \] where \( \theta_0 \) is the maximum angular displacement
      (amplitude) in radians. This shows that as the amplitude
      increases, the period also increases, though the effect is
      relatively small for moderate amplitudes.

- In essence, for SMALL ANGLES, the period is independent of the
  amplitude and only dependent on the length. 

- For LARGER ANGLES, the amplitude does have an effect on the
  period, but this is a more complex relationship.
