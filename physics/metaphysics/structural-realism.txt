### Definition
    - Structural realism argues we don’t have direct knowledge of
      objects as independent units ("monads").
    - Instead, our knowledge is basically about relationships
      between objects, captured in `equations` and
      `interactions`, rather object attributes in isolation.
    - ie: Monadic propositions ("electron has charge `-e`") would
      describe properties of particles as if they exist
      independently of other entities.
    - But Structural Realism says we only know particles (like
      electrons, quarks) through the network of relationships and
      interactions they participate in, rather than as
      standalone, monadic objects.
    - This aligns with Kant, where phenomena are (only) things as
      they APPEAR to us through their `relationships` and
      `effects`, rather than as `things-in-themselves`.

- Structural realism (SR), and structuralism more generally, has
  recently received much attention from philosophers of science.
  There are now as many variants of SR as there are proponents,
  as well as a range of critiques of both individual variants and
  the programme as a whole. 
- Over of eight weeks, this reading group will explore some of
  the more recent developments of SR, especially in its ontic
  variety. 
- We start with an overview of SR in the philosophy of science,
  and then dive into David Wallace's popular `math-first`
  approach that aims to articulate a clear notion of structure
  suited to modern physics. 
- After considering the strengths and limitations of the
  math-first approach, we move via a case study on phlogiston
  into the `real patterns` approach, inspired by Daniel Dennett
  and advocated by James Ladyman. We end with a connection
  between SR and recent `effective` realisms.
