- 'Transcendentalism' has its roots in the work of Immanuel Kant
  and extends to the American transcendentalist movement led by
  thinkers like Emerson and Thoreau.

### Etymology of "Transcendentalism"
    - Latin Roots: The word "transcendental" is derived from the
      Latin *transcendere*, meaning "to climb over" or "to go
      beyond."
    - Philosophical Context: 
        - Kant introduced "transcendental" in a unique sense.
    - For him, transcendental knowledge meant knowledge that goes
      beyond mere EMPIRICAL EXPERIENCE, focusing on the `a
      priori` conditions of human understanding — that is, the
      conditions that allow us to have experiences in the first
      place.
    - So, "transcendental" refers to elements of knowledge or
      thought that transcend particular empirical observations
      but are necessary to understand them.

### Transcendentalism in Philosophy

- In Kantian philosophy, `transcendental concepts` or conditions
  are those that STRUCTURE all possible experience, like space,
  time, and causality.
- Kant’s "transcendental idealism" holds that while we cannot
  know things as they `are` in themselves (noumena), we can know
  things as they `appear` to us, structured by these
  transcendental conditions.

### American Transcendentalism

- The American transcendentalist movement, inspired partly by
  Kant, was more spiritual and emphasized intuition,
  individualism, and the divine immanence in nature.
    - Thinkers like Emerson adapted the idea of "transcendental"
      to mean insights or truths that go beyond material reality
      and reason, accessible through personal intuition and
      reflection.

- In essence, transcendentalism, from Kant's use to the American
  movement, centers around the idea of knowledge, truth, or
  reality that "transcends" ordinary _sensory experience_ and is
  accessible through inner faculties, like intuition or the
  inherent structures of thought.
