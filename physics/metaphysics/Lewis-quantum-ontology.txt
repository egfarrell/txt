### Quantum state
    - If a Quantum state has 4 possibities,
        - then the state vector has 4 numbers in it:
        - `|1 2 3 4>`

    - differential OPERATOR:
        - turns a `function` of `x`
        - into another `function` of `x`

    - ... recall the question that is dodged by the theory:
        - what PHYSICAL STATES do the `non-eigenstates` of
          a given operator represent?
    - With entanglement, we've no physical iterpretation of the
      entangled state `|S>` in terms of the properties of the
      electrons, individually or collectively.
    - Similarly, for interference, no physical interpretation is
      offered of the wave functions involved in the explanation,
      either as waves or as particles or something else entirely.
    - Without a physical interpretation of the quantum state that
      is supposedly doing the explaining, its not clear we have
      an explanation in any sense worthy of the term.

    - But the point I want to stress is that even if we just see
      QM as a useful summary of empirical phenomena, it still
      challenges our classically tutored metaphysical intuitions.
    - There is no obvious way to explain those phenomena in terms
      of the objects and properties we typically take to populate
      the world.
      - Indeed, the challenge to ordinary metaphysics inherent in
        quantum phenomena is really the same difficulty as our
        inability to interpret the theory.

    - Bohr claims that the possession of physical properties
      needs to be re-conceptualised for the quantum realm:
        - QM requires "a radical revision of our attitude towards
          the problem of physical reality".
            - cf: $MY_NOTES/physics/QM/Despagnat-reality.txt
        - The radical revision he has in mind seems to be a kind
          of _contextualism_:
            - The properties of a system depend not only on its
              quantum state but also on its physical environment.
        - E.g., Bohr claims the `z-spin` of an electron is only
          _defined_ when the physical environment of the system
          is such that we could actually measure its `z-spin`.
        - But the physical environment can never be such that we
          can actually measure `z-spin` and `w-spin` at the same
          time, since the measuring magnets need to be aligned
          along different directions in each case.
        - So the `z-spin` and `w-spin` of an electron are never
          simultaneously defined.
        - This allows Bohr to argue QM is complete.
    - We don't need to supplement QM to ascribe both a `z-spin`
      and `w-spin` property to a particle because a particle
      cannot have a spin property along 2 distinct directions
      simultaneously.

    - Bohr calls pairs of properties of this kind, where the
      definability of one precludes the definability of the
      other, complementary properties and he makes
      `complementarity`, the existence of complementary
      properties, the heart of his interpretation of QM.
        - So Bohr had to re-conceptualise physical properties as
          contextual, dependent on the physical context of
          a system, not just on the systems _intrinsic_ state.
        - This is a radical departure from our classical view as
          Bohr admits. It is not clear if such a conception is
          metaphysically tenable:
            - how exactly does the physical environment affect
              which properties a system has?
        - Nor is it clear that it solves `entangled states`.
          Recall that measuring the 1st particle does not
          physically disturb the 2nd electron but how does that
          affect the 2nd electron then?
        - The choice of measurement direction for the first
          electron changes the physical environment for that
          electron, but its hard to see how it can also change
          the physical environment of the 2nd electron, when they
          are spatially separated.

### Double-slit Experiment
    - So far, this is just a classical wave phenomenon. A more
      surprising phenomenon is the production of an interference
      pattern using a stream of electrons instead of a beam of
      light.
        > I never appreciated this fully; that even MATERIAL,
        > PARTICLE electrons interfere! Not just photons!

    - Suppose the light source is replace by an electron gun, and
      the display screen by a fluorescent screen. Then you can
      produce interference effects exactly like that produced by
      light...
    - This behaviour is utterly inexplicable from a classical
      point of view.
    - So perhaps we were wrong about the nature of electrons.
    - Perhaps they are really waves, not particles.
    - This is what de Broglie proposed - that every so-called
      particle can be treated as a wave with wavelength
        - `λ = h/mv`.
    - So far, the explanation of electron interference doesn't
      require any fundamental metaphysical reorientation.
        - Electrons are simply reclassified as waves rather than
          particles.
    - In fact, the situation is far more mysterious than it first
      appears. Suppose we reduce the firing rate of the electron
      gun so they pass through the slits one at a time.
        - If we look at the distribution of flashes over time, we
          see it forms an INTERFERENCE PATTERN!
    - Note the interference pattern is present even though the
      electrons are passing through singly; each electron
      interferes with _itself_ (?) not other electrons.
    - But more problematically, what we see seems to require that
      we treat the electron as _BOTH_ a wave and a particle.
    - The interference pattern apparently requires us to treat
      the electron as a wave...
        - but the discrete flashes on the screen requires that
          each electron behaves as a particle. Und zwar
          a particle passes through one or the other slit!!!
    - The same phenomenon can be observed with light:
        - use the word "photon" to refer to the particle-like
          constituents of light etc...
    - Physicists describe this situation as "wave-particle
      duality" but naming the mystery doesn't make it any less
      mysterious.
    - The pseudo-classical "explanation" of the phenomenon is
      flat-out contradictory, describing the same entity as
      a discrete particle and a spread-out wave.
        - We cannot simply combine elements of our classical
          metaphysical picture to form an inconsistent hybrid.
        - If we are to solve the mystery, it seems we need to
          replace our classical metaphysics with SOMETHING ELSE
          entirely.

    - This all seemed fine until we ran into the recalcitrant
      fact that the speed of light is the same relative to every
      observer. This sets light apart.

    - To accomodate this anomolous behaviour, Einstein suggested
      that a change in our choice of time axis needs to be
      accompanied by a corresponding change in our space axis.
      This results in SR.

    - Figure 5.2, p. 110: If we choose time axis `t'` instead of
      of `t` then we also must switch our space axis:
      - from `x` : the set of points where `t=0`
      - to   `x'`: the set of points where `t'=0`

    - Note the symmetry here:
        - In general the speed of an object depends on the
          coordinate choice, but for an object travelling at
          light speed, the dashed diagonal trajectory, the speed
          is the same in the primed and unprimed coordinates.

    - The light travels far less in the primed coordinates, but
      also takes less time. These factors exactly cancel out.

    - Consider events `A` and `B` in figure 5.2.
        - In one choice of coordinates they happen at the same
          time (have the same `t` coordinates),
        - but under another choice `B` occurs before `A` (they
          have different `t'` coords).
      - Just as "rest" is entirely conventional according to
        Leibniz in classical mechanics, so simultaneity is
        entirely conventional in SR.

    - Now we see why `causal locality` is required by SR.
    - Suppose the motion of a particle depends on the value of
      some DISTANT property _right now_ - e.g. on the mass of
      a distant star.
        - According to SR, whether 2 events are simultaneous is
          a matter of conventional choice (a matter of coordinate
          choice?) and not an objective fact.
    - The motion of a particle here and now cannot depend on
      DISTANT states of affairs, not because such instantaneous
      action at a distance would be "spooky" but because there is
      not objective fact about which  distant states are
      simultaneous with HERE and NOW.
        - Only local causal explanations are allowed, because
          only local causal explanations are _well defined_.

    - How does QM fare against SR?
    - One of Bells assumptions is also "locality". How is this
      assumption related to the causal locality we are
      considering here?
    - Bells locality assumption says that for a pair of spatially
      separated particles, a measurement on one particle cannot
      instantly affect the properties of the other.
        - Clearly any process that allows instantaneous causation
          at a distance is causally nonlocal:
            - The behaviour of the affected particle does not
              depend only on its intrinsic properties (prior to
              measurement) and its immediate environment.
        - So if we want to get around Bell's theorem by violating
          his locality assumption, we need to posit a causally
          nonlocal theory.

    - So at least some ways of getting around Bells theorem are
      in conflict with SR (those that violate the locality
      assumption).
    - If a theory says the subsequent behaviour of an object
      depends on whats going on right now at some distant
      location, then the theory is prima facie incoherent,
      because "right now" cant be defined objectively for
      spatially separated points.
    - This means some `causal` stories that appear to be good
      explanations may turn out to be unacceptable after all.

    - But my question: why would the motion of a particle EVER
      depend on the value of some DISTANT PROPERTY right now?
        - We know because of fields that we're never going to
          have stuff depending on DISTANT objects???
        - So even regardless of the fact that we can't define
          "right now", any motion depending on a distant property
          is SPOOKY anyway...

    - Crux of Bohm analysis:
        - This is an odd situation. Particle 1 is measured as
          "SPIN-DOWN" if its spin is measured _first_, but as
          "SPIN-UP" if it's spin is measured second (after
          paricle 2).
      - But under some coordinate choices, particle 1 will be
        seen as measured first, but under other choices it isn't.
        Since Bohmian dynamics depends on there being such
        a fact, the dynamics is simply ill-defined according to
        SR.

      DIRECTLY ascribe spin properties to particles, only to
      _positions_

    - Interesting point: Bohm isn't actually ruled out by SR. The
      conflict in the previous paragraph isn't actually an
      empirical defect.
    - In Bohms theory, the position of a particle in a wave
      packet cannot in principle be known with more precision
      than the Born Rule stipulates!!!
    - So in this case, there's a 50% probability anyway that
      particle `1` will go up, regardless of whether it's
      measured before or after particle `2`.
    - Became we cant _place_ the particle at a particular
      location in the wave packet, we cannot empirically
      demonstrate the violation of locality shown in figures 5.3,
      5.4

    - Still a conflict is a conflict, even with no empirical
      consequences. It's worth considering what we'd need to make
      Bohmian dynamics well defined.
    - What we'd need is an ABSOLUTE STANDARD OF SIMULTANEITY,
      that we can appeal to in cases like this one to decide
      which measurement was performed first.
        - In terms of figure 5.2, we'd need to pick one axis,
          `t=0` or `t'=0` or some other, as _PRIVILEGED_.
        - (!!! well the One said there's a preferred frame)

    - Woa listen to this: But note that the specification of an
      ABSOLUTE STANDARD OF SIMULTANEITY doesnt affect any
      empirical predictions.
        - Just as in classical mechanics, Newton's concept of
          ABSOLUTE REST has no empirical consequences, so in
          reativistic mechanics, a putative absolute standard of
          simultaneity would have no empirical consequences.
      - Einstein follows Leibniz's reasoning that if an element
        of a physical theory would make no empirical difference

        > - However it is NOT as if SR rules out the existence of
