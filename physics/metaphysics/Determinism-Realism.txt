- In the philosophy of physics, *realism* has more than one
  meaning, and it is distinct from *determinism*, though they can
  sometimes overlap in certain discussions, which can lead to
  some confusion.

### Realism in Physics 
    - In general, realism in the context of physics refers to the
      idea that there exists an `objective reality` independent
      of our observations or theories about it. 
    - However, the term *realism* can take on different nuances
      depending on the specific context:

    1. Ontological Realism: 
        - This is the basic form of realism that asserts that
          physical entities (like particles, fields, or even the
          wave function in QM) have an existence independent of
          our measurements or knowledge of them. In this view,
          the physical world is "out there," whether or not we
          observe it.

    2. Scientific Realism: 
        - This is a broader philosophical position that extends
          ontological realism by claiming that scientific
          theories (or the mature, well-confirmed ones)
          accurately describe the real world. For instance,
          a scientific realist would say that atoms and subatomic
          particles actually exist and behave as described by our
          best theories, even if those theories evolve over time.

    3. Local Realism: 
        - In the context of quantum mechanics, local realism
          refers to the idea that information or influences
          cannot travel faster than the speed of light (locality)
          and that physical properties exist independently of
          measurement (realism). Violations of Bell’s
          inequalities in experiments with entangled particles
          challenge this type of realism, pushing physicists and
          philosophers to reconsider how "realism" applies in
          quantum mechanics.

    4. Wave Function Realism: 
        - Some interpretations of QM propose that the wave
          function is a real, physical object, while others see
          it as a tool for predicting probabilities. 
        - This is a specialized form of realism within the QM
          framework.

### Determinism 
    - Determinism is the concept that all events in the universe,
      including human actions, are the result of preceding
      causes. Deterministic theories imply that, given the state
      of the universe at one time, the future state of the
      universe is completely determined by physical laws. This is
      a separate concept from realism but can sometimes intersect
      with it.

    - Classical Determinism: Classical mechanics is deterministic
      because, if you know the position and velocity of every
      particle, you can predict their future states indefinitely.

    - Quantum Mechanics and Determinism: Quantum mechanics,
      however, is inherently probabilistic (in the standard
      Copenhagen interpretation), and it challenges determinism.
      Some interpretations of quantum mechanics, like the
      *many-worlds interpretation*, aim to preserve a form of
      determinism, while others, like the *Copenhagen
      interpretation*, embrace indeterminacy at the quantum
      level.

### Realism and Determinism in Physics 
    - While realism and determinism are separate, they often
      appear together in discussions about the nature of reality
      in physics:

    - Realistic Determinism: In a classical physics setting,
      realism and determinism naturally go hand-in-hand.
      Particles have real positions and velocities (realism), and
      their future states can be precisely predicted by physical
      laws (determinism).

    - Quantum Mechanics and the Realism-Determinism Tension:
      Quantum mechanics challenges both realism (because the
      existence of physical properties prior to measurement is
      uncertain) and determinism (because of the probabilistic
      nature of quantum outcomes). Some interpretations of QM
      (like *Bohmian mechanics*) try to maintain both realism and
      determinism, while others (like the *Copenhagen
      interpretation*) _reject both_, at least at the level of
      individual quantum events.

### Summary In short:
    - Realism in physics means believing in an objective reality
      independent of observation, but it has different versions
      (ontological, scientific, local, etc.).
    - Determinism is the idea that all events are causally
      determined by prior states.
    - They are distinct ideas: realism pertains to the existence
      of an objective reality, while determinism concerns the
      predictability of that reality.
    - In classical physics, realism and determinism align well.
      In quantum mechanics, they often conflict, leading to
      debates and various interpretations to reconcile or abandon
      one or both.

- So, while realism and determinism are sometimes associated,
  they are not synonymous, and one can adopt one without
  necessarily committing to the other.
