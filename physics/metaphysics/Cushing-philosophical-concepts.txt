- Why does the QM formalism make it so difficult to describe
  physical phenomena in a way we understand?

- The key problem is the `entanglement` (nonseparability) of
  quantum states that gives rise to:
    - the measurement problem
    - to nonlocality
        - (for example, the long-range quantum correlations, such
          as those of Eq. (22.7)).
    - This entanglement makes it impossible to assign
      _independent properties_ to an arbitrary isolated physical
      system once it has interacted with another system in the
      past, even though these two sytems are no longer
      interacting.

- Within standard QM, there seems to be no way to account for the
  classical behavior of everyday objects that do, in fact,
  exhibit such possessed properties.

- Some claim we need a new concept of _causality_, but it's not
  clear what that should be.
    - Heisenberg suggested we introduce a new class of physical
      entity, `potentia`, into our theories and ontology.
    - This move tries to explain how definite results appear or
      actualize in a single localized region of space-time.
    - It attempts to cope with the reduction of the wave packet
      (see Eq. (21.9)) which is the most basic problem for the QM
      formalism (as standardly interpreted).

- A different approach says that, since direct, local causal
  links and local common causes are impossible for quantum
  correlations, these correlations themselves should be taken as
  the `irreducible brute facts` of primitive givens, upon which
  we build any further explanations.
    - This would sidestep the issue of understanding.
    - One characterization of QM is:
        - 'It [QM] is... the blackest of black-box theories;
          a marvelous predictor but an incompetent explainer.'
    - Such an attempt to place the brute facts first, as a basis
      for an explanation, is not inconsistent with a claim that
      we cannot understand quantum phenomena.
    - So, if we must wholeheartedly buy into an indeterministic
      worldview, it may be that we cannot understand this world
      view anyway.

- However, by taking `position` as the only possessed property
  (or only observable) and by accepting the inseparable
  nonlocality of quantum phenomena, _Bohm's theory_ avoids the
  measurement problem.
    - It also has an ontology that produces no conceptual rift
      with our classical representations of a real world with
      objectively possessed properties.

- This yields a specific, picturable, more traditional (almost
  'classical') interpretation of QM.
    - It is completely deterministic in which particles have
      objectively existing trajectories at all times and they
      interact via a `quantum potential`.
    - The price is nonlocality (direct, instantaneous, long-range
      interactions), but of a fairly benign variety that produces
      _no empirical conflict with relativity_. (? wow)
    - One could claim this model produces more of a sense of
      understanding than other attempts just mentioned.

- Let's summarize the problem Duhem-Quine `underdetermination`
  poses for the scientific realist:
    - If there are two empirically-adequate theories, agreeing on
      all empirical tests and being observationally
      indistinguishable, but they support radically incompatible
      ontologies, then such a situation may frustrate the
      scientific realist searching for the correct scientific
      theory giving a true picture of the world.
    - As long as the two theories differ in relatively minor
      respects regarding their ontologies, one can simply decide
      to bracket these as inessential differences.
    - The case we have been discussing involves one theory that
      represents fundamental physical processes as inherently and
      irreducibly `indeterministic` and another theory based on
      absolutely deterministic behavior in the physical universe.
      This doesn't appear to be a minor or irrelevant difference.

- This situation represents a double threat to the scientific
  realist. Firstly, the standard Copenhagen interpretation is
  often seen as a challenge to a realistic construal of QM.
    - The core difficulty is the measurement problem, one
      consequence of which is that a physical system cannot, even
      in principle, possess definite, but merely unknown to us,
      values of all physically observable attributes (such as
      position, velocity, all components of the spin, etc.).
        - I.e., a simple 'ignorance' interpretation is not
          consistent with the assumed completeness of QM.
    - Accepting standard QM as an actual representation of the
      physical world at the level of individual micro-entities
      requires we accept a rather bizzare ontology (if, indeed,
      it's a genuine ontology at all).
    - The measurement problem has been around for years (since
      1930) and has resolutely defied any generally accepted
      solution.
    - This provides ammunition for the _antirealist_ who begins
      at the level of micro-phenomena, accepts Copenhagen QM as
      the fundamental theory of physical processes, and throws
      down the challenge to the realist to construct a coherent
      ontology consistent with QM.
    - Such an _antirealist_ begins their argument in the
      microrealm, extrapolates to the macrorealm of everyday
      experience and leaves the ensuing conundrum at the doorstep
      of the realist.

- Apropos of this, Schrodinger said it is impossible to decide on
  the basis of observation alone whether the world is basically
  deterministic or indeterministic.
    - Which description one uses is a purely pragmatic matter,
      dictated by convenience.
    - In his 1929 inaugural address at the Prussian Academy of
      Sciences, Schrodinger claimed:
        - 'The most that can be decided [on this issue of
          `determinism` versus `indeterminism`] is whether the
          one or the other leads to a simpler and clearer survey
          of all the observed facts'.
        - So, the scientific realist could write off the
          difference between indeterminism and determinism in the
          ontology of the world as an insignificance, but that
          would be strange for one concerned with a reliable and
          meaningfully complete picture of the world.

- De Broglie felt that classical `Hamilton-Jacobi theory`
  provided an embryonic theory of the union of waves and
  particles, in a manner consistent with a 'realist' conception
  of matter.
    - With the concept of the _quantum potential_, one could
      envisage a model for fundamental processes.
    - De Broglie's commitment to a realistic interpretation of QM
      is consistent with his high estimate of philosopher Emile
      Meyerson, as stated by de Broglie in a preface to
      Meyerson's 'Essais' (1936).
    - Meyerson attempted to dispel the positivist bias and held
      that the goal of science is an ontological one.

- It is useful to review Einstein's central philosophical
  commitments.
    - In Section 19.5 we saw his view of the physical world was
      a rational, _causal_ one.
    - He had a strong belief in a `realistic` world view in which
      microentities have a continuous, objective,
      observer-independent existence.
    - Two other core commitments for him were:
        1. event-by-event causality (essentially determinism) and
        2. locality.
    - These were three desiderata he felt any fundamental
      physical theory should satisfy.
    - Now the entanglement or nonlocality of this stochastic
      mechanics formalism would soon have become apparent.
        - Einstein would have rejected any model with this
          property. Now let us return to our counterfactual
          scenario.

- Albert Einstein, Reply to Criticisms:
    - However scientists cannot afford to carry their striving
      for epistemological systematic [too] far.
    - They gratefully accept epistemological conceptual analysis;
      but the external conditions, which are set by the facts of
      experience, do not permit them to be too restricted in the
      construction of their conceptual world by adherence to an
      epistemological system.
    - Therefore they must appear to a systematic epistemologist
      as a type of unscrupulous opportunist:
        - They appear as _realist_ insofar as they seek to
          describe a world independent of the acts of perception;
        - as _idealist_ insofar as they look upon the concepts
          and theories as the free inventions of the human spirit
          (not logically derivable from what is empirically
          given);
        - as _positivist_ insofar as they consider their concepts
          and theories justifed only to the extent to which they
          furnish a logical representation of relations among
          sensory experiences.
        - They may even appear as _Platonist_ or Pythagorean
          insofar as they consider the viewpoint of logical
          simplicity as an indispensable and effective tool of
          research.

    - The theoretical physicist builds idealized models that can
      be treated precisely mathematically.
    - Such models can never give a complete description of
      reality, for they would be far too complicated to treat in
      full mathematical detail.
    - The _real art_ in building or conceiving such models is to
      incorporate a few of the truly essential properties of the
      real system so the model will still mirror reality well
      enough to be able to make predictions of physical interest.
      (upon suitable mathematical analysis)
    - One difference between the 'games' played by theoretical
      physicists and those played by pure mathematicians is that,
      aside from meeting the demands of internal consistency and
      mathematical rigor, a physical model must ultimately meet
      the inflexible boundary condition of agreeing with physical
      reality.
    - A simple example of a model is an 'ideal gas' consisting of
      point molecules in the kinetic theory of gases.
    - We are able to treat that model fairly completely with just
      elementary mathematics and thereby explain the gross
      properties of real gases over a wide range of pressures and
      temperatures.
    - Still, the model is not a perfect reflection of reality and
      ceases to be valid at high densities for example.
    - Einstein emphasized that, as physical models and theories
      manage to organize more and more phenomena of reality in
      terms of fewer and fewer premises, they become ever more
      abstract.

- Poincare took a very different position, however.
    - Although impressed with the philosophical criticisms of
      Newtonian physics by Kirchhoff, Hertz and Mach, he felt
      that the general laws of physics could not be simply
      _direct summaries_ of observations and data, because the
      distance from immediate sense perceptions to these abstract
      laws is so great.
    - Poincare's writings on this subject were very influential
      and led to what is termed "_conventionalism_".
    - This sees the principles of science as free creations of
      the human mind that are neither true nor false 'a priori',
      but which may be useful in organizing the experiences of
      reality.
    - For example, the axioms of Euclidean or any non-Euclidean
      geometry need not be true in the real world.
        - However, once we establish the convention that
          a physical light ray travels along a straight line (or
          a geodesic), then one of these systems becomes relevant
          to physical phenomena.
    - Similarly, Poincare viewed the Law of Inertia as
      a `convention` or `definition` stipulating that uniform,
      straight-line motion was the criterion for force-free
      motion.
        - (Recall Poincare's analysis of the logical structure of
          classical mechanics in Section 7.5.)
        - We will see that the principles of mechanics, although
          more directly based on experience, still share the
          `conventional` character of the geometrical postulates.
    - With regard to hypotheses that are really conventions,
      Poincare wrote:
        - Such conventions are the result of the unrestricted
          activity of the mind, which in this domain recognizes
          no obstacle. But let us clearly understand that while
          these laws are imposed on our science,... they are not
          imposed on Nature.
        - Are they then arbitrary? No, for if they were, they
          would not be fertile. Experience leaves us our freedom
          of choice, but it guides us by helping us to discern
          the most convenient path to follow.

- The French theoretical physicist-philosopher `Duhem` also
  believed the aim of physical theories is to offer a condensed
  representation of phenomena, rather than to give an explanation
  of them in terms of 'true' theoretical constructs.
