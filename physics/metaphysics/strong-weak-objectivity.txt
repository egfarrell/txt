
### Strong objectivity

- A theory or statement is strongly objective when it can be read
  as describing how the world is *in itself*, independently of
  observers, measuring devices, or information-gathering
  conditions.

- In strong objectivity, statements have the form “System X has
  property P,” where P is intended as a mind-independent feature
  of reality.

- Classical mechanics, classical EM, and GR are usually taken to
  have this status: their core claims are meant as literal
  descriptions of the physical world.

Key characteristics:
- claims refer to properties of systems themselves, NOT to
  observations
- meaning does not depend on how the system is probed
- truth conditions do not involve observers or preparation
  procedures

### Weak objectivity

- A theory or statement is weakly objective when it makes claims
  that are objectively *testable* and publicly *agreeable*, but
  whose meaning essentially depends on observational or
  preparational conditions. The statements are not
  straightforwardly about “how things are,” but about what any
  observer would *obtain* in specific experimental contexts.

- Standard textbox QM and much of statistical mechanics fit this
  category: their basic laws refer to measurement outcomes,
  ensembles, coarse-graining procedures, or preparation
  protocols.

Key characteristics:
- claims concern what is observed, recorded, or statistically obtained
- meaning depends on defined measurement or preparation procedures
- truth is still objective in the sense of intersubjective agreement, but not clearly about observer-independent reality

## 2. Who else uses or examines these concepts?

- D’Espagnat coined the strong/weak objectivity terminology, but
  the underlying distinction has close parallels in several
  philosophical and physical discussions. 

### Concept in Physics (foundations of QM)

- Niels Bohr
    - Although he didn’t use “weak objectivity,” his emphasis on
      classical descriptions of measurement outcomes and the
      contextual nature of quantum properties lines up closely
      with D’Espagnat’s weak objectivity.

- Werner Heisenberg and the Copenhagen tradition
    - The insistence that quantum theory describes “phenomena”
      (observer-accessible facts) rather than intrinsic
      microscopic properties is effectively a weak-objectivist
      stance.

- John Wheeler
    - His “no phenomenon is a phenomenon until it is an observed
      phenomenon” formulation aligns strongly with weak
      objectivity.

- QBism (Fuchs, Mermin, Schack)
    - Much more radical, but it also rejects strong objectivity
      for quantum states. Their work often discusses the notion
      of objectivity explicitly, though not with D’Espagnat’s
      terminology.

- Relational quantum mechanics (Rovelli)
    - This is another alternative to strong objectivity, treating
      properties as relative to observers or systems.

- Operational quantum foundations 
    - (e.g., works by Chiribella, Hardy, Spekkens)
    - These frameworks emphasize laboratory operations,
      preparation/measurement procedures, and intersubjective
      agreement. They embody weak objectivity.

### Concept in Philosophy of physics

- Although D’Espagnat’s phrasing is not widely adopted, the
  distinction reappears under other labels:

- Ontic vs epistemic readings of theories
    - Ontic 
        - strong objectivity (theory describes reality)
    - Epistemic 
        - weak objectivity (theory describes knowledge,
          experience, operations)

- Realism vs operationalism / empiricism
    - Operationalism (Percy Bridgman), logical empiricism
      (Carnap, Reichenbach), and van Fraassen’s `constructive
      empiricism` all align with weak objectivity.

- Structural realist debates (Worrall, Ladyman)
    - Structural realism keeps some strong-objectivist ambitions
      (structure is real) while acknowledging that our access is
      limited—echoing D’Espagnat’s “veiled” reality.

- Michel Bitbol
    - Discusses objectivity in quantum mechanics in ways strongly
      resonant with D’Espagnat’s distinctions.

### In textbooks or research literature

The terms *strong* and *weak* objectivity themselves appear mostly in:
• D’Espagnat’s own papers/books
• A small set of quantum foundations discussions directly influenced by him

But conceptually equivalent distinctions are extremely common in:
• discussions of measurement in QM
• operational vs ontological models (Spekkens, Harrigan & Spekkens, Leifer)
• the PBR theorem literature (differentiating ontic and epistemic state models)


## Summary

Strong objectivity = claims describing observer-independent reality.
Weak objectivity = claims describing universally agreed-upon observational outcomes, not necessarily reality “as it is”.

D’Espagnat’s terms are not widely adopted verbatim, but the distinction itself is central in quantum foundations, operational approaches, and much of philosophy of science. Multiple major physicists and philosophers work with equivalent ideas under different names.
