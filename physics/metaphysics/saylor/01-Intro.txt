### Unit 1: What Is Philosophy of Science?
- “Philosophy of science is about as useful to scientists as
  ornithology is to birds.”
    - Attributed to Richard Feynman in *Science Askew:
      A Light-Hearted Look at the Scientific World* (2002)

- Of all the intellectual disciplines, science makes the
  strongest claims to provide us with knowledge of the way the
  world is.
- But how does science work? What do scientists do that makes
  their research particularly worthy of our attention? Why do
  they pursue some inquiries rather than others? And does
  science really live up to its claims to provide us with
  objective and rational knowledge?
- Answering these questions involves attention to scientific
  practice and the results of scientific research, as well as
  familiarity with past and present episodes of scientific
  inquiry.
- But beyond such sociological and historical information,
  answering this requires subsuming the myriad details of
  scientific research and practice under general concepts and
  judging them in light of evaluative norms. These further tasks
  fall within the province of the 'Philosophy of Science'.



### Unit 1: Learning Outcomes

    - Upon completion of this unit, the student will be able to:
        - List some standard opinions from scientists about
          scientific practice and scientific knowledge.
        - Summarize some opinions from scientists about the
          relevance of philosophy to scientific practice.
        - Distinguish _philosophical_ questions about science
          from psychological, sociological, and historical ones.
        - Identify questions about science that fall within the
          province of philosophy of science.

### 1.2 Is Philosophy Relevant to Science?
    - Reading: Philosophy Now:
        - Mike Adler’s “Newton’s Flaming Laser Sword”
        - $MY_NOTES/physics/metaphysics/saylor/Newtons-Laser-Sword.txt

    - With his version of the scientific method, mathematician
      Mike Adler represents a common opinion of scientists toward
      philosophy.
    - What reasons does Adler give for supposing that philosophy
      is not a reliable method for gaining access to truths about
      the world? Why does he think science offers a better
      method?

    - Reading: NPR: Alva Noë’s “A Little Philosophy is
      a Dangerous Thing”
        - $MY_NOTES/physics/metaphysics/saylor/little-philosophy-dangerous.txt

    - Noë identifies certain problems that scientists are not
      able to solve by doing science.
        - What are some distinctively philosophical problems,
          prompted by science, that Noë identifies?
        - The core problem Noë identifies—that scientists cannot
          solve by doing more science—is the problem of
          _reconciling_ scientific knowledge with our _everyday
          experience_ of the world.
        - Specifically:
            - The conflict between `appearance and reality`:
                - Science (especially physics) tells us the table
                  in front of us is a cloud of particles, devoid
                  of color or solidity.
                - Yet we perceive it as brown, solid, and
                  rectangular.
                - This _gap_ between how things seem and what
                  science says they are is not something science
                  can resolve internally—it’s about what science
                  means and how it relates to human experience.
            - The meaning and interpretation of `scientific
              models`:
                - Hawking and Mlodinow suggest “model-dependent
                  realism”—that it's pointless to ask if a model
                  is "real", only whether it agrees with
                  observation.
                - But Noë points out that observation itself
                  depends on interpretation, interest, and
                  theoretical framing.
                - This leads to philosophical questions about:
                    - What counts as observation?
                    - What is reality?
                    - What's the relationship between models and
                      the world?
            - Understanding the status of `science itself`:
                - The question "What is science telling us about
                  reality?" isn’t a scientific question; it’s
                  about science—its assumptions, implications,
                  and limits.
                - Philosophers call this a `meta question`.
                - These aren't testable or empirical in the same
                  way scientific questions are.

    - He also identifies certain philosophical ideas that
      scientists may take for granted without realizing their
      complexity.
    - Question: What are the philosophical ideas that he believes
      some scientists have adopted uncritically?
        - Well Noë criticizes Hawking and Mlodinow for relying on
          philosophical ideas without seeming to recognize that
          they _are_ philosophical, or appreciating their depth
          and historical complexity.
        - `Model-dependent realism`:
            - Hawking and Mlodinow suggest that asking whether
              a model is really true is pointless—what matters is
              whether it agrees with observation.
            - Noë points out this is not a purely scientific
              claim. It’s a philosophical stance about what truth
              and reality mean—rooted in ideas from earlier
              thinkers like the logical positivists (Carnap) and
              even David Hume.
            - They seem unaware this view has been widely
              debated, and criticized within philosophy.
        - `Theory-ladenness of observation:`
            - They assume observations are neutral and
              self-evident, but Noë emphasizes that even what we
              “observe” is shaped by our prior beliefs,
              expectations, and conceptual frameworks.
            - This is a central issue in _epistemology_ (theory
              of knowledge), going back at least to Kant, and
              explored in 20th-century philosophy by thinkers
              like Kuhn.
        - `The mind as a model-builder:`
            - The idea the `mind` constructs a model of the world
              from raw sensory data is itself a philosophical
              theory—commonly associated with `empiricism`.
            - Noë sees this as a reductionist view of experience
              and perception, one that overlooks alternative
              philosophical approaches to consciousness,
              selfhood, and embodiment.
    - Finally, relate Noë’s opinions about the relation of
      science and philosophy to Adler’s opinions in the previous
      reading.
