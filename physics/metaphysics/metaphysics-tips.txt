### Philosophy of Maths: Shadows of Syntax
    - Shadows of Syntax: Revitalizing Logical and Mathematical
      Conventionalism 
    - https://www.amazon.co.uk/Shadows-Syntax-Revitalizing-Mathematical-Conventionalism/dp/0190086157
    - recommended on Robinson Erhardt podcast
        - https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q_vC073Re5A
        - Jacob Barandes: A New Foundation for Quantum Mechanics
    - Thesis:
        - Logical and mathematical truths are not facts about
          reality, but arise from our linguistic conventions
          (rules of language use).
        - These truths are “shadows of syntax” — they reflect the
          rules governing how we use symbols and make inferences.
        - This view aims to avoid:
            - Platonism (math truths exist independently of us)
            - Kantian-style a priori faculties
        - Instead, it fits into a naturalistic, empiricist
          worldview
    - key moves:
        - Truth = use
            - Meaning and truth come from how we use symbols in inference
        - No need for:
            - Abstract objects
            - External “truth-makers”

### haecceitist
    - A haecceitist difference is a difference in IDENTITY, not
      in PROPERTIES.
    - Two situations differ haecceitistically when all
      qualitative facts are the same, but a different
      `individual` occupies the role.
    - Basically Leibnitz 'Identity of indiscernibles' is
      anti-haecceitist.

### Underdeterminism from symmetry
    - $MY_NOTES/physics/metaphysics/underdeterminism-v-indeterminism.txt
    - The problem is this: certain symmetries imply redundancies,
      and this leads to an inability of the theory to ‘choose’
      between states or configurations related by the symmetry.
    - In certain cases this leads to `indeterminism`, but in
      general we have an `underdetermination` of the state by the
      theory. 
    - The connection of such matters to ontological issues are
      old news: the debate between Newton and Leibniz over the
      nature of space and time was one such underdetermination
      problem (the ‘shift argument’). 
    - Two sides were carved out of this debate: 
        - absolutism (or ‘`absolutist substantivalism`’), which
          accepted the existence of multiple physical states that
          the theory could not distinguish between (these states
          differing solely haecceitistically), and 
        - `relationism`, which denied such a profusion. 
    - See also the 'hole argument'

    - As is well known, mutatis mutandis, the hole argument, as
      revived by `Σi John i` (`i` = Stachel, Earman, and Norton)
      hangs on the same choice, though here the
      underdetermination of states by theory is tantamount to
      indeterminism; the latter being understood as an
      unpalatable consequence of substantivalism, a mere
      metaphysical position.


### Strong/Weak objectivity
    - $MY_NOTES/physics/metaphysics/strong-weak-objectivity.txt
    - STRONG: A theory or statement is strongly objective when it
      can be read as describing how the world is *in itself*,
      independently of observers, measuring devices, or
      information-gathering conditions.
        - In strong objectivity, statements have the form “System
          X has property `P`,” where `P` is intended as
          a mind-independent feature of reality.
    - WEAK: A theory or statement is weakly objective when it
      makes claims that are objectively *testable* and publicly
      *agreeable*, but whose meaning essentially depends on
      observational or preparational conditions. The statements
      are not straightforwardly about “how things are,” but about
      what any observer would *obtain* in specific experimental
      contexts.
        - Standard textbox QM and much of statistical mechanics
          fit this category: their basic laws refer to
          measurement outcomes, ensembles, coarse-graining
          procedures, or preparation protocols.

### Symmetry: condense info
    - $MY_NOTES/physics/People/My-life-quant.txt

      part of an object from another. It condenses information.
        - Saying that a human face is symmetrical is another way
          of saying you don't need to draw both sides of it;

          the left and right sides.


### Scientific Explanations
    - `Reductionist` explanations:
        - focus on breaking complex phenomena down to their
          simplest components and understanding the system by
          analyzing these parts. 
        - Eg, explaining the behavior of a gas by studying the
          motion of individual molecules is reductionist.
    - `Functionalist` explanations:
        - focus on the role or _purpose_ of a phenomenon within
          a larger system. Instead of looking only at the parts,
          functionalism asks 
            - "what does this do?" or 
            - "what role does it play?" 
        - Eg, in biology, explaining the heart by its function of
          pumping blood, rather than by the mechanics of
          individual cells, is functionalist.

      that can happen does happen (or at least everything that is
      not forbidden by some law happens). It has many variants.

      philosophers of possibility, some speculative physics, etc.

### mereology (parts)
    - From the Greek word `méros`, meaning "part," combined with
      suffix `-logy`, from Greek 'logos', meaning "study,"
      "discourse," or "reason." Think:
        - isomer: compounds with the same parts (atoms)
        - polymer: compounds with many parts
    - Mereology means "the study of parts" or "the discourse on
      parts."
    - `Mereology` is the study of _part-whole_ relations, and how
      the structure of the universe at different levels, such as
      subatomic particles, molecules, and macroscopic systems,
      fits into a coherent ontological framework.
    - `Mereological` speculation explores how parts relate to
      wholes in the physical world, often in relation to
      foundational theories like QM, GR, or emergent phenomena.

    - Mereological speculation:
        1. Composition and Reductionism: (Emergence)
            - Are larger-scale entities _reducible_ to their
              constituent parts?
            - E.g., can the behavior of a molecule be fully
              explained by understanding its atoms, or does it
              have emergent properties that require a different
              conceptual framework?
        2. Fundamentality of Parts or Wholes?
            - Does physics suggest that "ultimate reality"
              consists of fundamental particles (parts), or are
              larger systems (wholes) ontologically _primary_?
                - E.g., Quantum entanglement challenges
                  traditional notions by showing that the
                  properties of a system may not be attributable
                  to its individual parts _alone_.
        3. Ontological Status of Boundaries:
            - Do the boundaries between parts and wholes in
              physical systems objectively exist, or are they
              observer-dependent abstractions?
                - This is especially relevant in QFT, where
                  `particles` and `fields` blur traditional
                  part-whole distinctions.
        4. Non-locality and Mereological Challenges:
            - Phenomena like `QM entanglement` suggest that
              non-local connections can challenge classical
              mereology, which assumes that parts are spatially
              localized.

        - Mereological debates:
            - Whether the universe is fundamentally
                - discrete (has smallest indivisible parts) or
                - continuous (with infinitely divisible wholes).
            - How to reconcile `theories` applying at different
              scales (e.g., QM and GR) within a unified
              part-whole framework.
            - The role of the `observer` in defining parts and
              wholes, particularly in quantum measurements.

        - Mereology is vital for understanding the metaphysical
          implications of physical theories and developing
          a coherent ontology that aligns with our best
          understanding of the universe.

### Monadic and Structural Realism
    - $MY_NOTES/physics/QM/dEspagnat-paper-Reality.txt
    - Despagnat:
        > This reminds us of _structural realism_, the idea,
        > already present in Poincare's work, that we only know
        > the _RELATIONSHIPS_ between objects (the equations),
        > not the objects themselves. One implication is that the
        > monadic(?) propositions of contemporary physics when
        > dealing with electrons, quarks etc merely describe
        > _PHENOMENA_ in the Kantian sense of the word.
    - In this context, `monadic` refers to statements that
      describe objects in _isolation_, without reference to their
      relationships or interactions with other things.
    - It comes from Greek root `monas`, meaning "unit" or "one".
    - Here, "monadic" implies descriptions that focus on
      individual entities (like electrons or quarks) in
      a self-contained way, as if they have independent,
      standalone attributes.
    - Why "Monadic" is Important in Structural Realism
        - Structural realism argues we don’t have direct
          knowledge of objects as independent units ("monads").
        - Instead, our knowledge is basically about relationships
          between objects, captured in `equations` and
          `interactions`, rather object attributes in isolation.
        - In other words:
            - Monadic propositions ("electron has charge `-e`")
              would describe properties of particles as if they
              exist independently of other entities.
            - But Structural Realism says we only know particles
              (like electrons, quarks) through the network of
              relationships and interactions they participate in,
              rather than as standalone, monadic objects.
            - This aligns with Kant, where phenomena are (only)
              things as they APPEAR to us through their
              `relationships` and `effects`, rather than as
              `things-in-themselves`.
    - So Structural Realism posits our knowledge consists of
      structures or relations rather than monadic, isolated facts
      about individual objects.

### Conventional Realism vs. Open Realism
    - Two forms of realism !
    - Conventional realism assumes there's a mind-independent
      reality that is _KNOWABLE_, at least in principle.
    - Open Realism suggests a mind-independent reality exists but
      may be _fundamentally UNKNOWABLE_.
    - This distinction allows for a version of realism where
      human beings cannot fully comprehend this reality.

### Transcendentalism v Conventional Realism
    - Divide between them:

    - Background:
        - ... transcendental knowledge means knowledge that goes
          beyond mere EMPIRICAL EXPERIENCE, focusing on the `a
          priori` conditions of human understanding — i.e., the
          conditions that allow us to have experiences in the
          first place.
        - $MY_NOTES/physics/metaphysics/Transcendentalism.txt

    - ### Transcendentalism
        - This is the view that human cognition has _inherent
          limits_ when it comes to accessing or fully
          understanding mind-independent reality (the
          `thing-in-itself`).
        - It was popularized by philosophers like Kant, who
          argued that the mind shapes what we perceive and
          understand, but only within certain `boundaries`.
        - In other words, even if an objective, independent
          reality exists, our mental frameworks—our senses,
          concepts, and ways of thinking—will always restrict our
          understanding of it.
            - We can only know how things _appear_ to us, not how
              they truly are in themselves, beyond our
              perception.
    - In transcendentalism:
        - Humans are only able to perceive and understand reality
          _filtered_ through our mental limitations.
        - There’s a permanent gap between what reality `is` and
          what we can `know` about it.
        - Human knowledge will always be limited to our
          experiences and perceptions, meaning we may never fully
          grasp reality as it actually exists.

    - ### Conventional Realism
        - This holds we *can* eventually achieve a true and
          complete understanding of mind-independent reality.
        - This view asserts that, with enough knowledge,
          observation, and scientific advancement, humans can
          uncover reality as it objectively exists, independent
          of our perceptions or limitations.
        - In other words, conventional realism believes:
            - There is an objective reality outside of us, and
              humans have the potential to describe it
              accurately.
            - Science and observation can ultimately reveal the
              true nature of reality, without being limited by
              our own subjective perspective.
            - Any current limitations are only temporary hurdles;
              with time, we’ll gain a complete picture of how
              things _really_ are.

    - ### Key Difference
        - Transcendentalism argues we’re fundamentally *unable*
          to know reality as it truly "is" because of our
          cognitive limitations.
        - But conventional realism believes these limitations are
          temporary, and with enough effort and knowledge, we
          *can* fully understand objective reality.


### Mutatis mutandis (Latin)
    - The things needing to be changed (mutandis) having been
      changed (Mutatis).
        - "the necessary adjustments having being made."
        - ... "making the mutations you must."
    - Used to indicate that a statement or situation is similar
      to another with appropriate modifications.
    - It’s like saying "the same, but with appropriate
      differences accounted for."
    - _mutatis mutandis_ is used when extending principles or
      equations from one context to another, where adjustments
      are necessary due to differing parameters or boundary
      conditions. Eg:
    - Classical to Quantum Mechanics:
        - When comparing a classical concept, like the energy of
          a particle in a potential field, to its quantum
          counterpart, we say that "the principles of energy
          conservation apply in QM, mutatis mutandis,"
          acknowledging that while the basic idea is similar, we
          need to account for quantum-specific aspects like
          wavefunctions and probabilistic interpretation rather
          than deterministic trajectories.
    - SR to GR:
        - If explaining how SR’s concepts apply to GR, we might
          say: "The concepts of time dilation and length
          contraction in SR apply in GR, mutatis mutandis."
        - This means these principles still hold, but they
          require modification to account for curved spacetime
          and gravitational effects, unlike in the flat spacetime
          of SR.
    - Newtonian to Relativistic Physics:
        - When moving from Newtonian physics to Einstein’s theory
          of relativity, one might state:
            - "Newton’s laws of motion can describe high-speed
              particles, mutatis mutandis."
        - This signals that Newton’s laws need to be modified to
          include relativistic corrections (e.g Lorentz
          transformations) for accuracy at speeds near the speed
          of light.
    - In each case, _mutatis mutandis_ acknowledges the analogy
      but emphasizes that essential adjustments are needed due to
      the different physical frameworks.

### Hermeneutics (interpretation)
    - This is the theory and methodology of `interpretation`,
      especially of biblical texts, wisdom literature, and
      philosophical texts.
        - Stemming from `Hermes` - the Greek god of
          communication, messages, language, and... interpreting
          the will of the gods. Messenger of the Gods.
        - Hermeneutics: the art of Hermes.
    - Hermeneutics involves understanding more than the literal
      meaning of a text, but also its broader context, purpose,
      and implications. Key aspects:
        1. Historical Context:
            - Understanding the time, culture, and circumstances
              when a text was written.
        2. Authorial Intent:
            - Understand the intentions and motivations of the
              author.
        3. Textual Analysis:
            - Analyzing the structure, language, and literary
              devices used in the text.
        4. Reader's Perspective:
            - Consider how different readers might interpret the
              text based on their own backgrounds and
              experiences.
        5. Philosophical Hermeneutics:
            - A broader approach which interprets not just texts
              but also human experiences and social practices.

    - Key figures include:
        - Friedrich Schleiermacher:
            - Considered the father of modern hermeneutics, he
              emphasized the importance of understanding the
              author's intent and the historical context.
        - Hans-Georg Gadamer:
            - Expanded hermeneutics to include the concept of the
              "fusion of horizons," where the interpreter's
              perspective interacts with the historical context
              of the text.
        - Paul Ricoeur:
            - Integrated hermeneutics with phenomenology,
              focusing on the interpretation of symbols and
              narratives.
                - Phenomenology see:
                  $MY_NOTES/physics/metaphysics/philosophy-Moone-Bolton-2009.txt

    - Hermeneutics is applied in fields such as theology,
      philosophy, law, and literary studies.
        - It provides tools and methods for interpreting complex
          and layered texts, allowing for a deeper understanding
          of their meanings and implications.

### GR isn't the final story? "Under-determination"?
    - Audio 2019-05-06
    - when Einstein published GR, Moritz Schlich came up with the
      idea that there's a one-way coordination between the theory
      and the world and it goes from the theory to the world but
      the same coordination doesn't go from the world back to the
      theory. What Don Howard is really saying is that Schlich
      was actually arguing for some kind of under-determination
      a bit like Dunheim.
    - Alternative 1:
        - Schlick thought the theory gives us a `map` of how the
          world works, but the world doesn’t give us only one
          possible map in return.
        - So different theories (or different versions) might all
          match what we observe, at least for now.
    - Alternative 2:
        - Schlick thought about how GR relates to the real world.
        - He said GR tells us how the world behaves – there's
          a one-way link from GR to the world.
        - But he pointed out that the reverse isn't true: the
          world doesn't fully determine the theory.
            - You can’t always go from the world back to the
              exact same theory.
        - This is "under-determination": different theories might
          explain the same observations.
        - Howard interprets Schlick as arguing for
          under-determination: similar to what Pierre Duhem
          argued earlier.

### deductive-nomological model
    - The deductive-nomological model of explanation is
      a framework for explaining `phenomena`.
    - Developed by Carl Hempel & Paul Oppenheim in the 1940s.
    - Also known as `the D-N model`.
    - Aside:
        - "nomological" refers to laws
            - esp. scientific or natural laws.
        - In the deductive-nomological model, explanations are
          built by deductively deriving
            - a phenomenon (the explanandum)
            - from general laws (nomological statements)
            - and specific facts (initial conditions).
        - If you know the laws of nature and the relevant
          conditions, you can logically deduce why something
          happened.

    - An explanation is structured as follows:
        1. _Explanandum_: This is the phenomenon or event we want
           to explain. It's the thing we find puzzling or want to
           understand.
        2. _Explanans_:
            - Consists of two components:
            - Deductive Statements:
                - These are scientific laws or general principles
                  that describe how the world works. They are
                  typically in the form of if-then statements,
                  such as "If A and B, then C."
            - Initial Conditions:
                - These are specific facts or observations about
                  the particular situation or case being
                  explained.

        3. _Deductive Argument_:
            - The explanation involves constructing a deductive
              argument where the explanans (general laws
              & initial conditions) logically entail or imply the
              explanandum (the phenomenon to be explained).
            - In other words, if you accept
                - the general principles and
                - initial conditions as true,
                - then you must also accept the explanation for
                  the phenomenon as true.

        4. _Covering Law Model_:
            - The D-N model is often referred to as the "covering
              law" model because it suggests that scientific
              explanations "cover" or account for specific
              phenomena by showing how they are the necessary
              consequences of general laws and specific initial
              conditions.

    - A simplified example:
        - _Explanandum_:
            - A glass bottle broke when dropped to the floor?
        - _Explanans_:
            - Deductive Statement: The law of gravity (objects
              fall when dropped).
            - Initial Condition: The bottle was dropped from
              a certain height onto a hard floor.

        - _Deductive Argument_: If we accept the law of gravity
          and the initial condition that the bottle was dropped
          from a certain height onto a hard floor, then we can
          deduce that the bottle broke when it hit the floor.

        - In this example, the D-N model explains the phenomenon
          of the bottle breaking by showing it's a logical
          consequence of known scientific laws and specific facts
          about the situation.

    - The deductive-nomological model has been critiqued and
      refined over the years, and may not capture all aspects of
      scientific explanation.
        - However, it remains a significant framework for
          understanding how scientific explanations are
          structured and evaluated.


### Reification
    - aside: `res` = Latin for thing, matter, affair
        - `republic` = res publica = 'the public thing'
        - `Real`, Reality, Realism
        - `Real Estate`
        - `Reside, Residence`
        - `in re` Smith v MJ = in the matter of
            - often shortened to '`re`... something'
    - Reification: also known as
        - 'concretism',
        - 'hypostatization',
        - or the 'fallacy of misplaced concreteness'
    - It is a fallacy of ambiguity, when an abstraction (abstract
      belief or hypothetical construct) is treated as if it were
      a concrete real event or physical entity.
    - Why is reification a fallacy of ambiguity?
        - Reification (from res, Latin for "thing") happens when
          you take something abstract—like justice, freedom, the
          economy, the mind—and talk about it as if it were
          a concrete, physical thing.
        - Example:
            - `"The market decided to punish the company."`
            - The market is an abstraction—a human-made system.
            - But the sentence treats it like an agent, a being
              that decides and punishes, like a person.
            - The ambiguity lies in how "market" is first
              understood as an abstract system, then treated as
              a concrete actor.
            - That shift—from abstract to concrete—is a semantic
              trick. And that’s why it counts as a fallacy of
              ambiguity:
                - the meaning of the concept shifts subtly in the
                  argument, causing a breakdown in clarity and
                  logic.

### What is Trancendental Idealism
    - https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant-transcendental-idealism/
    - In the 'Critique of Pure Reason' Kant argues that space and
      time are merely _formal features_ of how we _perceive_
      objects, not things in themselves that exist independently
      of us, or properties or relations among them.
        - Objects in space and time are said to be “appearances”,
          and he argues that we know nothing of substance about
          the things in themselves of which they are appearances.
          Kant calls this doctrine (or set of doctrines)
          “transcendental idealism”.
    - Ever since the publication of 'Critique of Pure Reason' in
      1781, readers have debated, what exactly transcendental
      idealism is, and have developed quite different
      interpretations.
    - Some, including many of Kant’s contemporaries, interpret
      transcendental idealism as essentially a form of
      phenomenalism, similar to that of Berkeley, while others
      think it's not a metaphysical or ontological theory at all.
    - There is probably no major interpretive question in Kant’s
      philosophy on which there is so little consensus.

### Anthropic Principle
    - $MY_NOTES/physics/metaphysics/Baggot-farewell-to-reality.txt
    - Carter intended the anthropic principle as a direct
      challenge to the Copernican Principle.
        - He presented his arguments at an International
          Astronomical Union symposium in Cracow on 10—12
          September 1973.
    - The conference had been dedicated to commemorate the _five
      hundredth anniversary_ of Copernicus’ birth.
    - Whereas the _Copernican Principle_ insists that intelligent
      life occupies no privileged position in the cosmos, the
      anthropic principle suggests that our status as conscious
      observers of our universe demands at least some form of
      privileged perspective.
    - Carter offered two versions.
    - Weak anthropic principle
        - ‘we must be prepared to take account of the fact that
          our location in the universe is necessarily privileged
          to the extent of being compatible with our existence as
          observers’.
            - This seems like common sense, if a bit of
              a tautology. In essence, it is a statement that
              relates to the notion of observer self-selection.
            - The universe we observe must, by definition,
              include observers like us capable of observing it.
              Our observations are necessarily biased by virtue
              of our own existence.
            - We are (again, by definition) unable to observe
              a universe in which observers like us cannot exist.
            - Carter used the weak anthropic principle to argue
              for the existence of what he called an ensemble of
              universes — meaning simply that there are many
              (maybe an infinite number) — without specifying
              precisely what these were or where they might have
              come from. There are many different possibilities,
              some of which were discussed in Chapter.
            - Readers interested in a more comprehensive review
              of the different multiverse theories should consult
              Brian Greene’s The Hidden Reality.
    - Strong anthropic principle
        - This is Carter’s second version of the anthropic
          principle:
            - ‘the Universe (and hence the fundamental parameters
              on which it depends) must be such as to admit the
              creation of observers within it at some stage’.
        - In this definition the key word is ‘must’, and one
          (somewhat irresistible) interpretation restores
          intelligent life to its pre-Copernican, fully
          privileged status.
        - The strong anthropic principle tips us towards
          teleology — philosophical positions that perceive
          ultimate purposes, final causes and deliberate design
          in nature.
        - It puts ‘us’ firmly back at the centre of the picture.
          To be fair to Carter, both his weak and strong
          anthropic principles were based on the notion of
          intelligent observers, not necessarily carbon-based
          life forms like ourselves. He later regretted his use
          of the word ‘anthropic’.

### Structural Realism
    - $MY_BOOKS/Physics/QM/QFT/Structure-Interpretation-Standard-Model-McCabe.pdf
    - Two versions of structuralism will be relevant.
    - Firstly, there is _structural realism_, which asserts there
      is a `physical domain` which exists beyond the empirical
      phenomena, and in mathematical physics at least, this
      objective physical domain is an instance of a mathematical
      structure.
    - Secondly, there is _structural empiricism_, which holds
      that empirical phenomena are organised, explained and
      predicted by embedding the empirical data into mathematical
      structures, but those mathematical structures do not
      characterise anything which lies beyond the empirical
      phenomena.
    - We might suppose that some theories of mathematical physics
      are susceptible to a realist interpretation, and others to
      an empiricist interpretation.
    - E.g, while GR might provide a characterisation of the
      objective ontology of space–time, quantum theory provides
      only an empiricist calculus for particle phenomena.
    - If so,
        - GR -> structural realism
        - QM -> structural empiricism
    - If QM admits only an empiricist interpretation, then none
      of its structures capture the ontology of a world lying
      beyond the phenomena.
    - While not dismissing such a possibility, the interpretation
      proposed here will apply 'structural realism' to the
      standard model of particle physics.
    - Ladyman (1998) has distinguished between _two versions_ of
      structural realism: the ontic and epistemic versions.
        - Epistemic structural realism
            - holds that mathematical physics provides knowledge
              of the structure which the physical world possesses
              beyond the empirical phenomena, but accepts there
              is more to the world beyond the structure that it
              possesses.
        - Ontic structural realism
            - In contrast, holds that the structure of the
              physical world is the only thing which exists.
            - It is the ontic version of structural realism which
              provides the rationale for the mathematical
              exposition in this book.
    - Many authors take structural realism to assert merely that
      the physical world possesses a `second-order structure`.
        - TODO: insert gpt session about 2nd order structure
    - Different sets, with different first-order properties and
      relationships, can possess the same second-order structure.
        - So the general assertion that a mathematical structure
          has a physical instance, doesn't pick out any
          particular set amongst those which have the first-order
          properties and relationships possessing this structure
          (see Votsis, 2003).
    - This approach to structural realism is inspired by Bertrand
      Russell. In "The Analysis of Matter" (1927), he argued that
      the most we can know is the second-order structure of the
      world, which lies beyond the empirical phenomena.
        - We can't know the first-order physical properties and
          relationships which possess that structure. (?)


### Aristotles theory of time (Presentism)

    - Amazon review:
        - There are key lessons to be learned from Aristotle’s
          metaphysics.
            - His theory of time is called _Presentism_, where
              past entities, states and events are those that did
              exist; present entities, states and events are
              those that do exist; and future entities, states

        - Past entities, states and events are contained within

          scholars who might be mocked or criticized for studying
          it. Barrow argues that:
            - Even if people make fun of you for studying math,
              those criticisms are shallow and baseless.
            - Math is deeply valuable—it trains the mind, brings
              clarity, avoids pointless debates, and leads to
              real knowledge.
            - It doesn’t rely on faith or trickery—it conquers
              reason itself with clean logic and undeniable
              truth.
            - Once you understand math, it stays with you. It
              becomes part of how you think, and no one can take
              that away.
            - Barrow's tone is both defensive (he's responding to
              critics or skeptics of math) and exalting (he's
              lifting math up as _nearly divine_).

### Hardy: Mathematical Discovery
    - “I've always thought of a mathematician as an _observer_,
      someone who gazes at a distant range of mountains and notes
      down their observations.
    - The object is simply to distinguish clearly and notify
      others of as many different peaks as one can.
    - There are some peaks easily distinguishable, while others
      are less clear. 
        - One sees `A` sharply, while of `B` one can obtain only
          transitory glimpses.
    - At last the mathematician makes out a ridge which leads
      from `A`, and following it to the end discovers that it
      culminates in `B`. 
        - `B` is now fixed in their vision, and from this point
          they can proceed to further discoveries.
    - In other cases perhaps one can distinguish a ridge which
      vanishes in the distance, and conjectures that it leads to
      a peak in the clouds or below the horizon. But when one
      sees a peak one believes it is there simply because one
      sees it.
    - If a mathematician wishes someone else to see it, they
      point to it, either directly or through the chain of
      summits which led them to recognise it themselves. 
        - When a pupil also sees it, the research, the
          argument, the proof is finished.”

### HN: Structural Realism, 3rd way

### Emergence
    - This new conception of emergence is independent of
      reduction and derivation.
    - Instead, a low-energy theory is understood as emergent from
      a high-energy theory if it's novel and autonomous compared
      to the high-energy theory. 
        - Also the low-energy physics is dependent (in
          a particular, minimal sense) on the high-energy
          physics. 
        - This dependence is revealed by the techniques of
          effective field theory and the renormalisation group.
    - These ideas are important in exploring the relationship
      between QG and GR, where _GR is understood as an effective,
      low-energy theory of Quantum Gravity_.
    - Without experimental data or a theory of QG, we rely on
      principles and techniques from other areas of physics to
      guide the way.
    - As well as considering the concept of 'emergence'
      appropriate to treating GR as an effective field theory,
      I investigate the emergence of spacetime (and other aspects
      of GR) in several approaches to QG, including the condensed
      matter approaches, "discrete approaches" (causal set
      theory, causal dynamical triangulations, quantum causal
      histories and quantum graphity) and loop quantum gravity.

### Metaphysics of Science
    - Craig Dilworth
    - In early civilisation, people commonly held mythological
      world-views. Virtually all humans living 3,000 years ago
      believed that the gods caused major world events.

    - As described by some archaeologists, people held an
      `I-Thou` relation to the world they lived in.

    - Around 2,600 years ago a great intellectual revolution of
      occurred. It was suggested that major events, such as
      earthquakes, were not caused by irate gods, but stemmed
      from the inanimate world itself.

    - Thales is associated with this revolution and attempted
      to explain earthquakes by likening the earth to a ship
      floating on water...

    - ... we suggest 3 streams in Greek scientific thought:
        1. physicalist
        2. formal
        3. biological

    - The `physicalist` stream began with Thales and was mainly
      developed by the Pre-Socratic philosophers. Its
      presuppositions were basically those of modern science.


### Backreaction: Discovery or invention
    - Although we discovered QM _effects_ out there in "the real
      world", I think the theory we use to explain them is
      a human construct. It's an invention.

    - It uses variables and language specific to our species. It
      carries the history of particle-wave duality. It's suitable
      to describe the data we measure with our devices.

    - An alien civilization might discover the same _effects_,
      but might invent a different story to explain them. Their
      theory could explain their data in entirely different ways.


### Supervenience
    -  an ontological relation to describe cases where:
        - the UPPER-LEVEL properties of a system
        - are determined by its LOWER-LEVEL properties

    - Aside: The root `venience` comes from Latin `venire` ("to
      come"). cf:
        - Convenience: from con- ("together") + venire, "coming
          together" or "suiting well"
        - Intervene: from inter- ("between") + venire, "to come
          between"
        - Advent: from ad- ("to") + venire, "arrival" or "coming"
        - Circumvent: from circum- ("around") + venire, "to go
          around, avoid"
        - Convene, event, revenue, and venture are also related
          through `venire`
        - supervene - come above, come upon...

    - Some philosophers posit the world is structured into
      a _hierarchy_ of properties, where the higher level
      properties supervene on lower level properties.

    - According to this view:
        - social        properties supervene on  psychological ones
        - psychological properties supervene on  biological    ones
        - biological    properties supervene on  chemical      ones
        - etc.

    - For example, MIND-BODY supervenience holds that:
        - every mental phenomenon must be grounded in, some
          underlying physical base (neural state?).
        - This means mental states can occur only in systems that
          can have physical properties; namely physical systems.

### Normative
    - In philosophy, normative statements affirm how things
      SHOULD or OUGHT to be:
        - how to value them,
        - which things are 'good' or 'bad',
        - which actions are 'right' or 'wrong'

    - `Normative` claims are usually contrasted with `Positivist`
      ones. (ie descriptive, explanatory, or constative ones) 
        - Positivist statements are FACTUAL statements that
          attempt to describe reality.

    - Examples:
        - Normative: "Children should eat vegetables"
        - Positive:  "Vegetables contain a high proportion of vitamins"

        - Normative: "Those who sacrifice liberty for security deserve neither"
        - Positive:  "A common consequence of sacrificing liberty for security is a loss of both"

    - Whether a statement is normative is logically independent
      of whether it is verified, verifiable, or popularly held.
