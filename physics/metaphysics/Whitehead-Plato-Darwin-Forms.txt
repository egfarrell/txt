### 1. Summary

- The article contrasts two big ways of thinking about “form” in
  nature: Platonism (forms are somehow already there) and
  Darwinism (forms emerge historically through variation and
  selection), and argues that this opposition is too simple.

- Pure Darwinism can’t work without some stable background
  structures, and pure Platonism can’t explain novelty; real
  creativity requires variation happening against relatively
  invariant constraints.

- Drawing on Kant and Schelling, the author argues that even the
  basic structures that make experience and thought possible
  (space, time, causality) are not eternal givens but emerged
  through nature’s own history.

- Ancient ideas like Aristotle’s “entelechy” (things actively
  realizing themselves) reappear today in modern terms like
  dynamic attractors and complex systems, without requiring
  fixed, pre-given forms.

- Following Whitehead, the article suggests that an evolving
  universe with changing “laws” is actually closer in spirit to
  Plato’s Timaeus than to Newton’s mechanical physics, and that
  metaphysics should synthesize across sciences rather than
  reduce everything to one explanation.

### Article

- This article is about a long-standing philosophical question:
  do the forms we see in the world already exist in some sense,
  or are they created over time?

- One traditional answer comes from Plato. On a rough reading,
  Platonism says that forms (like shapes, structures, or kinds of
  things) exist independently of the physical world, and that
  what we see around us somehow copies or instantiates them.

- A very different answer comes from Charles Darwin. Darwin
  showed how biological forms can arise historically through
  variation and natural selection, without needing to assume that
  those forms already existed in advance. New forms appear
  because organisms vary, and some variations survive better than
  others.

- Some philosophers and scientists divide modern views into
  several camps: mechanical physics, Platonism, neo-Darwinism,
  and more speculative extensions of Darwinism beyond biology.
  The author suggests that this can be simplified to a basic
  tension between Platonism and Darwinism.

- Mechanical physics (especially the Newtonian picture) and
  neo-Darwinism often claim to reject Platonism. But the author
  argues that they secretly keep it. Fixed mathematical laws in
  physics, or the idea that genes contain a kind of pre-written
  blueprint for organisms, both smuggle in something like
  pre-given forms.

- Darwin’s real contribution was to put variation first. Forms
  are not assumed at the beginning; they accumulate over time. As
  Darwin famously said, from simple beginnings, “endless forms
  most beautiful” have evolved.

- Still, the author thinks it’s a mistake to choose between
  “everything is variation” and “everything is fixed form.” Pure
  variation by itself cannot produce anything meaningful. For
  selection to work, there must be some stable features that
  persist long enough to matter. Without any regularity at all,
  there is nothing to select.

- So the author proposes a middle position, which he calls
  “evolutionary Platonism.” Some forms or structures are stable,
  at least from our perspective, and these provide the background
  conditions that allow genuinely new and contingent forms to
  emerge over time. Stability and creativity depend on each
  other.

- This idea connects to Immanuel Kant. Kant argued that we never
  experience reality “as it is in itself.” Instead, anything that
  can appear to us must already be shaped by basic structures
  like space, time, and causality. These are not objects floating
  in a Platonic heaven; they are the conditions that make
  experience possible at all.

- From our point of view as creatures embedded in the universe,
  these structures are effectively invariant. We never encounter
  pure randomness. We always encounter variation against
  a background of habits, regularities, and constraints that make
  memory, learning, and understanding possible.

- Friedrich Schelling took this a step further. He argued that
  these mental structures could not simply appear fully formed in
  human minds. Instead, they must have emerged through nature’s
  own history. On this view, human reason is a late achievement
  of nature itself. Mind is nature reflecting on its own order,
  not an external observer imposing rules on a lifeless world.

- The article also defends the use of myth and story alongside
  formal reasoning. Plato didn’t just replace myth with logic. He
  used both. Logical argument pushes thought to its limits, and
  when it breaks down, myth steps in to express what can’t be
  captured purely in abstract concepts. Even today, scientific
  equations still need stories to explain what they mean for
  human beings.

- The discussion then turns to Aristotle, who argued that
  understanding something requires multiple kinds of explanation:
  what it’s made of, what caused it, what form it has, and what
  it is tending toward. Although Aristotle was wrong about many
  scientific details, his idea that things actively realize
  themselves (entelechy) turns out to resemble modern ideas in
  complex systems theory. What Aristotle called entelechy looks
  a lot like what scientists now call dynamic attractors.

- The author emphasizes the role of metaphysics. Science explains
  specific things within specific domains. Metaphysics looks for
  patterns that cut across domains and warns against mistaking
  one kind of explanation for the whole story.

- Finally, drawing on Alfred North Whitehead, the article
  compares Isaac Newton’s physics with Plato’s Timaeus. Newton
  assumes fully formed space, time, matter, and laws from the
  start. Plato, surprisingly, imagines a cosmos that emerges from
  disorder through the gradual establishment of patterns and
  structures.

- Whitehead argues that our modern picture of an evolving
  universe, with changing laws and emergent orders, is actually
  closer to Plato’s vision than to Newton’s. The universe is not
  a finished machine running on eternal rules, but an ongoing
  creative process in which stability and novelty continually
  arise together.
