
### Me
    - "Does gravitomagnetism basically treat spacetime like
      a physical substance, essentially reviving the idea of an
      ether?"

### Claude
    - This intuition is shared by many serious people, including
      Einstein. In his 1920 Leiden lecture, "Ether and the Theory
      of Relativity," he argued that GR brings back a kind of
      ether: 
        - space with PHYSICAL PROPERTIES, which can affect matter
          and be affected by it. 
    - But he added it's an ether with NO STATE OF MOTION, so you
      can't say it's flowing past you or that you're at rest
      relative to it. 
    - That is the key difference from 19th-century ether, which
      had a preferred rest frame. Relativity kills that, but not
      the idea that spacetime itself has PHYSICAL PROPERTIES.

### What pushes toward "spacetime is a thing"
    - Frame dragging means the local inertial frame depends on
      what's nearby. Spacetime is not a passive stage, because
      the geometry responds to rotating matter and can in turn
      push gyroscopes around.
    - Gravitational waves are ripples in the metric that carry
      energy and momentum and propagate with no matter present.
      That is arguably stronger evidence than gravitomagnetism,
      since it's a dynamical, self-standing degree of freedom.
    - Kerr black holes are vacuum solutions that still have
      a rotation and frame dragging. The "rotation" is a property
      of the geometry itself, not of anything else you can point
      to.

### What pushes back
    - Gravitomagnetism is an analogy valid in the weak-field
      limit. Like E and B in electromagnetism, the split between
      "gravitoelectric" and "gravitomagnetic" depends on the
      observer's frame, so it's a bit of a stretch to read
      metaphysics off it alone.
    - Frame dragging is also read as Machian: inertial frames are
      fixed by the distribution of mass-energy, which is
      a *relationalist* theme. Einstein hoped GR would be fully
      Machian, though vacuum solutions like Kerr complicate that.
    - The hole argument (Earman, Norton) is a technical problem
      for the view that the bare manifold of points is
      a substance. Many philosophers retreat to "metric field
      substantivalism": the thing that exists isn't the points
      but the metric field, which is both the gravitational field
      and the spacetime structure.

- This lands on a live debate in philosophy of physics. Most
  would say GR shows that the gravitational/metric field is
  a real physical entity, much like the EM field.
- Whether that makes it "substantival spacetime" or just a field
  that happens to define geometry is the open question. I'd say
  it's partly a matter of taste about what we mean by "thing."

- If you want to dig in, Earman's *World Enough and Space-Time*
  and Rovelli's writing on this (he argues spacetime *is* the
  gravitational field, which pushes against the old
  absolute-container picture) are good starting points.
