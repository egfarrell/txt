### Abstract
    - First, I briefly review the differing conceptions of time
      held by three rival interpretations of QM:
        1. Collapse of the wave-packet
        2. Pilot-wave interpretation
        3. Everett interpretation

    - Then I turn to a much less controversial task:
        - expounding the recent understanding of the `time-energy
          uncertainty principle` established by authors like
          Busch, Hilgevoord and Uffink.
        - Although this may seem a narrow topic, I point out
          connections to other topics about time in QM:
            - eg, under what circumstances there is a time
              operator...

### Intro
    - `Time`, like space and matter, is a core concept in any
      physical theory, especially in fundamental ones.
    - How a theory treats `time` is therefore of philosophical
      interest.
        - This is even more true for a theory like QM; our best
          available and also philosophically puzzling.
    - Key philosophical issues, like non-locality and the
      measurement problem, affect how we think about `time`.
    - Different QM interpretations offer different views on these
      issues, and thus lead to different ideas about the nature
      of time.


- This tendency, for reflection to turn towards the general topic
  of interpreting QM, is aggravated by another deflating line of
  thought.
    - Namely: when we set aside non-locality and measurement, and
      ask how QM’s formalism treats time, the answer is roughly:
        - QM has nothing very special to say about time.
        - It just tags along with whatever is said by the cousin
          classical theory, be it Newtonian or relativistic...

- The 'pilot-wave' refers not only to de Broglie’s and Bohm’s
  original theories (point particles with definite positions) but
  also to similar theories—including _field theories_.

- The collapse is postulated in order to solve the measurement
  problem:
    - viz. by securing that measurements have definite outcomes,
      albeit ones that are NOT determined by the previous quantum
      state.

- Anyone who advocates such a collapse faces several questions.
- Three of the most pressing are:
    1. Under exactly what conditions does the collapse occur?
    2. What determines the physical quantity (in the formalism:
       the 'basis') with respect to which it occurs?
    3. How can the collapse mesh with relativity?

- These are questions for the foundations of physics, not
  philosophy.
    - But however they are answered, the proposed `irreducible
      indeterminism` raises a philosophic question on how exactly
      to understand the various alternative futures.
    - In particular:
        - is indeterminism compatible with the idea of a single
          actual future, i.e. the ‘block universe’ or ‘B-theory
          of time’ ?
        - Some say _Yes_
            - (Lewis 1986, p.207-208; Earman 2008,p.138 ftnote 8)
            - Broadly speaking, the idea is:
                - an indeterministic event requires that two or
                  more possible histories (possible worlds) match
                  utterly up to the event in question, but
                  thereafter fail to match (‘diverge’),
                  reflecting the differences in the event’s
                  various possible outcomes and in these
                  outcomes’ later causal consequences.
        - Others say _No_ (e.g. McCall 2000).
            - The idea is:
                - an indeterministic event requires that a single
                  possible history (possible world) splits at the
                  event in question, with future branches
                  incorporating the different outcomes and their
                  respective causal consequences.

- Pilot-wave: the original version postulates that in
  non-relativistic quantum theory, the preferred quantity is the
  _position_ of point-particles

- But as the phrase ‘at any time’ hints, most current versions of
  pilot-wave use an _absolute time_ structure (frame-independent
  simultaneity), as in Newtonian physics ...

- Agreed, some of these versions, especially in field theory,
  secure the Lorentz symmetry characteristic of SR.
    - But this is an approximate and _emergent symmetry_,
      governing a certain regime or sector of the theory, not
      a fundamental one.
    - (In a similar way, the pilot-wave recovers the apparent
      indeterminism of orthodox quantum theory (“the collapse of
      the wave packet”) as an emergent feature due to averaging
      over the unknown, but deterministically evolving, definite
      values.)

- ... these versions resurrect the ABSOLUTE TIME structure of
  Newtonian physics.
    - For the philosophy of time, that would put the cat among
      the pigeons.
    - After a century of accommodating our metaphysics and
      epistemology of time to relativity’s denial of absolute
      time, philosophers would be back to square one.

- Thus Bell, in his masterly (1986) introduction to interpreting
  quantum theory, wrote:
    - it ‘is surely the most bizarre of all [possible
      interpretations]’ and seems ‘an extravagant, and above all
      extravagantly vague, hypothesis.
    - I could almost dismiss it as silly’ (pp. 192, 194).

- I turn to Section 1’s second deflating thought:
    - that, interpretations aside, quantum theory’s formalism has
      nothing special to say about time; it just tags along with
      whatever is said by the cousin classical theory, be it
      Newtonian or relativistic.

- If one wishes, one can treat time in a manner similar to the
  position of a particle.
    - In both the Lagrangian and Hamiltonian formalism, `t` is
      then treated as one of the configuration coordinates `q`,
      all of which are functions of a temporal parameter, `τ`

    - I shall not go into details about this:
        - Hilgevoord warns it is liable to confuse time’s roles:
            - as a PARAMETER and

- When we turn to considering GR as the cousin classical theory,
  the situation becomes notoriously murky.
    - There is no corresponding quantum theory of gravity;
    - nor even a consensus of what it'd look like.
    - And the stakes are high: finding such a theory is widely
      considered the holy grail of theoretical physics.
        - (For philosophical intros to the issues see Butterfield
          and Isham 2001, Rovelli 2006, Huggett and Wuthrich
          (this volume).

- Besides, in one main approach to QG: the quantization of
  canonical (Hamiltonian) formulations of GR, this conflict is
  especially striking, indeed severe, regarding the treatment of
  time rather than space:
    - and accordingly, it is called ‘the problem of time’.

- Broadly speaking, quantum theories have an operation of
  TIME-REVERSAL on their space of states
    - (often defined by analogy with a time-reversal operation on
      the classical state-space);
    - and the theory’s equations of motion (laws of evolution for
      the state) are time-reversal invariant—
        - i.e. if the equations allow a certain evolution
          (temporal sequence of states, or possible history),
          then they also allow its time-reverse.
    - But various questions arise:
        - what is the best justification of the time-reversal
          operator’s definition?

- And what should we make of experiments indicating that certain
  weak interactions VIOLATE time-reversal invariance?

- ... the rest of this paper reports on authors such as Busch,
  Hilgevoord and Uffink in sorting out long-standing confusions,
  especially about:
    - the TIME-ENERGY UNCERTAINTY PRINCIPLE.

- My overall theme will be that there are very different versions
  of the time-energy uncertainty principle:
    - i.e. a relation like `∆T∆E ≥ 1/2 ħ` that are valid in
      different contexts.
    - Some won't use the _variance_, but rather some other
      measure of SPREAD.
    - Some will involve a time operator, but others will not;
        - Indeed, for some problems (eg Hamiltonians), an
          appropriate time operator cannot exist.
        - But there is no established general theory of time
          measurements, telling us for which Hamiltonians a time
          operator exists...

- Furthermore: one of the main novelties in these uncertainty
  principles, viz. the use of measures of SPREAD other than the
  VARIANCE, is just as important for quantities other than time
  and energy, such as position and momentum.

- The usual textbook version of the uncertainty principle is
  naturally understood as a constraint on statistics, or
  equivalently on state preparation.
    - This is a limitation: because Heisenberg’s original (1927)
      argument concerned how minimizing the error in a position
      measurement implied a greater disturbance in the system’s
      momentum, and this argument spawned countless discussions
      of the uncertainty principle in terms of _error_ and
      _disturbance_.

- Classical analogues: In recent decades, research in symplectic
  geometry that underlies Hamiltonian classical mechanics has
  uncovered remarkable CLASSICAL ANALOGUES of the uncertainty
  principle.
    - The breakthrough was Gromov’s 1985 symplectic no-squeezing
      theorem. It is a remarkable strengthening of `Liouville’s
      theorem`, that any canonical transformation is
      VOLUME-PRESERVING.
    - For it roughly says, that the surface area, defined by the
      projection of a phase space region onto the PLANE given by
      conjugate coordinates `q_i` and `p_i` , is also preserved.
    - This theorem has been nicknamed ‘the symplectic camel’:
        - In light of the Gospel ‘It is easier for a camel to go
          through the eye of a needle, than for a rich man to
          enter into the kingdom of God’.
        - Thus the theorem says: a ball in Hamiltonian phase
          space of radius `R` (‘the camel’) can be canonically
          transformed so as to pass through an ‘eye of a needle’
          defined by a disc of radius `r` in a conjugate
          coordinate plane (`q_i`, `p_i`) only if `r > R`.
    - For a glimpse of this rich subject, and its application to
      the classical-quantum relationship, see:
        - De Gosson (2001, Section 3.7; 2009),
        - De Gosson and Luef (2009).

- In fact, this result arises from more general results based on
  the statistical distance between two probability distributions:
    - an idea which makes sense for discrete distributions (and
      so: not for Fourier-related distributions), and which has
      wide application.
- In particular, this idea leads to `entropic uncertainty
  relations`, and Uffink’s generalizations of them

- Both the measures of uncertainty so far introduced—the familiar
  notion of variance, and Section 3.2’s notion of WIDTH, are
  about uncertainty in the `value` of a quantity.
    - But one can instead be uncertain about the system’s state.
    - There is a natural measure of this second kind of
      uncertainty;
        - Hilgevoord and Uffink show the remarkable result that
          it combines with Section 3.2’s notion of the width `W
          α`, to give uncertainty principles.
    - Thus these uncertainty principles involve two different
      notions of SPREAD, each of them different from the familiar
      notion of VARIANCE.
        - They also provide an exactly SIMILAR TREATMENT of space
          and time, and thus also of the respective conjugate
          notions, energy and momentum.

              equal/same?

- Busch (1990, Section 2; 2008 Section 2) articulates
  a TRICHOTOMY of roles (or senses) of time, which is invaluable
  for organizing discussion and preventing confusion about time
  in quantum physics.
    - It is equally useful for discussing time in classical
      physics; (albeit with some obvious adjustments, e.g.
      adjusting the representation of physical quantities from
      self-adjoint operators, to functions on a phase space).
    - I shall present EACH ROLE in a Subsection.
    - Beware: Busch’s trichotomy, and his labels for the three
      roles, is not used by most other authors;
        - He also suggests more than one label for each role.
        - I will use the labels of his later discussion (2008).

- Although I follow Busch, it will be clear that the trichotomy,
  or rather the three binary distinctions it involves, are common
  currency:
    - they are accepted by other authors, although under
      a different label (or none).
    - For example, the distinction between Busch’s 1st role, and
      his 2nd and 3rd roles is effectively the distinction
      I stated at the start of Section 2.2, as common to
      classical and quantum physics: - namely, between:
        - (a) time as a COORDINATE of spacetime
            - and an independent variable in the equations of
              motion, and
        - (b) time as a FUNCTION of other quantities,
            - as in the pointer-variable of a clock (cf. (iii) of
              Section 2.2).
        - This distinction is also emphasized by Hilgevoord (1996
          Section 2; 2002, Section 2; 2005, Sections 1, 2;
          Hilgevoord and Atkinson 2011, Section 2).
        - He argues that much of the historIcal confusion about
          time in quantum theory (including von Neumann and
          Pauli) can be dispelled by respecting this distinction,
          and the corresponding distinction about space, or
          position. i.e. between:
            - (a) position as a COORDINATE of spacetime, and
            - (b) position as a DYNAMICAL VARIABLE,
                - e.g. the position of a point-particle.


    - Hilgevoord makes the following points:

    1. For clarity, we should distinguish between two roles that
       time can play:
        - Use `x` to represent time as a _coordinate_ (i.e.
          a label or parameter);
        - Use `q` to represent time as a _dynamical variable_
          (i.e. something that evolves or interacts).
        - Accordingly, write the wave function as `ψ(q)`, not
          `ψ(x)`, to reflect this distinction.

    2. With this distinction in place, there's no reason to claim
       that quantum physics breaks from classical physics by
       treating _position_ as a dynamical variable but _not
       time_.
        - In fact, both classical and quantum physics allow both
          time and position to be treated:
            - either as _coordinates_,
            - or as _dynamical variables_— depending on the
              context.


- There is a long-standing tradition in quantum mechanics—dating
  back to its founding figures—that proposes an uncertainty
  principle involving energy and time. More specifically, it
  suggests that there is a trade-off between:

    - The duration of an energy measurement, and
    - One of the following:
        - The _range_ of possible changes in the system’s energy that can't be controlled,
        - The _resolution_ (i.e. precision) of the energy measurement,
        - Or the _statistical spread_ (i.e. uncertainty) in the system’s energy.

- In short, the idea is: the shorter the measurement time, the
  LESS PRECISELY you can measure energy (or the more the system’s
  energy might change during the process).

- However, Busch (a modern quantum theorist) argues that this
  traditional view is flawed. He agrees with Aharonov and Bohm
  (1961) who presented a thought experiment (or model) which
  shows it is possible in principle to measure the energy of
  a system both rapidly and with arbitrary accuracy.

- In other words, there is no fundamental limitation that forces
  a trade-off between the speed and the accuracy of an energy
  measurement.

- Their model involves two free particles (i.e. not interacting
  with anything else), both moving along a single line (1D). The
  first particle is the system being measured, and the second
  acts as a measuring device.

    - During the measurement, there’s a very short interaction
      ("impulsive") in which the second particle measures the
      momentum (and thus the energy) of the first.

    - The second particle's own momentum is used as the pointer
      (i.e. it carries the result of the measurement).

- While the technical details are complex, the key point is that
  this model demonstrates how an energy measurement can be both
  fast and precise.

- Busch notes that to properly understand and validate this kind
  of measurement, one needs to use a modern mathematical
  framework known as `POVMs` (Positive Operator-Valued Measures).
    - POVMs are a generalisation of the usual notion of quantum
      observables (which are normally represented by Hermitian
      operators).
    - They allow for more realistic descriptions of quantum
      measurements, especially when the measurement process is
      not ideal or involves interaction with a measuring device.
