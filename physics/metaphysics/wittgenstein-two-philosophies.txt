### 01 Tractatus:
    - Tractatus central question: how is language possible?
        - People had discussed _meaning_ before, but Wittgenstein
          generalised it immensely. How can humans use marks
          (writing) and noises (speech) to represent something
          utterly different from the marks and noises.

    - Why is the existence of language a philosophical problem?
      - Here we have some elements of the world intelligently
        reproducing other elements of it within themselves.
      - It's a question so obvious most people don't bother to
        ask it. Like Newton asking why the planets didn't charge
        off in all directions. It's got the same naive, pristine
        fundamentalness.

### Tractatus Questions
    - He asked with utmost generality:
        - Q1: What is the relationship of language to the _world_?
        - Q2: What is the relationship of language to _thought_?

        - Answer:
            - Language represents the world by _DEPICTING_ it.
            - `Propositions` are pictures of facts and
              `propositions` are the expressions of thought, the
              vehicles of thought.
                - ie `Propositions` are the key thing.
                - The fundamental units of language are
                  propositions and they are like images of
                  reality.
                - Propositions (language) are also how thoughts
                  are formed and conveyed.

    - He was concerned with the _limits_ of what could be
      expressed in thought, ie what language could do.
        - He was also concerned with what language can't do.

    - He insisted the limits of language were clearly stateable
      and this stems from his idea that language was essentially
      (and literally) `pictorial` in character.

    - And he thought the relation of language to the world
      couldn't be meaningfully discussed in language.

### Tractatus solutions
    - The scope of language:
        - Language has to fit the world very directly.
          A meaningful proposition has to be consituted with
          names that fit directly onto objects in the world that
          are exactly correlated to the objects they are the
          names of.
        - And a true proposition will be an arrangement of names
          that mirrors the arrangement of objects it treats of.
        - This limits language to the description of that which
          is outside language.

    - The Picture theory of language.
        - How can a sentence be a picture of a fact.
        - Well he concedes the sentences of ordinary language
          don't look like pictures, but if they are to have any
          meaning they must be capable of being decomposed into
          a set of elementary sentences which really are
          pictures.
            - (which consist purely of names which are directly
              correlated with the objects that are being talked
              about)

    - But I often say many false things. How is that explained?
        - Propositions can be moved around like counters, and
          when their configuration mirrors an actual state of
          objects in the world, the proposition is true.
        - If I say some propositions that are a possible state of
          affairs but not actually in the world, the statement is
          false.
        - But if the state of affairs isn't possible in the world
          at all, then my utterance is meaningless.
        - Three-fold analysis: True, False, Meaningless.

    - What about other parts of language e.g. aesthetic
      judgements? Well these aren't really explained, they're
      just not part of language proper.

    - So Wittgenstein must be saying the World itself is
      constituted in a certain way:
        - it must ultimately consist of simple objects that are
          related in certain ways.
        - The World must have the structure to be capable of
          being rendered in language.
        - (Rephrase: The World must have a structure capable of
          being rendered in language?)
        - (Rephrase: The World must have structure capable of
          being rendered in language?)

    - Now what about the "un-sayable". Well nothing can be said
      about the relation of language to the world.

    - The sentence has an internal structure that can be
      EXHIBITED by the propositions, which relates the names in
      the sentence to each other, in a way that corresponds to
      how objects in the world are related to each other, but it
      is only exhibited by the propositions and not stated.
    - It's shown by the proposition but can't be expressed in
      language.

    - Nonsensical: Judgements of value are not matters of
      describable fact (they're transcendental). He gives no
      positive account in his book of what they are.

    - Why did this philosophy have such an impact?
        - It seems a strange one that leaves a lot out?!

    - All truths of Maths and Logic are tautologies. Just
      unpacking what is already contained in the proposition.
        - Yes they give no information about the world in itself,
          they are symbolic in character.

    - Vienna Circle interpretation:
        - Other philosophers interested in Wittgenstein provided
          an easier explanation of language.
        - That the propositions boiled down to reports of sense
          experiences of the speakers and that was the starting
          point of analysing etc...
        - (and not just configurated names of simple objects
          no explanation of how those objects relate to our
          awareness)

    - After Tractatus he abandoned Philosophy and worked as
      a Monastery Gardner!
        - (His Tractatus work reflected his own isolated
          situation - he didn't touch on the social aspects of
          language, it was just a reporting tool)


### 02 His Second Philosophy
    - Doesn't provide clear definite principles. Approaches
      language as a natural human phenomenon.

    - A central feature of this 2nd philosphy is that language is
      a public or social phenomenon. It can only function if
      there are rules accepted by more than one person.

    - He no longer sees MEANING as a `PICTURE` of the world, but
      more as to the `USE` it can be put. He sees a sentence as
      a tool and the meaning of a sentence is the sum total of
      its possible uses.

    - Language games. (draws attention to the fact they are
      rule-governed practices)

    - There's no common characteristic to all games. Games are
      related to one another by family resemblance.

    - His view of philosophy: its an activity, not a theory.
      It's something you do, not a final body of doctrine.

    - Philosophy: an investigation into the multiple ways
      language can mislead us in thinking about the world.

    - He attacks his earlier view that words are essentially
      names, his view now is that the use of names is one of
      these "language games", just one element in language, and
      the teaching of names is a language game...

    - He feels language is a publicly available social reality,
      not language as some kind of essence you can work out in
      your head by pure reasoning.

    - Task of Philosophy: we become knotted up in some false view
      of reality by langauge, and philosophy can untie these
      knots. (theraputic aspect of philosophy)

    - His work encouraged a certain kind of relativism? There's
      a recognition of the equal rights of all "language games"
      provided it passes the test of workability.

    - Impact on Antropology: If you are critiquing another
      "tribe" from the outside and not participating in their
      rule and language game you fail to understand the correct
      context... (not being "within" the life in which these
      rules operate)

    - Impact on theory of mind: his 2nd philosophy showed that
      what we say about our own internal mental life is tied
      a lot to behaviour (and the physical world). There is not
      this traditional split between the two as was thought
      beforehand.

### 03 GPT
    - Wittgenstein's philosophical journey is often divided into
      two distinct periods, marked by his seminal works: the
      *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* (early philosophy) and
      *Philosophical Investigations* (later philosophy). While
      seemingly contradictory, these two phases represent
      a profound evolution in his understanding of language,
      meaning, and the nature of philosophy itself.

    - The early Wittgenstein of the *Tractatus* envisioned
      language as a precise, logical mirror of reality. He
      proposed the "picture theory" of meaning, asserting that
      propositions are "pictures of facts." For a proposition to
      be meaningful, it must depict a possible state of affairs
      in the world, sharing a logical structure with that
      reality. The world, according to this view, is a totality
      of facts, and meaningful language strictly corresponds to
      these facts. Anything beyond this factual realm – such as
      ethics, aesthetics, or metaphysics – was deemed "unsayable"
      or nonsensical, something to be "passed over in silence."
      The *Tractatus* aimed to define the limits of language and
      thought, revealing what *can* be meaningfully said and, by
      implication, what cannot. His philosophy at this stage was
      highly rigorous, seeking an underlying, universal logical
      structure for all language.

    - In contrast, the later Wittgenstein, particularly in
      *Philosophical Investigations*, abandoned this rigid,
      formalist approach. He recognized that language is far more
      diverse and fluid than his earlier theory allowed. The
      pivotal concept of his later work is "language games."
      Here, meaning is not derived from a direct correspondence
      to facts, but from the *use* of words within specific,
      socially-contextualized activities. Just as a piece in
      a game of chess has meaning only in relation to the rules
      and practices of that game, so too do words derive their
      meaning from the "game" they are played in.

    - Wittgenstein argued that there isn't one universal logic
      governing all language, but rather a multitude of "language
      games," each with its own rules and purposes. 
        - These games are interwoven with our "forms of life,"
          the shared human activities and practices that give
          language its context. 
        - He emphasized that philosophical problems often arise
          from a misunderstanding of how language actually
          functions, by trying to apply rules from one language
          game to another or by seeking a singular, essential
          meaning where only diverse uses exist. 
        - For the later Wittgenstein, philosophy's task shifted
          from uncovering a _hidden logical structure_ to
          dissolving confusions by observing how language is
          actually used in everyday life. 
        - He moved from a search for universal truths to an
          exploration of the practical, public, and contextual
          nature of meaning.
