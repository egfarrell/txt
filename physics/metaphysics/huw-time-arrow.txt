### Amazon Review
    - The main purpose of the book is to make the case for
      a `Block Universe`, one in which time does not flow, but
      rather resides already as a fourth dimension.
    - The "Archimedes' Point" refers to the need to step out of
      our shells as agents in time, and view nature "from nowhen"
      if we are to truly understand the concept.

    - Price begins by analyzing what appears to be the natural
      symmetry of time as outlined by the mathematical
      description of physics. He then presents common
      time-asymmetric arguments, one after another, and shows
      them to be ultimately fallacious, contradictory, or
      suffering from anthropocentric double standards.

    - Near the end of the book Price incorporates the strange
      behavior of QM, which can plausibly be explained by
      advanced action (aka causes coming from the future), to
      bolster his "Block Universe" claims. In the end I almost
      take the Block Universe for granted, and from this
      perspective the book is a resounding success.

    - The end of each chapter contains a _summary_ of its
      contents which was fantastic.

    - The _final chapter_ again contains a summary of the major
      logical points made throughout the book which helps tie
      everything together. If you're a casual reader looking to
      be entertained then this book probably isn't for you.

### Amazon review
    - I found it difficult to rate this book, wavering between
      3 and 4 stars. Eventually, I opted for 4... it covers many
      interesting points and I find the arguments persuasive.

    - On the other hand, it's more obscure than necessary due to
      the long-winding linguistic style interspaced with
      analogies / metaphors that are sometimes more puzzling than
      revealing...

    - ... contrary to the Preface, some background in physics is
      needed to appreciate the arguments. Besides, it doesn't
      explain the concept of objective (physical) time well and
      I feel it fails to give the non-professional reader a clear
      idea how to view the things from an `atemporal standpoint`
      ('view from nowhen').

    - My first try at the book was a fiasco due to the lack of
      a clear idea about objective (physical) time, entropy,
      thermodynamical processes, causal forks, a state
      function...

    - But I acquired the required knowledge from:
        1.  Records of the Future: Classical Entropy, Memory, and
        the 'Arrow of Time' (Quantum Physics free of Folklore 1)
            - https://www.amazon.com/Records-of-the-Future-Classical-Entropy-Memory-and-the-Arrow-of-Time-Quantum-Physics-free-of-Folklore-1/dp/B00AWGEQQQ
        2. Galloping with Light: Einstein, Relativity and
        Folklore
            - https://www.amazon.com/Galloping-Light-Einstein-Relativity-Folklore/dp/1456373854

    - The main argument in the book is our intuitive/subjective
      sense of time has a great effect on how we think about time
      and the temporal aspects of reality.
        - But we should try to distinguish how the world actually
          is from how it seems to be from our particular
          standpoint.
        - Price argues that the concept of 'time' is a secondary
          quality such as colour, leaving it to the reader to
          figure out what the primary quality in the case of
          'time' might be.
            - (As my inference was influenced by Records of the
              Future: Classical Entropy, Memory, and the 'Arrow
              of Time' (Quantum Physics free of Folklore #1)
        - I'll keep silent on this so as not to spoil the
          enjoyment of finding it out for yourself!

    - A central question of the book is how can we reconcile the
      `time-symmetric laws` we find in physics with the world we
      find around us, which appears to have a _preferred
      direction_ from past to future.
    - In order to reach the answer, the first half of the book
      deals with the categories/phenomena (so-called 'arrows of
      time') which our subjective sense of time relies on:
        - the arrow of thermodynamics,
        - the arrow of radiation,
        - the cosmological arrow,
        - the arrow of causation and
        - the arrow of counterfactual dependence.

    - The question now is whether these 'arrows of time' only
      reflect our anthropocentric view or are an actual feature
      of reality.
    - In general, the low-entropy condition of the present and
      past universe is puzzling and Price argues that purely
      statistical reasoning is unreliable and unsatisfactory
      because it is in conflict with the prevailing
      time-symmetric physics and involves unjustified temporal
      double standards.

    - In this context, Price expounds his reflections on the
      basic dilemma of cosmology, which I found interesting.
    - It seems that we have to accept either Gold's hypothesis
      that entropy must decrease toward both ends of the
      Universe, or Penrose's idea that the low-entropy big bang
      is simply not explicable by time-symmetric physics unless
      we were prepared to allow it is just a statistical 'fluke'.
      The author argues in favor of Thomas Gold's time-symmetric
      Universe (recollapsing/low entropy at both ends).

    - I found it interesting that Price's reflections on
      Wheeler-Feynmann `Absorber Theory` (apparently it involves
      the misleading concept of temporal asymmetry of radiation)
      and objections to Hawking's assumption that the Universe
      has an objective start, failing to apply the statistical
      arguments and the 'no-boundary' (low-entropy) condition
      equally both to what we call the big-bang and the supposed
      big-crunch, thus putting asymmetry in his theory 'by hand'.

    - The second half of the book deals with the asymmetry of the
      principle of `μInnocence` (common past hypothesis at the
      microscopic level) and our intuitive view of `cause-effect`
      relations.
    - Price proposes that causal asymmetry might be perspective
      dependent. What gives us the 'causal temporal arrow' is the
      fact that it is impossible for us, as agents in the world,
      to reverse the order of things, i.e., to achieve an earlier
      end(effect) by a later means (cause).
    - We take it for granted that events in the world are
      independent unless they share some _common causal history_.
    - In Price's opinion, QM seems to provide a confirmation
      against the asymmetry of the principle of `μInnocence`
      because it's actually not observable and only reflects our
      macroscopic view of cause-effect relation.

    - However, one consequence of abandoning `μInnocence` is
      surprising. Price favors Einstein's view of QM, arguing
      that the _asymmetry of the state function_ is unproblematic
      if it is simply an incomplete description of QM.
        - (Never knew the wave function is asymmetric?)
        - (Ah is it because of spontaneous wave collapse?)
    - He suspects that Bell thought both the 'common past
      hypothesis' and the 'common future hypothesis' conflict
      with our intuitive assumption that experimenters are free
      to choose measurement settings and that these settings are
      free variables.
    - Price thinks "it's fair to say that Bell saw how Einstein
      could be right about QM, but didn't understand what he
      saw".

    - Price further proposes we might give up Bell's
      `independence assumption` and save Einsteinian realism and
      locality as well as free will.
    - The secret lies in advanced action/backward causation.
      While the maths remains the same, the benefits of 'advanced
      action' to explain reality at microscopic level are:
        - it avoids nonlocality
            - (faster than light influence can be considered
              a backward influence from some inertial frame of
              reference),
        - denies that a collapse of the wave function
            - corresponds to a real change in the physical system
              and it does not encounter the measurement problem.

    - Overall, the book is worth it if you're intrigued with the
      subject and prepared to do some hard work.

### Amazon Review
    - A signpost along the road...
    - This is a very good book, but I feel something is missing.
    - Imagine if someone in 1820 had written the definitive book
      about electricity and magnetism:
        - It would have contained all kinds of information about
          Leyden jars, dynamos, induced current, electro-magnets,
          compasses, etc.
        - It would not mention the electro-magnetic field, nor
          would it mention electro-magnetic radiation.
    - This is not a criticism of the author: No one today could
      write a complete book on the subject of time, because we
      still do not really understand it
    - The author does a good job of covering entropy, causality,
      and quantum `time asymmetry`,
        - ... but I believe that these phenomena are all aspects
          of the same thing. The scientific and philosophical
          study of time is still at the stage where we are
          "collecting stamps", rather like biology was in the
          period before Darwin.
    - We are still waiting for an integrated theory of time,
      which can explain the direction of causality, the
      non-reversibility of radiation, and all of the other
      time-asymmetries in one package.
