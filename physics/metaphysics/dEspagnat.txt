### Book: "Physics and Philosophy": review
    - Amazon:
        - https://www.amazon.com/Physics-Philosophy-Bernard-dEspagnat-ebook/dp/B09K4R78CL
    - This is a superb work on the philosophical implications of
      QM, particularly as it relates to _epistemology_.
    - D'Espagnat's concept of `Veiled Reality` is akin to
      Immanuel Kant's `Noumenal Reality`: both propose the
      existence of a deeper, underlying "something" that serves
      as the foundation for the empirical reality we perceive.
        - This underlying reality is not directly accessible but
          is philosophically necessary to explain and give
          structure to our observable world.
    - While D'Espagnat found Kant's original arguments obsolete,
      he nevertheless derived views that somewhat coincide with
      the core of Kant's conclusions, through a analysis of
      modern physics.
    - D'Espagnat introduces the idea of `extended causality`,
      which doesn't operate between observable phenomena, as
      traditionally understood, but between ultimate Reality
      itself and the phenomena we observe. This Reality acts as
      an unknowable foundation or underlying structure that gives
      rise to the laws of nature.
    - Physics does not provide knowledge of _Independent Reality_
      as in Objective Realism, ...but rather empirical reality
      (phenomenal reality),
        - That is, reality as experienced and conceptualized, and
          so can only provide predictive knowledge of
          observables.
        - Given the refutability of scientific experiments, there
          must be a 'Something' which says "no".
        - This justifies the notion of a Independent Reality,
          (though formless and unknowable),... as opposed to an
          Hegelian idealism. Thus, D'Espagnat, like Kant avoids
          that progression.

- Here are my thoughts (not necessarily a reflection of
  dEspagnat)
    - It's reasonable to suggest that, relative to Independent
      Reality, the mind's a priori intellectual faculties—evolved
      to synthesize experience at the macroscopic level—are
      conceptually inadequate or "artificial" when applied to the
      microscopic (quantum) realm.
        - As a result, key concepts we rely on for intuitive
          understanding—such as locality, counterfactuality,
          space, time, separability, and causality—fail to truly
          align with the nature of Reality at the quantum level.
    - The epistemological upheaval brought by QM could be seen as
      a rediscovery of the implications of Kant’s 'Copernican
      Revolution' in the 'Critique of Pure Reason'. 
        - While I believe Kant’s reasoning remains sound (I do
          not see non-Euclidean geometries as a refutation, since
          in GR, the tangent space of a pseudo-Riemannian
          manifold is locally Euclidean—or more accurately,
          Minkowskian), it is necessary to revisit his core ideas
          in light of modern scientific understanding. 
        - d'Espagnat carries out this reevaluation here.
    - Abraham Pais, the biographer and friend of Einstein,
      remarked that Bohr was the natural successor to Kant.
        - Heisenberg had developed a non-intuitive, purely
          operational approach, in matrix mechanics, while
          Schrödinger along with Einstein still expected 'Reality
          per se', to conform to intuitive principals. 
        - Bohr won this epistemologically-based debate.
