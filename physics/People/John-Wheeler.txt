    - For just about everything in the world around you, you can
      easily tell the difference between
        - forward-in-time and
        - backward-in-time.
    - But the lesson of 19th century physics is that this
      difference is a matter of probabilities, not absolutes.
    - One direction might be quite likely, the other direction
      incredibly, ridiculously unlikely. Yet unlikely is not
      quite the same as impossible.
    - So Newtonian time-reversal invariance is saved, even if it
      doesn’t seem to have much to do with the world that we
      experience.
    - more info:
        - Newton’s equations are time-symmetric.
            - If you film two billiard balls colliding and then
              run the film backward, the reversed motion still
              satisfies Newton’s laws. The equations themselves
              don’t pick out a preferred time direction.
        - But the world looks one-way.
            - Eggs crack, not un-crack etc etc. These processes
              scream of a _forward arrow_ of time.
        - The bridge between symmetry and reality is probability.
            - Enter 19th-century statistical mechanics
              (Boltzmann, Gibbs). They showed that while the
              micro-laws of motion don’t care about time
              direction, the statistics of huge numbers of
              particles do.
            - Systems tend to evolve from less probable states
              (low entropy, like all gas molecules bunched in one
              corner) to more probable states (high entropy,
              evenly spread gas).
            - A glass shattering is overwhelmingly probable,
              because there are astronomically more ways for
              shards to be scattered than for molecules to line
              up into an intact glass.
            - The reverse—shards flying together to assemble
              a glass—is possible, but the probability is so
              absurdly tiny that you’ll never see it in the age
              of the universe.
        - So when Wheeler says the difference is "a matter of
          probabilities, not absolutes," he’s reminding us that
          the arrow of time isn’t written into Newton’s equations
          as an absolute law.
            - It emerges statistically, from the overwhelmingly
              likely direction of entropy increase.
        - In other words, the past-future distinction we feel in
          daily life is not carved into the microscopic rules,
          but into the probabilistic behavior of billions of
          particles acting together.
        - Wheeler gestured at that central insight of
          19th-century physics:
            - the arrow of time is emergent, not fundamental.
    - If you wanted to tie a bow on it: it’s about the
      reconciliation between the time-reversal invariance of
      mechanics and the irreversibility of experience.
