Hilary Putnam (1926–2016)

- Originally proposed that quantum mechanics required
  a revolutionary shift to "quantum logic". Later, he rejected
  this as unworkable, instead advocating for a scientific realism
  that grapples with the ontological, or "what there is,"
  implications of quantum mechanics, particularly the
  interpretation of the wavefunction and the measurement problem.

- Key Aspects of Putnam, Quantum Mechanics, and Ontology:

    - Rejection of Quantum Logic:
        - In the 1960s-70s, Putnam and David Finkelstein argued
          that quantum mechanics required a revolution in logic
          itself, treating logic as empirical (similar to
          geometry). By the 1990s, Putnam considered this
          "quantum logic" approach unworkable and returned to
          classical logic.

    - Scientific Realism:
        - Putnam's central goal was a "scientific
          realism"—understanding quantum mechanics as
          a description of reality, rather than just a predictive
          tool.

    - Ontology of the Wavefunction:
        - He questioned what the mathematical formalism of
          quantum mechanics tells us about the world. A major
          ontological puzzle he focused on is that the wave
          function does not "live" in 3+1 dimensional spacetime,
          but rather in a higher-dimensional configuration space.

    - Rejection of Certain Interpretations:
        - Putnam criticized interpretations where "collapse" is
          brought about simply by measurement, as well as the
          Many-Worlds interpretation.

    - Evolution of Thought:
        - As is common with his work, Putnam frequently revisited
          and altered his views, moving from a radical revision
          of logic to a more nuanced, yet still realist, approach
          to the ontology of quantum mechanics.

- Putnam argued that while quantum mechanics is highly
  successful, it poses a profound threat to our traditional,
  intuitive ontology—our deeply held, classical view of the
  material world.


### An Anecdote of Carnap’s
    - In 1953-54 , my first year of teaching at Princeton
      University, Carnap[1] was on leave at the Institute of
      Advanced Studies (also in Princeton, but not connected to
      the University), and I went to talk to him frequently, and
      found him not only impressive intellectually (as goes
      without saying), but also friendly and charming and free of
      any trace of “Herr Doktor Professor-ism”. One day he told
      me the following anecdote. When I recounted it to a friend
      a few days ago, I realized that Carnap may never have
      written it down, and that by publishing it here I could
      make sure it is on the record.

    - In 1918, after a few years as a student at the University
      of Berlin, Carnap enrolled as a graduate student at the
      University of Jena to obtain a Ph.D. in physics. As Carnap
      explained it to me, his plan was to formalize Einstein’s
      theory of relativity using notations from Principia
      Mathematica  (although the notation that figures in this
      anecdote is older, from the “algebra of relations” of
      Peirce and Schröder). Using the notion of the square of
      a relation­ – that is the product of a relation[2] with
      itself – Carnap found that he could write the statement
      that the relation T, “earlier in time than”, is transitive
      and dense simply as T=T2.  When Carnap went to his
      professor’s office to discuss his plan (here Carnap
      imitated the professor’s pompous voice) the professor said,

    - “It seems to me that if T=T2, then either T=1 or T=0”.
      Carnap explained that T was not a symbol for a number but
      rather for the temporal precedence relation, etc., etc.,
      etc., and at the end the professor said, “It still seems to
      me that if T=T2, then either T=1 or T=0. Young man, you had
      better try the philosophy department.”
