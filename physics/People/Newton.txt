From Isaac Newton to Jason Arday
How higher education’s capacity for challenging thought deteriorated.
Louise Perry
Aug 12, 2026

Photo: Shutterstock

William Wordsworth wrote the following of a bust of Isaac Newton that is still on display at the University of Cambridge:

    And from my pillow, looking forth by light / Of moon of favouring stars, I could behold / The Antechapel where the statue stood / Of Newton, with his prism and his silent face, / The marble index of a Mind for ever / Voyaging through strange seas of / Thought, alone.

Newton is remembered now as a great genius, which he was. He was also a peculiar man. He was famously rude and antisocial, with little apparent interest in friendships or romantic relationships. He was obsessed with his work, which included theology as well as natural science, and he would labor on a problem for days without food or sleep. An assistant who worked for him for five years saw the great man laugh only once: when someone asked Newton why anyone would want to study the mathematics of Euclid. His was a mind that not only voyaged “through strange seas of thought,” but did so—as Wordsworth understood—alone, for all his life. Brilliant people are not like the rest of us.

I wonder how Newton would fare in the Cambridge of today. Would he be able to negotiate funding applications? HR? Student politics? Would he demonstrate social grace, prudence and a veiled but also irrepressible drive toward acquiring power within the institution? Would he use the correct pronouns, honor the correct identity groups and keep perfectly within the confines of the Overton Window at all times? Would he succeed as a 21st-century academic? Something tells me he wouldn’t.

Some experts now believe that Newton was autistic, a diagnosis that could explain both his genius and his social agony. Successfully navigating complex social systems that rely on subtle cues is difficult for most people, and impossible for the autistic.

There’s another former professor at the University of Cambridge who has much to say on the plight of the autistic. Jason Arday was, until recently, a professor of sociology, feted as the youngest black professor ever appointed at Cambridge. He describes himself as autistic, an identity that—along with his race and class—allowed Mr. Arday to present himself as a marginalized figure, thus gaining access to certain advantages within the university system.

Mr. Arday recently resigned from his position after credible allegations of plagiarism emerged alongside a bizarre record of fantastical claims. The stories he has told about his life are both self-aggrandizing and self-contradictory. He claims to have been mute and illiterate for much of his childhood, to have suffered from brain cancer (or was it testicular cancer?) while completing his doctorate, to have run 600 miles in six days, to have raised millions for charity, and also to have spent several months comatose and suffering from locked-in syndrome. There are many more stories like these, all detailed in Mr. Arday’s newly published memoir, and all apparently accepted by his academic colleagues without demur during his time at Cambridge. The shoddiness of Mr. Arday’s academic work has belatedly come under scrutiny, and former students have revealed the shoddiness of his teaching, too. His was not a serious academic appointment.

I am not Mr. Arday’s doctor, and so I can’t comment on the reasoning behind his autism diagnosis. What I will say is that when Mr. Arday speaks, he does so with an amiability and openness that is unusual among those diagnosed with a condition characterized by social inelegance. What Mr. Arday lacks in academic talent, he more than makes up for in social talent. It was his charm that won him his place in the academy—a charm that is often lacking in the kind of strange, brilliant people who are most likely to make great contributions to intellectual life.

Modern elite universities are trying to do two things at the same time. Their primary, if unspoken, role is to act as a finishing school for the elites. The academic training may be rather lax, but it is offered alongside superb social training. An attentive student emerges from a university like Cambridge with the skills necessary to integrate into any elite profession. That includes a strong grasp of progressive ideology, and particularly an understanding of what one should not say in polite company. It also includes exposure to more subtle signs of elite status. In “Hillbilly Elegy,” Vice President JD Vance wrote memorably of attending a formal dinner while at Yale Law School, tasting sparkling water for the first time and spitting it out in horror. A preference for gross water was, he discovered, one of those things that separated the wheat from the chaff. You learn these things at Yale.

If you are running an elite finishing school in the 21st century, hiring someone like Mr. Arday is a great idea. His job is to introduce students to the vocabulary of progressivism and to improve the vibes of the place. It doesn’t matter if his academic contributions are minimal, because that’s not why he’s there. Students who study under someone like Mr. Arday graduate with that most valuable of credentials: social status.

Of course, there is another purpose that the modern university also claims to serve. Cambridge still takes pride in having historically been a site of intellectual excellence. The alumni of Isaac Newton’s Trinity College have been awarded more Nobel Prizes than the citizens of most nations. Even today, most students there are still expected to demonstrate intellectual skill, and brilliant academics can still sometimes pursue their work without interference. That older role has not been entirely abandoned.

But a Cambridge that truly gave priority to the pursuit of truth over the pursuit of status would look very different from the Cambridge of today. It would be a lot smaller, welcoming only the most gifted minority of students and academics. It would be full of obsessive oddballs, many of whom struggled with eye contact, let alone glad-handing. It would be an arena of constant debate, sometimes offensive and vituperative. A lot of visitors would feel excluded and alienated by a status hierarchy assessed solely on intellectual skill. To the extent that Newton could feel comfortable anywhere, he would feel comfortable in such a place.

It was always difficult for the university to play the role of both a finishing school and arbiter of truth. It is particularly difficult right now, since the reigning ideology of our times is so often at odds with this latter endeavor. A graphic displayed in guidelines issued by the Smithsonian famously described “objective, rational linear thinking” and an “emphasis on [the] scientific method” as aspects of “whiteness” and thus implicitly undesirable. Progressivism does not smile on budding Isaac Newtons.

The irony in all this is that the record of the man who first exposed Mr. Arday perfectly reveals the true nature of the university system now. Between 2022 and 2025, Nathan Cofnas was a philosophy fellow at Cambridge, but he was forced out by his college after he wrote a blog post, linked to one of his key areas of academic interest, arguing that racial differences in IQ scores are partly attributable to genetics. It’s hard to think of a more taboo subject of study—endorsements of child murder or bestiality are typically safer intellectual ground—and yet Mr. Cofnas seems not to care about social desirability. In fact, he seems to be exactly the kind of disagreeable, obsessive, obstinate oddball who might once have found a permanent home in a place like Cambridge, but who can’t possibly negotiate the university in its modern finishing school mode.

Messrs. Arday and Cofnas represent the two extremes of Cambridge’s two possible roles. Which will it choose? I think the answer is obvious. Mr. Arday has lost his position not because his colleagues at Cambridge finally got their act together, but because legacy media picked up on Mr. Cofnas’s exposé of his plagiarism and put pressure on the institution. I don’t expect Cambridge, or any other elite university, to abandon its finishing-school role anytime soon.

I wonder if over time the private sector may take on the intellectual role once played by the university. There are companies you can employ to answer almost any question you like— most commonly: “Where should I invest my money?”—as well as plenty of companies that employ scientists to carry out research that might once have been done by academics. “Objective, rational linear thinking” will reliably have market value, even if its social status is diminished. Isaac Newton might not have found a place at today’s Cambridge, which would be a great loss to the institution. But I have faith that someone, somewhere will always be willing to pay brilliant people to be weird.

Ms. Perry is a Free Expression columnist at WSJ Opinion.

### Nature is conformable (Howard Stein)
    - This is precisely the picture presented by Newton near the
      end of Query 31 of the Opticks. There he makes the explicit
      distinction, among natural powers or forces of nature,
      between 
        - the vis inertiae, as a “passive principle,”
        - and the other forces, which are “active principles”; 
    - but in both cases, he makes clear, what characterizes
      a particular such force is a law of nature (of the
      appropriate kind). 
    - Here are the principal statements; they are preceded by
      a lengthy survey of physical and chemical phenomena, all to
      show that our understanding of nature depends on the
      determination of forces of attraction and repulsion among
      particles:

        > And thus Nature will be very conformable to herself and
        > very simple, performing all the great Motions of the
        > heavenly Bodies by the Attraction of Gravity which
        > intercedes those Bodies, and almost all the small ones
        > of their Particles by some other attractive and
        > repelling Powers which intercede the Particles. 

        > The 'Vis inertiae' is a passive Principle by which
        > Bodies persist in their Motion or Rest, receive Motion
        > in proportion to the Force impressing it, and resist as
        > much as they are resisted.

    - Newton regards as the law characterizing the intrinsic
      force of matter as a natural power, not what we call the
      “law of inertia,” but the conjunction of ALL THREE Laws of
      Motion.
    - This accords with his statements in his discussion of
      Definition 3 of the Principia, where he describes the
      TWOFOLD manifestation or “exercise” of the force of
      inertia:
        - in reducing the acceleration of the body acted upon by
          an impressed force (Law 2),
        - and in the reciprocal “endeavor to change the state” of
          the body responsible for that impressed force (Law 3).

        - This view aligns with his remarks in Definition 3 of

### A priori / Rationality
    - page 327
    - In the preface to the first edition of the Principia, and
      in the third “Rules of Philosophizing” at the beginning of
      Book 3, Newton states:
        - (a) geometry is founded in _experience_; as he says,
          “in mechanical practice” and
        - (b) so is everything we know about bodies, in
          particular, their “extension, hardness,
          impenetrability, mobility, and vis inertiae”.
    - ... note these statements leave little scope, in Newton’s
      view ... for the 'a priori' or purely rational. `[19]`

        - Note `[19]`: For clarity, we should add that besides
          knowledge of God “from the appearances of things,”
          which “belongs to Natural Philosophy,” Newton also
          holds there is knowledge of God through _revelation_.
        - Of course, this is also through experience; for Newton,
          the domain of “revealed” theology, is apparently
          accessible only through historical documents. 
            - He doesn't subscribe to any claim of immediate
              religious authority; nor claim access to revelation
              through _personal inspiration_. 
            - Therefore a very arduous historical-critical
              investigation of such documents is demanded.
        - In any event, Newton never suggests that our knowledge
          of anything pertaining to natural philosophy can be
          derived from revealed truths. 
        - (He does relate some aspects of his views about space,
          for example, to passages in ancient writings, both
          pagan and Judeo-Christian; but this is far from
          ascribing evidentiary or conceptually binding force to
          such relations)
