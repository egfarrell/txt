### TYP:  Electrodynamics
    - My number one recommendation is
        - `A Student’s Guide to Maxwells Equations` (Fleisch)
        - The sole focus is to explain the fundamental EM
          concepts and equations in a student-friendly way.
        - No real-world applications but perfect for
          fundamentals.
    - Additional good textbooks
        - `Feynman Lectures Vol 2`
        - `Intro to Electrodynamics` by David J. Griffith
    - The standard reference professors love to recommend is
      "Classical Electrodynamics" by John D. Jackson.
        - However Jackson’s book is not really good for
          beginners; he spends too much time on complicated
          calculations. And it’s often not clear what's
          fundamental and what's only important for applications.


### TYP:  SR
    - A great book to understand the fundamentals is `Spacetime
      Physics` by Taylor and Wheeler. A rare exception where two
      experts have written a very student-friendly book.

    - SR is so important because it's key insight of the constant
      speed of light reveals a fundamental symmetry of nature.
        - In general, a symmetry is a set of transformations that
          keep the object in question unchanged.
        - Using SR, we learn that the speed of light must remain
          unchanged, and so, we can start to think about all
          allowed transformations for which this is true.
        - The set of these transformations is known as the
          _Poincare symmetry_ and lies at the heart of the
          Standard Model of Particle Physics and GR.

    - Knowing that our laws must be invariant under all Poincare
      transformations is a very powerful restriction and is
      almost sufficient to derive all fundamental equations
      (Klein-Gordon equation, Maxwell, the Dirac equation).

    - The math toolbox to describe symmetries is _group theory_.
    - The set of transformations that leave a given object
      unchanged along with a rule to combine them is called
      a "group".
    - The symmetry at the heart of SR is the Poincare group and
      consists of all transformations (e.g. rotations and
      translations) that leave the speed of light unchanged.

    - Therefore, to understand the deep implications of SR, you
      should learn the principles of group theory and how to
      apply them to study the Poincare group. Luckily, there are
      many great books on group theory.

    - I've written `Physics from Symmetry` and there're great
      alternatives like
        - Kurt Sundermeyer: `Symmetries in Fundamental Physics`
        - Matthew Robinson: `Symmetry and the Standard Model`

    - Be warned: all these books go far beyond SR because it's
      hard not to get excited about all the things we can learn
      once we understand group theory.


### TYP:  QM
    - My favorite QM book: `Quantum Mechanics` by David Griffith.
        - He explains the fundamental aspects of QM in detail and
          always makes sure you understand how each concept is
          used in practice.
        - And in contrast to other books, he dives right in
          without 100+ pages of preparation chapters. This makes
          the book extremely enjoyable to read and ideal for
          self-study.

    - Other good textbooks are the `Feynman Lectures Vol 3` and
      `Principles of Quantum Mechanics` by Ramamurti Shankar.

    - The standard recommended by professors is 'Quantum
      Mechanics' by Claude Cohen-Tannoudji.
        - But it's not really an intro book because it starts
          with around 100 pages of math without any proper
          motivation, and is generally lacks insightful
          comments on what is happening in calculations.


### TYP:  QFT
    - By far the best QFT textbook is `Student-Friendly Quantum
      Field Theory` by Klauber.
        - $MY_BOOKS/Physics/QM/QFT/Student-Friendly-QFT-Klauber.pdf

        - Don't let the weird cover fool you. It's a serious
          textbook with all relevant equations.
        - But it presents them as gently as possible and ensures
          the reader doesn't get lost.
            - Eg, overview charts to help show how each concept
              and equation fits into the bigger picture.

    - Other good ones:
        - Griffith: "Introduction to Elementary Particles"
        - Schwartz: "Quantum Field Theory & the Standard Model"
        - Alvarez-Gaume and Vazquez-Mozo:
            - "An Invitation to Quantum Field Theory"
            - $MY_BOOKS/Physics/QM/QFT/Invitation-to-QFT-Gaume.pdf

    - Professors recommend :
        - `An Intro to Quantum Field Theory` by Peskin & Schroder
        - `The Quantum Theory of Fields Vol. 1` by Weinberg.
        - But you should consult these only sporadically,
          especially as a beginner, because they make QFT appear
          more complicated than it really is.
        - A beginner can get lost in the details and it's hard to
          keep track of which derivations are essential.


### TYP:  GR
    - My favorite is `Relativity, Gravitation and Cosmology` by
      Ta-Pei Cheng.
        - $MY_BOOKS/Physics/Spacetime/GR-Basic-Tai-Pei-Cheng-2e.pdf
        - In contrast to almost all other books on GR, it's
          a quick read but still it explains the fundamentals
          carefully and clearly. This makes Cheng's book an ideal
          starting point.
        - But before reading it, it makes sense to read a short
          primer: `A No-Nonsense Introduction to General
          Relativity` by Sean Carroll
            - $MY_BOOKS/Physics/Spacetime/GR-Carroll-Summary.pdf

    - Other good textbooks are:
        - "Gravity" (Hartle)
            - $MY_BOOKS/Physics/Spacetime/GR-Basic-Hartle.pdf
        - "Einstein Gravity in a Nutshell" (Zee)
            - $MY_BOOKS/Physics/Spacetime/GR-Zee-Brief.pdf
            - $MY_BOOKS/Physics/Spacetime/GR-Zee.pdf
        - "A First Course in General Relativity" (Schutz)
            - $MY_BOOKS/Physics/Spacetime/Schutz-gr.pdf

### TYP:  Thermo & SM

    - The thermo lectures by Sanjoy Mahajan have nice examples
      for intuitive understanding and a useful "further reading".
        - $MY_BOOKS/Physics/Thermo/Sanjoy-Mahajan-Termo-Lectures-2004.pdf

    - Arguably the most important notion in thermodynamics is
      _entropy_. The following helped me understand it:

        - The essay on Entropy and Gambling by Brian Skinner.
            - https://gravityandlevity.wordpress.com/2009/04/01/entropy-and-gambling/

        - Entropy? Honest! by Tommaso Toffoli.
            - $MY_BOOKS/Physics/Thermo/Papers/Entropy-Honest-Tomasso-Toffoli.pdf

        - Edwin T. Jaynes' papers The Second Law as Physical Fact
          and as Human Inference and Gibbs vs Boltzmann
          Entropies.
            - ET Jaynes:
            - The second law as physical fact and as human
              inference, (1998)
            - Gibbs vs Boltzmann Entropies. doi:
              10.1119/1.1971557 (1965)

        - Thermodynamics and classical mechanics are a lot more
          similar than one might think at first glance(!).
        - This is discussed nicely in a post titled "Classical
          Mechanics versus Thermodynamics" by John Baez.
            - https://johncarlosbaez.wordpress.com/2012/01/19/classical-mechanics-versus-thermodynamics-part-1/

        - What is Statistical Mechanics? by Roman Frigg.
          Excellent starting point to get an overview quickly.
            - http://philsci-archive.pitt.edu/9133/

    - I really enjoyed `Statistical Mechanics` by James Sethna.
        - $MY_BOOKS/Physics/Thermo/Statistical-Mechanics-Sethna.pdf

    - Another good one is:
        - `Fundamentals of Statistical and Thermal Physics`
        - by Frederick Reif.
        - $MY_BOOKS/Physics/Thermo/Fundamentals-Statistical-Physics-Reif.pdf

    - When you've developed an understanding, you might enjoy:
        - The "quantropy" posts (John Baez).
            - https://johncarlosbaez.wordpress.com/2011/12/22/quantropy/
        - There's an intriguing connection between quantum
          theories and statistical mechanics.

        - There are interesting attempts to understand GR from
          a thermodynamical perspective.
        - This idea goes by the name "_entropic gravity_".
        - Quanta magazine's "The Case Against Dark Matter" by
          Natalie Wolchover has a gentle intro.
            - https://www.quantamagazine.org/erik-verlindes-gravity-minus-dark-matter-20161129/
        - see also:
            - Classical, deterministic GR encodes thermodynamic
              (statistical) behavior!
            - $MY_NOTES/physics/thermo/BH-Info-GR-Thermo.txt
