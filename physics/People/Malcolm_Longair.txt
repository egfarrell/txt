



### Preface: Quantum Concepts in Physics
    - $MY_BOOKS/Physics/Undergrad/Overview-Longair-concepts-QM.pdf
    - "An Alternative Approach to the Understanding of QM"
    - This book fulfills a long-held ambition to continue the
      story begun in *Theoretical Concepts in Physics* (Longair,
      2003). 
    - That earlier volume traced the rise of theoretical ideas up
      to the discovery of quanta and their acceptance as
      essential to the new physics of the early twentieth
      century. There was then neither space nor scope to go
      further—the mathematics and complexity of quantum theory
      deserved a book of their own.
    - Here I attempt to do for QM what I did for classical
      physics and relativity: to reconstruct how the theory
      emerged from the tangled interplay of experiment and
      mathematics between 1900 and 1930. Quantisation and quanta
      are, in my view, the greatest discoveries of
      twentieth-century physics. Though their effects are
      invisible in our everyday classical world, quantum
      mechanics underpins every phenomenon of matter and
      radiation—and thus every aspect of modern civilisation.
    - Most QM texts take the axiomatic route, beginning from
      postulates and building upward, as in Dirac’s *Principles
      of QM* (1930). 
    - My aim is different: to understand *how* those axioms arose
      and *why* the theory had to take the form it did. The
      inspiration came from van der Waerden’s *Sources of QM*
      (1967), the monumental six-volume *Historical Development
      of Quantum Theory* by Mehra and Rechenberg (1982–2001), Max
      Jammer’s *Conceptual Development of QM* (1989), and Abraham
      Pais’s *Inward Bound* (1985). These are magnificent but
      demanding works, best appreciated by readers already fluent
      in the subject. I have tried to offer a more accessible
      path.
    - My teaching experience convinced me of the value of
      approaching physics historically while assuming only the
      mathematics typical of an early undergraduate. The approach
      grew out of discussions in the Cavendish Laboratory in the
      1970s, when we designed a course called *Theoretical
      Concepts in Physics*—a voluntary, non-examinable summer
      series meant purely to deepen understanding. Its themes
      were the dialogue between experiment and theory, the
      importance of mathematical tools, the power of symmetry and
      invariance, the role of models and approximations, and the
      analysis of real scientific papers. Most of all, it aimed
      to convey enthusiasm for the living, evolving nature of
      physics.
    - Although my own research lies in astrophysics and
      cosmology, I regard these as branches of physics writ
      large. I have been privileged to witness extraordinary
      revolutions in our understanding of the Universe, but the
      same vitality pervades all physics. This book shares that
      spirit. It is not a textbook or substitute for formal
      courses, but a companion to them—an invitation to
      understand QM as a human, intellectual adventure.
    - The material is vast, and I have necessarily been
      selective. Readers seeking greater depth will find it in
      Mehra, Rechenberg, Jammer, and Pais. Like them, I am
      a traveler on this road rather than a “black-belt” quantum
      physicist. As Fitzgerald once wrote in 1902, “A Briton
      wants emotion in his science, something to raise
      enthusiasm, something with human interest.” I belong firmly
      to that school, and I hope you will share the excitement of
      the journey.
    - *Malcolm Longair* Cambridge and Venice, 2012
