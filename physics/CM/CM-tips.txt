### 'Absolute' time
    - From an orthodox Newtonian standpoint, we can think of
      "absolute time" differently from the way Newton did.
        - Instead of talking about "time itself" "flowing
          equably" etc, just take Newton as having shown there is
          a deep _preferred way_ of scaling time intervals.
        - Take any isolated system. Refer its motion to an
          inertial frame. Then there'll be a way of scaling time
          that radically simplifies its dynamical equations: 
            - the scaling that is one of the preferred Newtonian
              measures of lapse of time.
        - It is a VERY DEEP FACT ABOUT THE WORLD, then, that the
          preferred time scalings for each of the isolated
          systems are all linearly related to one another.
          Absolute time for one system is absolute time for any
          other.

### HN: History of 'action'. 'Function S'
    - $MY_NOTES/physics/CM/Schrodinger-eqn-from-CM.txt
    - Hamilton did not discover any "action". Like everybody
      since Maupertuis, Hamilton used "action" with the meaning
      "accumulated living force".
    - "Living force" is a translation of the Latin "vis viva",
      which is the old name of kinetic energy.
        - ("kinetic energy" was only introduced in 1854 by
          William Thomson; for a short time before 1854 the term
          "actual energy" was used instead of "kinetic energy"
          and was opposed to "potential energy", as defined by
          Rankine in 1853).
    - So "action", in the sense introduced by Maupertuis and used
      by everybody till the 20th century, meant integral of the
      kinetic energy (actually the 'living force' was `mv^2`, so
      double the kinetic energy).
    - Hamilton introduced a new physical quantity, never used by
      anyone before him, which he named `function S`. Unlike the
      principle of minimum action, which is false except for
      certain restricted cases, Hamilton's variational principle
      of the `function S` is always true, including in
      relativistic mechanics and QM.
    - Nowadays Hamilton's "function S" is usually called
      `Hamilton's action`, because it has the same measurement
      unit like the traditional action, even if it is a DIFFERENT
      PHYSICAL QUANTITY. 
        - "Hamilton's" is frequently dropped, which does not
          cause much ambiguity, because now the TRADITIONAL
          "ACTION" is SELDOM MENTIONED.
    - Nevertheless, whenever history is discussed, a clear
      distinction between "action" and Hamilton's `function S`
      should be maintained, otherwise it's impossible to
      understand the evolution of physics.
    - Hamilton discovered his `function S` by starting from
      Lagrange's system of equations and finding a way to deduce
      them from a simpler principle.
    - Although Lagrange, along with Euler, discovered the
      Euler-Lagrange equations for variational problems early in
      his career, he didn’t apply any variational techniques when
      later formulating his equations of dynamics. 
        - In fact, he dismissed the principle of minimum action
          as rarely useful. As a result, it was Hamilton who
          eventually discovered the connection between these
          variational principles and dynamics.
    - While Lagrange was the first to use the correct definitions
      for kinetic energy and potential energy, he just named them
      `fonction T` and `fonction V`, similarly to Hamilton's use
      of just `function S`.

### Difference configuration space, phase space
    - https://physics.stackexchange.com/questions/185612/what-is-the-difference-between-configuration-space-and-phase-space
    - key:
        - Configuration space:    positions
        - Velocity phase space:   positions and velocities
        - (Momentum) phase space: positions and momenta
    - Technical:
        - Lagrangians are defined over the `tangent bundle` to
          the configuration space. (below: the tangent bundle is
          actually the velocity space phase space?)
        - The (momentum?) phase space is just the `co-tangent
          bundle` to the configuration space.
        - The BRIDGE between the two is the natural symplectic
          structure, and the relation between the Lagrangian and
          Hamiltonian is a Legendre transform.
    - Better:
        - Configuration space is the possible `POSITIONS` of the
          mechanical system.
        - The states of `motion`, eg. velocities/momenta are NOT
          part of the configuration space.

        - The configuration space (especially when constraits are
          present) is modelled as some real, n-dimensional
          differential manifold, denoted as `C`

        - The velocity phase space is the set of all "positions"
          and "velocities" together. 
            - If `C` is the configuration space, then the
              velocity phase space naturally has the structure of
              the tangent bundle over the configuration space,
              `TC`.
            - For a point `p ∈ C`, then the elements of the fiber
              `TpC` are the possible generalized velocities of
              the system in the "`p` configuration".

        - Since the Lagrangian depends on positions AND
          velocities, it is a scalar field on `TC`

        - The momentum phase space, which is usually called the
          phase space is the COTANGENT bundle `T*C`. In this
          case, the fiber `Tp*C` is the set of all possible
          MOMENTA of the system in the "`p` configuration".

    - Jakob
        - By the `configuration` of our system, we mean
          a complete description of all locations.
        - In contrast, the `state` of a system corresponds to
          a complete description of all locations AND all
          momenta.


### Angular momentum (spinning)
    - Formula: `L = mass x velocity x radius`
    - Angular momentum is the total "rotational oomph" an object
      has behind it. While Linear momentum quantifies how hard it
      is to stop an object using a specific force, angular
      momentum tells us how hard it is to stop it _SPINNING_.
    - 3 aspects to angular momentum:
        - `MASS`: 
            - Definitely harder to stop the rotation of an iron
              dumbbell than a Styrofoam dumbbell.
        - `SPEED`: 
            - harder to stop a fast-spinning dumbbell
        - `RADIUS`: 
            - more subtle: harder to stop a long dumbbell from
              spinning

### Post-Newton: 3 developmental streams
    - 1ST STREAM: "NEWTONIAN"
        - (useful for continua & rigid bodies)
        - preferred state of motion: inertial etc but two new
          additions to Newton:
            - `infinitesimal`:
                - the fundamental `F=ma` can be applied even to
                  infinitesimal parts of a system.
                - (This allows dynamics to be applied to continua
                  e.g. fluids in motion)
            - `Rotation`:
                - there can be unchanging constant angular
                  rotation about a fixed axis when no torques
                  exist. (analogous to uniform inertial motion
                  when no forces are present)
                - and corresponding to `f=ma` there must be
                  another fundamental equation :
                    - applied torque = (moment of inertia)
                                       * (angular acceleration)
                - "moment of inertia" takes the place of inertial
                  mass
                - So, the principle of conservation of momentum
                  must be extended to include conservation of
                  angular momentum
            - These two additions allow one to deal with the
              dynamics of rigid bodies and the dynamics of
              continua.

    - 2ND STREAM: BERNOULLI / VIRTUAL WORK
        - (useful for constraints)
        - The second developmental stream has its origins in
          a wonderful generalization by James Bernoulli of an
          important principle of statics: the principle of
          virtual work.
        - Starting from an attempt to find a general method for
          dealing with the problem of the _compound pendulum_,
          James Bernoulli realized that by adding to the usual
          forces of statics, a kind of “negative dynamical force”
          whose form is taken from Newton’s Second Law, ie `−
          ma`, a new general dynamical principle can be
          uncovered.
        - Just like the development of the Newtonian mode, it was
          realised that along with forces, moments of forces,
          (ie. forces multiplied by lever arms), must be taken
          into account in solving dynamical problems.
        - In this mode, this insight is accounted for by the fact
          that the static principle of virtual work was already
          a principle generalized from the "laws of the lever"
          where the balance of moments was realized to be
          essential in finding static equilibria, along with the
          balance of forces.
        - The insights of Bernoulli were expanded by d’Alembert
          (whose name became attached to the general method by
          history), and later fully generalized by Lagrange.
        - In the hands of Lagrange this stream became a general
          and systematic method for dealing with an important
          class of dynamical problems, those where the motion of
          a system is _CONSTRAINED_ and we want to solve the
          dynamics without becoming stuck in the difficult task
          of discovering the magnitudes and directions of all the
          forces of _constraint_.


    - 3rd STREAM: 'LEAST ACTION'
        - The third developmental stream has the most curious
          origin of all. The crucial hint for finding a new
          fundamental dynamical postulate came from outside of
          dynamics. It came from optics, the theory of the light
          propagation.
        - In Alexandria, Hero noted that one could account for
          the simple law of reflection of light (that angles of
          incidence and reflection were equal), by noting that
          the path taken by light from the point of origin to
          that of reception was _shorter_ than any other path in
          which the reflecting surface was met.
        - In the 16th century, Fermat marvelously generalized
          this into a principle of "least time" for the
          propagation of light from which the Snell–Descartes law
          of refraction could be derived.
        - It was the genius of Maupertuis to see that an
          analogous principle, later to be called that of least
          action, could provide a new foundation for dynamics.
        - Euler refined Maupertuis' work, leading to the first in
          a series of formulations for dynamics (and later
          theories). These formulations stated that, in
          a process, a key quantity must reach an extreme value
          (maximum or minimum) along the actual path, compared to
          other possible paths.
    - Each path takes its own approach to the fundamental
      concepts and postulates on which dynamics is grounded.
    - Each path also provides new technical methods for solving
      particular dynamical problems.
    - And the three modes of developing dynamics are by no means
      entirely independent of one another. Each program avails
      itself of insights developed in the other approaches.

### F=ma? Euler or Newton
    - Michael Nielson: ./Nielson-Newtons-2nd-law.txt
    - We are taught the easier parts of rational mechanics from
      the Bernoullis, Euler, etc
        - ... NOT the primitive mechanics of Newton ...
    - Indeed in 1750, when Euler published the general vector
      equation `F=ma ` he announced it as "a new principle of
      mechanics;" as the axiom which 'includes all the laws of
      mechanics'".
    - Whereas today this is considered the defining emblem of
      "Newtonian" mechanics.
    - "The modern student may find it hard to understand how
      sixty years of experience with special cases had to follow
      [the Principia] before this simple conclusion was seen. (A
      conclusion which students are taught to accept
      unquestioningly in first-year physics.)
    - No-one [during these 60 years] doubted the correctness of
      'Newton's second law', at least as a rule for
      problem-solving, but what no-one saw, until it was shown,
      was that among all the various mechanical principles then
      used, it was the most general one:
        - It applies to every part of every system, and more than
          this, it suffices to get all the equations determining
          the motion of many systems."
        - "It is an incontestable fact that more than sixty years
          of research using more complicated methods even for
          rather simple problems took place before this 'new
          principle' was seen."
    - But this isn't all. Euler soon changed his mind and reached
      the conclusion that his "new principle" didn't in fact
      include "all the laws of mechanics".
        - Rather, the law of `moment of momentum` must be
          considered an independent, additional law, reducible to
          `F=ma` only in special cases:
    - "The tenet that Newton's Laws or the 'Newtonian' equations

      who limit their attention to mass-points, rigid bodies, and
      certain other special systems. ...
    - But in elasticity, Newton's laws never have been and never

        - instead "everyone in the 18th century who studied

### Discussion about F=ma
    - https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00407-019-00242-y
    - "The Principia’s 2nd law (as Newton understood it) from
      Galileo to Laplace"
    - Newton regarded his 2nd law of motion as a fundamental
      axiom of mechanics.
    - Yet the major works that came after the Principia, those
      treatises on the foundations of mechanics in the eighteenth
      century; by Varignon, Hermann, Euler, Maclaurin,
      d’Alembert, Lagrange, and Laplace — do not cite or discuss
      the Principia’s 2nd law.
    - Nevertheless, the present study shows that all these
      scientists do in fact assume the principle that the
      Principia’s second law asserts as a fundamental axiom in
      their mechanics. (For what that 2nd law asserts, we rely on
      Newton’s own testimony.)
    - Some, like Varignon and Hermann, assume the axiom
      implicitly, apparently unaware that any assumption is being
      made, while others, like Maclaurin and Euler, assume the
      axiom EXPLICITLY, apparently unaware that the assertion
      assumed is the 2nd law as Newton himself understood it.
    - But in every case, these scientists employ the principle
      asserted by the Principia’s second law while being unaware
      that they should be citing Neutonus, Prin., Phil. Nat.
