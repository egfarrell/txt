Dirac realized this wasn’t just a computational trick — it revealed that quantum mechanics is a non-commutative version of classical mechanics.
You can think of quantum theory as living on a “fuzzed-out” symplectic manifold, where the coordinates no longer commute, and uncertainty emerges geometrically.

Modern mathematical physics takes this very seriously:

    Geometric quantization tries to turn symplectic manifolds into Hilbert spaces systematically.

    Deformation quantization literally deforms the Poisson bracket into a noncommutative star product that encodes quantum corrections.

So Dirac’s observation was not just about operator algebra — it opened a whole century of research into the geometric and algebraic heart of quantization.

Dirac’s genius was realizing that quantum mechanics is just what happens when you let that bracket stop commuting.

1. Origins of phase space: where the idea was born

The concept of phase space arose in the late 19th century, mostly through Boltzmann, Gibbs, and Liouville, as a way to handle mechanical systems with many degrees of freedom.

Before that, Newtonian mechanics dealt with position and velocity separately — think of x(t) and v(t) for a particle.

But in Hamiltonian mechanics, we want to describe the entire state of the system at an instant — both where it is and how it’s moving.

So we form the state vector:

(q_1, ..., q_n, p_1, ..., p_n)

where:

    q_i are generalized coordinates (positions),

    p_i are conjugate momenta (which encode velocity + mass/geometry information).

This 2n-dimensional space — all possible combinations of positions and momenta — is the phase space.
A single point in it represents a complete instantaneous state of the system.
2. How it works

The motion of the system through time is a trajectory in phase space.
The laws of mechanics, in Hamiltonian form, say:

dq_i/dt = ∂H/∂p_i
dp_i/dt = -∂H/∂q_i

These equations define a flow on phase space — like a fluid moving through a 2n-dimensional ocean.

Liouville’s theorem says that this flow preserves phase-space volume, which is the geometric origin of statistical mechanics and conservation of probability.
3. Why mathematicians got involved

Physicists originally treated phase space as just R^{2n} — a coordinate space.
But mathematicians noticed the structure had invariant geometry independent of coordinates.

That “area element”:

Σ dq_i ∧ dp_i

behaves in a special way: it’s invariant under canonical transformations (coordinate changes that preserve Hamilton’s equations).

This observation led to symplectic geometry, which generalizes phase space to curved, coordinate-free manifolds where the same invariant structure exists.

So:

Flat R^{2n} phase space  →  Symplectic manifold (M, ω)

In short, the physicists built the house; the mathematicians later realized it was a temple.

Hamilton’s equations can be compactly written as:

        dX/dt = J ∇H

    Differential equations and dynamical systems

        Understanding trajectories, flows, equilibrium points, and conservation laws.

    Differential geometry (basic level)

        Tangent vectors, manifolds, and coordinate transformations.

Phase space is the arena of determinism.
Each point corresponds to a possible complete state of a system, and the Hamiltonian defines how those points move — deterministically, reversibly, and preserving volume.

A Sketch of the Lineage: From Newton to Symplectic Geometry
1. The Newtonian foundation (17th century)

    Isaac Newton (1643–1727) gave the first systematic laws of motion, F = ma.

    He replaced forces with a single scalar function, the Lagrangian L(q, dq/dt).

Using a Legendre transform, he defined the Hamiltonian H(q,p) and his canonical equations:

    dq_i/dt = ∂H/∂p_i

    Carl Gustav Jacobi (1804–1851) followed closely, developing the Hamilton–Jacobi equation, which linked classical mechanics to wave-like behavior — later echoing in quantum mechanics.

    Joseph Liouville (1809–1882) proved the conservation of phase-space volume (Liouville’s theorem).
    He implicitly used what we now call a volume form on phase space — an early encounter with geometric invariants.

    Ludwig Boltzmann (1844–1906) and J. Willard Gibbs (1839–1903) brought statistical mechanics to life.
    Their ensembles were probability distributions on phase space.
    Boltzmann’s “ergodic hypothesis” and Gibbs’s ensembles used Liouville’s measure without yet knowing the geometric term “symplectic.”

At this point, phase space was a physicist’s tool — geometric intuition without formal geometry.
4. The rise of differential geometry (late 19th – early 20th century)

    Bernhard Riemann (1826–1866) introduced the idea of a manifold and a metric tensor — the seed of modern geometry.

    Élie Cartan (1869–1951), a towering figure, invented the exterior calculus and differential forms.
    He realized that integration and differentiation could be written in coordinate-free fashion using wedge products ∧ and the exterior derivative d.
    His machinery made it possible to write ω = Σ dq_i ∧ dp_i and dω = 0 invariantly — this is precisely what makes a manifold symplectic.

    Around the same period, Henri Poincaré (1854–1912) brought geometry into mechanics directly.
    He studied the geometry of Hamiltonian flows and discovered Poincaré recurrence and Poincaré invariants.
    His 1899 paper Sur le problème des trois corps introduced the Poincaré integral invariants, the ancestors of the symplectic form.

Poincaré, not Hamilton, was the true progenitor of symplectic geometry as a concept.
5. The 20th-century synthesis

    Vladimir Arnold (1937–2010) reinterpreted classical mechanics in the modern language of manifolds and differential forms.
    His book Mathematical Methods of Classical Mechanics (1976) made symplectic geometry the canonical language of mechanics.

    Abraham and Marsden (1978) pushed the program further in Foundations of Mechanics, marrying physics intuition with modern differential geometry.
    This solidified the idea that “phase space = symplectic manifold.”

    Charles Ehresmann, Jean-Marie Souriau, and later Alan Weinstein formalized symplectic reduction and moment maps, connecting symmetries and conserved quantities geometrically (Noether’s theorem in symplectic dress).

6. Quantum echoes — Dirac and beyond

    Paul Dirac (1902–1984) noticed that Poisson brackets { , } in classical mechanics behave like commutators [ , ] in quantum mechanics.
    He proposed the Dirac quantization rule:

    {f, g} → (1 / iħ)[f̂, ĝ]

    establishing the direct link between symplectic geometry and quantum operator algebras.

    Later, Bargmann, Segal, Kostant, and Souriau developed geometric quantization, a rigorous attempt to construct quantum theory directly from symplectic manifolds.

    Deformation quantization (Bayen, Flato, Lichnerowicz, 1970s) completed the picture: quantum mechanics as a “noncommutative deformation” of the classical Poisson algebra.

7. Infinite dimensions — field theory and gravity

    Arnowitt, Deser, Misner (ADM, 1962) formulated General Relativity in Hamiltonian form, defining canonical variables (g_ij, π^ij) and constraints.
    This was the first infinite-dimensional symplectic system in mainstream physics.

    Dirac’s constraint theory also laid the groundwork for gauge theory and the Hamiltonian treatment of electromagnetism and relativity.

    Later, Marsden, Morrison, and others built Hamiltonian field theory for fluids, plasmas, and continua — applying symplectic ideas to PDEs.

8. Modern developments

By the late 20th century, symplectic geometry had matured into a field of its own:

    Gromov (1985) revolutionized it with symplectic topology and the concept of pseudoholomorphic curves.

    McDuff and Salamon turned those developments into the modern canonical reference.

Today, symplectic geometry connects:

    Classical mechanics and dynamical systems.

    Hamiltonian field theory.

    Quantum and semiclassical mechanics.

    String theory and mirror symmetry (where “symplectic” meets “complex” geometry).

Summary timeline

1600s  Newton — motion in space, calculus
1700s  Lagrange — generalized coordinates
1800s  Hamilton — phase space, canonical equations
1800s  Liouville, Boltzmann, Gibbs — statistical mechanics, volume invariance
1900s  Poincaré — integral invariants, topology in dynamics
1900s  Cartan — differential forms, exterior calculus
1900s  Dirac — Poisson bracket ↔ commutator (quantization)
1970s  Arnold, Abraham & Marsden — modern geometric mechanics
1980s+ Gromov, McDuff, Weinstein — symplectic topology, quantization, field theory

In essence

    Physics gave us the intuition (Hamilton, Lagrange, Dirac).

    Mathematics gave us the language (Poincaré, Cartan, Arnold).

    Together they built the symplectic framework:
    a geometry where energy, motion, and symmetry coexist as pure structure.

The fact that we can write both Newtonian trajectories and quantum commutators in the same geometric grammar — that’s the triumph of two centuries of intertwined thought.

Ah, yes — the mysterious “canonical.” It crops up so often in
Hamiltonian mechanics that it almost becomes invisible, like the
water fish swim in. It has a precise origin, and its history is
a bit of a linguistic fossil trail left by the 19th-century
mathematicians.

### 1. The original meaning: “canonical” = “according to rule”

    - The adjective canonical comes from the Greek kanon, meaning
      “rule,” “measure,” or “standard.”
    - In mathematics and physics, something canonical is the
      standard or natural form — the one that preserves the
      underlying structure, as opposed to an arbitrary
      re-labelling.
    - So a canonical transformation is, in essence, a lawful
      transformation — one that obeys the “rules” of Hamiltonian
      mechanics rather than breaking its geometric structure.

### 2. From Lagrange to Hamilton: where it entered mechanics

    - In Lagrangian mechanics, coordinates and velocities (q_i,
      dq_i/dt) are the variables.
    - When Hamilton rewrote the theory in terms of coordinates
      and momenta (q_i, p_i), the equations became first order:

        - `dq_i/dt = ∂H/∂p_I`
        - `dp_i/dt = -∂H/∂q_i`

    - These pairs (q_i, p_i) are not arbitrary; they come in
      canonically conjugate pairs.
    - They’re “canonically related” because they’re joined by the
      canonical 2-form (in modern language):
        - `ω = Σ dq_i ∧ dp_i`

    - Any transformation of variables (q,p) → (Q,P) that
      preserves this 2-form,
        - `Σ dq_i ∧ dp_i = Σ dQ_i ∧ dP_i` is called a canonical
          transformation.

    - That’s the rule — the canon — of Hamiltonian mechanics.

    - The form `dq ∧ dp` is the sacred object; 
        - transformations that preserve it are canonical.

### 3. Historical appearance of the term

    - The term “canonical” first appeared in Jacobi’s
      19th-century work on Hamiltonian systems and canonical
      coordinates (roughly 1830s–1840s).

    - He studied how to simplify Hamilton’s equations by changing
      variables while keeping their form invariant — much like
      rotations preserving the metric in Euclidean space.

    - In his work, transformations preserving the equations’
      canonical form were called canonical transformations.

    - Later, Poincaré and Darboux recast this more geometrically,
      showing that all symplectic manifolds locally look alike
      (Darboux’s theorem), which is the geometric justification
      for calling `(q,p)` canonical coordinates — the standard
      local form of the symplectic structure.

### 4. Why it’s still the right word

    - So “canonical” really means `structure-preserving`:
      a transformation that respects the canonical rules of
      Hamiltonian dynamics.

    - In modern symplectic geometry language:
        - The “canonical 1-form” is `θ = Σ p_i dq_i`
        - The “canonical symplectic form” is `ω = dθ = Σ dq_i ∧ dp_i`

        - A “canonical transformation” is a symplectomorphism:
          a diffeomorphism that preserves `ω`.

    - Thus, every use of “canonical” points back to this
      underlying structure — the geometry of phase space that
      Hamilton’s equations depend on.

5. Canonical’s career in modern physics

The word spread far beyond mechanics:

    Canonical quantization: Replace canonical variables (q,p) by non-commuting operators (q̂, p̂) satisfying [q̂, p̂] = iħ. The quantization preserves the canonical commutation relations — the quantum version of {q,p}=1.

    Canonical ensemble (statistical mechanics): An ensemble following the “standard rule” for equilibrium at fixed temperature. (Again, canonical = “by the rule.”)

    Canonical form (linear algebra): The standard or simplest form under structure-preserving transformations (e.g., Jordan canonical form).

So the word has a unifying sense: respecting the fundamental structure or rule of the system.
6. In short

“Canonical” began as an 18th–19th century way to say according to the standard form of Hamilton’s equations.
It survived into the 20th century as the marker of “structure preservation” — whether that structure is symplectic, algebraic, or physical.

Whenever you see “canonical,” your mental translation can safely be:

    “This transformation follows the sacred geometry of the system — it bends, but does not break, the law.”

That’s the philosophical punchline: canonical transformations are to Hamiltonian mechanics what isometries are to geometry — they preserve the inner fabric of motion itself.
