### 3 energies in a fluid
    - Quora: How is the 'flow energy' of a fluid different to its
      kinetic energy?

    - Any flowing fluid possesses 3 components of energy:
        - flow energy               (energy by virtue of its sustained pressure)
        - kinetic energy            (energy by virtue of motion of fluid)
        - potential or datum energy (energy by virtue of position of fluid)

    - 'Flow energy' of a fluid is energy possessed by virtue of
      its sustained pressure and it is represented by a static
      pressure head whereas kinetic energy is the energy a fluid
      has by virtue of its motion and is represented by
      a velocity head.

    - The `work` done by a section of fluid on its adjacent
      downstream section in order for the flow to take place is
      termed as 'flow work' and 'flow work per unit volume' gives
      the static pressure head.

### Stewart: Visions of Infinity
    - $MY_BOOKS/Physics/Maths/Misc-Overview/Visions-Infinity-Ian-Stewart.pdf
    - The millennium prize problems refer to _incompressible_
      flows because _compressible_ flows are known to be badly
      behaved.
    - E.g. aircraft equations run into trouble if the aircraft
      travels faster than sound. This is the famous ‘sound
      barrier’, which worried engineers when designing supersonic
      jetfighters, and the problem is related to the
      compressibility of air.
    - Incompressible fluid:
        - If a body moves through an incompressible fluid, it
          pushes the fluid particles out of the way, like
          tunnelling through a box full of ball bearings. If the
          particles pile up, they slow the body down.
    - Compressible fluid:
        - In a _compressible_ fluid, this doesn't happen;
          Because there is a limit to the speed with which waves
          can travel: the speed of sound.
        - At supersonic speeds, instead of being pushed out of
          the way, the air _piles up ahead_ of the aircraft, and
          its _density there increases without limit_.
    - The result is a SHOCKWAVE.
    - Mathematically, this is a discontinuity in the pressure of
      the air, which suddenly jumps from one value to a different
      one across the shockwave.
    - Physically, the result is a sonic boom: a loud bang.
    - If not understood and taken into account, a shockwave can
      damage the aircraft, so the engineers were right to worry.
    - But the speed of sound isn’t really a barrier, just an
      obstacle.
    - The presence of shock waves implies that the compressible
      Navier-Stokes equations need not have SMOOTH SOLUTIONS for
      all time, even in two dimensions. So the answer is already
      known in that case, and it’s negative.


### Chicken and Egg
    - Does a velocity field define streamlines?
        - Or do the streamlines create the velocity field?
    - Given a path, we can take its time derivative and get
      tangent vectors. The same works in reverse, given a (nice)
      vector fleld, one can find a family of paths so that every
      vector in the vector field is tangent to a path. Hence
      paths determine vector fields and vector fields determine
      paths. This applies to fluid dynamics in that `V` is
      a vector field tangent to the streamlines (paths).
    - You could think of the streamlines flrst and creating the
      velocity vector field, or start with the velocity vector
      field and from that construct the streamlines to which the
      vector field is tangent.
    - The conceptual and theoretical connection above is fairly
      clear. But actually going from vector eld to path can be
      a  little bit difficult. When you go from path to vector,
      you are taking a derivative. When you are going from
      a given vector eld to the paths it is tangent to, you are
      solving a differential equation.

        - CURRY: When I saw this, I thought the author was
          confused. This substantial derivative is nothing more
          than the regular full on derivative!
        - But that’s just not right. It looks like the CHAIN RULE
          formally, but the two objects are about different
          things! (different physical situations)
        - "TOTAL" Derivative
            - The chain rule says that if you have a thing moving
              along a path given by (x(t), y(t),Z(t)) and you
              want to flnd out how fast a function p(x, y, z, t)
              is changing with time, as the value of `p` changes
              everywhere and the point we are monitoring moves
              along the given path, then you use that terrible
              formula.
        - "SUBSTANTIVE" Derivative
            - But this is something else! The point we are
              monitoring does not move along the path! We keep
              staring at a flxed point `r` and watch what passes
              through it.
            - The fluid element passes through our monitoring
              point with velocity `V`. This is a physically
              different situation!
            - So, yes, the formula for the substantial derivative
              is formally the same as that for the ordinary
              derivative using the chain rule.
            - But they are completely different things because
              the substantial derivative describes a DIFFERENT
              PHYSICAL SITUATION.

### Important difference: Differential / Integral forms
    - There's a subtle difference between the integral
      & differential forms of the governing flow equations.
    - The integral form 
        - allows for the presence of DISCONTINUITIES inside the
          fixed control volume (fixed in space). 
        - There is no inherent mathematical reason to asume
          otherwise.
    - But the differential form 
        - assumes the flow properties are differentiable, hence
          _continuous_. 
        - This is very evident when we use the divergence theorem
          to derive the `differential form` from the `internal
          form`.
        - The divergence theorm assumes mathematical continuity.
    - There is a strong argument for the integral form to be
      considered more _fundamental_.
    - This distinction becomes more important when calculating
      a flow with real discontinuities, eg 'shock waves'

### Advection
    - A common problem in gas & liquid flows is advection: 
        - the process of fluid motion carrying some other
          quantity along with it. 
        - ("Fluid" here is generic, meaning liquid or gas)
    - Imagine putting a drop of dye into water. 
        - As the water flows, it carries the dye with it, and
          we'd like to calculate how much dye will be present at
          a given point in the water at a given time.
    - Another example: 
        - advection of heat by a fluid. 
        - The same idea as the dye, except _heat_ is being
          carried around by the fluid instead – warm and cold
          bits of fluid are moving around, and we want to know
          the temperature at any given point and time.
