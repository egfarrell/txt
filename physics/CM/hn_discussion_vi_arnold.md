# VI Arnold book (HN)

- "VI Arnold's Mathematical Methods of Classical Mechanics is one
  of my favorites."

- People keep saying that book is "out-of-date" with its
  approach, but I like it anyway.

- How is it out of date?

- I was told the perspective and techniques he uses are "old", in
  the sense they dont line up with how the subject is currently
  approached or taught.

- Is there any book on the subject thats up-to-date and well
  written?

- Well some people recommended the following:

    - Cannas da Silva, "Lectures on Symplectic Geometry"
    - McDuff & Salamon "Intro. to Symplectic Topology"
    - Zehnder          "Lectures on Dynamical Systems"
    - Audin & Babbit   "Hamiltonian Systems & Integrability"

- Personally, I've only been through the first two, and enjoyed
  them.

- Those recommendations arent on the same subject though. Arnold
  is a physics book really. All the books you suggested aren't.
  (save the last)

- I've a copy of Cannas da Silva's book and there's some physics
  stuff in there, but it's through the context of symplectic
  manifolds and Hamiltonian mechanics. Looking through the
  contents of McDuff and Salamon, it also talks about Hamiltonian
  stuff, as well as some perturbation theory.

- About a half of Arnold's book (excluding appendices) is on
  Hamiltonian mechanics, and he spends some time on forms and
  symplectic geometry.

- The other half is classical material like Newton's equations,
  and variational principles and Lagrangian mechanics.
    - If I'm not mistaken, the point we're making is Arnold's
      discussion of these topics is outdated, and other books
      make better introductions.
    - I can also vouch for Cannas da Silva, theres plenty of
      mathematical physics in there as well if that's what you're
      looking for.



- Arnold's book isn't really a physics book -  more a math book
  that uses classical mechanics as a motivation for the math.

- The main focus is more dynamical systems, Hamiltonian systems,
  symplectic geometry and perturbation theory.
  - Some of those topics are widely applied in physics, and in
    many cases developed due to physical problems, but the math
    is still the primary focus. It is a bit like calling a QM
    textbook a chemistry book.


- I read something by Arnold lamenting the separation of math and
  physics, so I would imagine he sees it as pretty well both
  a math and physics text.
