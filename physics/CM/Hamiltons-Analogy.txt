### Basics:
    - The propagation of light can be considered in terms of rays
      and wavefronts in ordinary physical 3D space.
    - The wavefronts are 2D curved surfaces; the rays are 1D
      curved lines.
    - Hamilton's analogy amounts to two interpretations of
      a figure like the one shown here.
    - In the optical interpretation,
        - the `green` wavefronts are lines of constant phase and
        - the orthogonal [^red] lines are the rays of geometrical
          optics.
    - In the mechanical interpretation,
        - the `green` lines denote constant values of action
            - (derived by applying Hamilton's principle to
              mechanical motion) and
        - the [^red] lines are the orthogonal object
          trajectories.
    - The orthogonality of the wavefronts to rays (or
      equal-action surfaces to trajectories) means we can compute
      one set from the other.

### 1. Geometrical Optics and Wavefronts
    - In classical (geometrical) optics, we often model light as
      traveling along straight lines or rays. These rays are
      perpendicular to surfaces of constant phase, called
      wavefronts. Think of a stone dropped into a pond—the
      expanding ripples are circular wavefronts, and the
      direction in which the ripple moves outward (perpendicular
      to the ripple lines) corresponds to the ray.
    - Rays: 1D paths that light follows.
    - Wavefronts: 2D surfaces of constant phase (e.g. all points
      on the wave that have oscillated to the same degree).
    - In a medium with varying refractive index (like air near
      a hot road), these rays bend—Snell’s law governs how—and
      the wavefronts become curved.

### 2. Hamilton's Principle in Mechanics
    - In mechanics, Hamilton developed a reformulation of
      Newtonian dynamics using the principle of stationary
      action. In this view, the path that a particle takes
      between two points in space and time is the one for which
      the action (a quantity integrating kinetic and potential
      energies over time) is stationary (usually minimized).
    - Action (S): Defined as the integral of the Lagrangian (L
      = T - V) over time.
    - Particles "choose" the path for which the action is
      stationary.
        - The surfaces of constant action can be thought of as
          mechanical wavefronts.

### Key point:
    - Now here's the leap:
        - these constant-action surfaces in mechanics behave
          mathematically like wavefronts in optics.

### 3. The Optical–Mechanical Analogy

    - Hamilton noticed you can interpret certain mathematical
      structures in optics as analogous to those in mechanics:

    - Optics                                      Mechanics
    - ------                                      ---------
    - Rays                                        Trajectories of particles
    - Wavefronts (constant phase)                 Surfaces of constant action
    - Snell's Law                                 Conservation of momentum
    - Fermat's principle                          Hamilton’s principle
        - (`light` follows path of least time)        - (`particle` follows path of stationary action)

    - In both cases:
        - Rays/trajectories are orthogonal to wavefronts/action
          surfaces.
        - You can compute rays if you know the wavefronts, and
          vice versa.

        - In this analogy, the equations governing light rays
          (from Fermat’s principle) and mechanical trajectories
          (from Hamilton’s principle) are mathematically
          identical—this is an example of a variational principle
          in action.

4. Visualizing the Analogy
    - $MY_IMAGES/physics/CM/hamiltons-analogy.jpg

    - Imagine a contour map (like those used for topography),
      where each contour line represents a constant value of
      action (in mechanics) or constant phase (in optics):

    - The particle or light ray always crosses these lines
      perpendicularly.

    - Where the lines are close together, the `gradient` is
      steep—meaning rapid change, which corresponds to higher
      speed or stronger force (in mechanics) or higher refractive
      index (in optics).

    - So:
        - Green lines: wavefronts (optics) / constant action surfaces (mechanics).
        - Red lines: rays (optics) / particle trajectories (mechanics).

    - By calculating the "green" lines using the Hamilton–Jacobi
      equation (a reformulation of Hamilton’s principle), we can
      find the red lines by following the gradient of these
      surfaces.

### 5. Deeper Implications and Unity

    - This analogy isn't just _mathematical poetry_—it's deeply
      rooted in the physics:
        - It bridges wave behavior (optics) and particle dynamics
          (mechanics).
        - It foreshadows wave-particle duality seen in QM.

    - In fact, Schrödinger’s equation (foundation of wave
      mechanics) comes from applying wave ideas to mechanics,
      inspired by this analogy.

    - The `eikonal equation` in optics and the `Hamilton–Jacobi
      equation` in mechanics are formally the same type of PDE.
