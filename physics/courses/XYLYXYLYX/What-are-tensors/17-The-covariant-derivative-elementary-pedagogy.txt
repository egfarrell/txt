    -  One thing that I struggle with:  In your derivation of the
       connection you obtain an estimate for X at the point
       `x+∆x` by using Taylor's theorem.  
    - I.e. you are using differentiation.  
    - And yet the whole problem with determining the covariant
      derivative is the impossibility of getting a sense of
      parallelism when dealing with vectors in two different
      vector spaces.   
    - Why does this problem not arise when you use Taylor's
      theorem to get an approximation for the value of `X` at
      `x+∆x` through the use of the value of `X` at `x`.
        - Excellet question. Its a good practice to always run
          down little nagging confusions like these. `X` is
          a _vector field_ and it is a function of space time:
          `X(x)`. 
        - Therefore the components of `X` are also functions on
          space time: `X^i(x)`. There is no problem taking the
          derivative of these component functions with respect to
          the coordinates. That is the ordinary derivative and it
          exists just fine. 
        - Since the ordinary derivative exists, Taylor’s theorem
          applies just fine as well to estimate the components at
          nearby points in space time. 
        - The problem is that these estimated new components
          describe a vector in a different vector space so we
          cant compare them with the components of the vector
          field at `x`. 
        - We have to find a vector at the new point, `x+∆x`,
          to compare with these new components. 
        - The big issue is that the ordinary derivative should
          also be a vector, but the ordinary derivative does not
          transform like a vector and therefore is not a vector.
          That does not mean the ordinary derivative is useless!
          In fact our final result shows that the connection
          gives us a “correction” term that makes the ordinary
          derivative a true vector.
