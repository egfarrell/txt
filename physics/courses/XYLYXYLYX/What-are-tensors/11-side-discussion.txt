    - Alan Grayson Let me lay the foundation for an answer:

    - The term “coordinate system”I have seen used two ways. The
      most important way, and the way you are using it I’m sure,
      means “coordinate system on the manifold.” That is the
      usage in GR and it is the most common usage. Keep that in
      mind for a moment.

    - Now, if we consider a single vector space V and its dual V*
      we can build up all the possible tensor product spaces. We
      can create an inner product or, equivalently, declare
      a particular (0,2) tensor to be the metric tensor on
      V (remember that defining an inner product on V and
      declaring a (0,2)-rank tensor as the special metric tensor
      are equivalent). When we do this, we typically also assert
      a basis in V even if the basis is rather abstract: “e_i”
      for example. If we do all this, we are really doing all
      this for only ONE vector space, namely “V”. This discussion
      had no manifold involved at all and therefore no
      “coordinate system”. In fact the words “coordinate system”
      , under the first usage, have no real meaning: no manifold,
      no coordinates. So the most technically correct answer to
      your question is “no”.

    - However, occasionally the use of the word “coordinate
      system” is applied to the basis of a single vector space
      and when we identify/choose a basis for V some might say we
      have “chosen a coordinate system on V”. After all, every
      vector in V can now be expresses by its components in that
      basis, which is an ordered n-tuple that can be treated like
      the “coordinates of a vector in V with the basis e_i”.
      Also, a vector space is also its own trivial manifold with
      one chart into R^n. Each possible basis on the vector space
      is actually a chart mapping onto R^n and therefore it is
      not wrong to think of a choice of basis in V to be
      “creating a coordinate system on V”. It is technically
      correct, but this usage is rare, but I have seen it and if
      you see it don’t let it confuse you.

    - In GR we always want to apply the first usage: coordinate
      system applied to the manifold of space time. In that work,
      we presume the existence of the space time manifold,
      presume the existence of coordinates on that manifold,
      presume the existence of tangent spaces at every point on
      the manifold, apply the “coordinate basis” to every tangent
      space at every point all at once, and proceed to study GR.
      All of these presumptions necessarily exist: a manifold by
      definition has coordinate charts homeomorphic to R^4,
      a manifold by construction has tangent spaces T(p), and the
      differential operators `\partial_\mu` can act as a basis for
      a realization of each of the uncountable infinite number of
      tangent spaces associated with the manifold.

    - However, if for some reason, as your question implies, we
      took only ONE tangent space and focused our attention only
      on that and set up some basis on that isolated tangent
      space ignoring all the others, then establishing a basis
      and inner product or metric in that single isolated tangent
      space would NOT have any implications for the manifold at
      all. It always goes the other way: the coordinate system
      assumed for the manifold creates a basis for every tangent
      space. Namely, the “coordinate basis”. From that basis we
      can make different basis using the “tetrad formalism” but
      in the end, AFAIK, it always ties back to the coordinate
      basis which is completely linked to coordinates on the
      manifold.

    - The next logical question is “well, then why do we speak of
      ‘coordinate free’ formalism at all?” <—- a bit of a long
      answer for later.

    - Alan Grayson It is a core assumption of GR that space time
      can be modeled as a 4D manifold. A 4D manifold by
      construction has coordinates. That is what a manifold is:
      a set that can be given coordinates. Then we assert, again
      by assumption, that the laws of nature do not care about
      the coordinate system chosen to establish those laws. Then,
      based on these assumptions, we are force to write down the
      mathematical laws of nature in a way such that the form of

      system changes. The only way to do that is with equations
      that are constructed from tensors.

      true for any physical law that was constructed from
      non-tensorial mathematical objects. The point is that
      coordinate systems can always be applied and they can also
      be arbitrarily changed. The form of physical laws must be
      blind to the choice and the change.

    - The notion of “coordinate free” formalism is just a way of
      writing tensor equations so that the arbitrary choice and
      change of coordinates is hidden from view. It does NOT mean
      “coordinates don’t exist”.
