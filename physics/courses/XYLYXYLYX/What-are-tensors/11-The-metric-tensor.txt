### Connection between a space and it's dual
    - Now we have a big change in the relationship between
      a vector space and it's dual space. 
    - Up to now you didn't really have a natural linkage between
      a vector in the underylying space, and in the dual space.
      Sure you could write out a co-vector in the dual with the
      same "components" as the underlying vector. But they have
      the same "components" but they're not related in any way.
    - The metric will help me pick out a natural connected vector
      in the dual space.

    - When you established the relation between the V and V*  you

        - Yes. The metric tensor is only "special" because we
          have chosen it to be special, but any (0,2) tensor will
          perform a mapping that could be used as a metric if we
          want. However, in GR we will have some restrictions on
          our choice. In particular will will be forced to choose
          a symmetric (0,2) tensor for example. Also, a GR metric
          will have to have a certain "signature" in order to
          connect GR with Special Relativity.. But mathematically
          speaking, we could choose any (0,2) tensor as a metric
          and they will all execute the same sort of mappings and
          contractions as you point out.

    - Can I summarize this lesson as: taking the inner product of
      A (superscript alpha) e (subscript alpha)  and B in V or

      and operating on B will result in the same real number.
      Hence in the end we don't care whether we used the dual

        - Yes, that is correct. However, in GR you will always
          use the dual space vectors to make the mapping because
          we always use the metric tensor to execute inner
          products. It is slightly hidden, but whenever you see
          a subscript on the vector component you know that the
          metric tensor has been applied to lower it. Many texts
          do not even mention the notion of an "inner product"
          and some even define `A^mu B_mu` as the "inner
          product." But if you understood this lesson then none
          of these differences will confuse you.
