# 06 Scalars, Vectors and All That

- I'd like to give the proper definition of scalars and vectors
  after motivating the reason for them.

- First, lets recall the naive (school) definition:
    - a scalar only has a magnitude
    - and a vector has both a magnitude and direction

- But these definitions aren't adequate and I'll puncture them. 

- Look at a particular point `p` in a coordinate plane with
  `x` and `y` coordinates:
    - $MY_IMAGES/physics/bala-fundamental/lec06-001.jpg

- Now is `x` is scalar?
    - Well `x` has a sign, it has both plus and minus
      possibilities. And is this quantity `|x|` a scalar? It only
      has a magnitude and runs from zero to arbitrarily high
      values. So is this a scalar?
    - And the answer is no, it's not a scalar (!).

- And if you think a vector only has magnitude and direction, the
  question is:
    - direction with respect to what?
    - And the answer is:
    - direction with respect to some fixed coordinate system.

- But there's no special coordinate system!
- ... and who gives you this coordinate system anyway!

- So, the point is: these naive definitions are inadequate.
  They're convenient once we know what we're talking about - but
  they're not rigorous and are highly incomplete.

- So we need proper definitions and an explanation of why we use
  them ...

- Now, here's Newtons 2nd law in a fixed coordinate system `S`:
- $MY_IMAGES/physics/bala-fundamental/lec06-002.jpg

- The directions `F` and `A` are specified with respect to
  a fixed origin `O` and axes `x` and `y`. (simplest case,
  2 dimensions)

- And the quantity `F` has two components: `F_x` and `F_y`.
- It's clear these components are wrt the origin `O` and specific
  to this reference frame.

- But another observer could examine the same physical force, on
  the same physical body, with the same mass, from another
  reference frame `S'` (primed).
- $MY_IMAGES/physics/bala-fundamental/lec06-001.jpg

- This primed coordinate system `S'`, has primed origin `O'` and
  primed coordinate axes `x'` and `y'`.
- The question: what is an observer in `S'` going to observe?

- Maybe they'll see a different result than the other `S`
  frame(?!) But our textbook just says `F = m A` without saying
  which coordinate system.

- So logic says it's probably independent of coordinate system.

- In other words,  for the primed `S'` frame, we presume the rule
  is: `F' = m A'`. ie the same form in all reference frames.

- (Aside: We made an assumption here that `m` doesn't change and
  it's a scalar. So, in some sense I'm already begging the
  question.
    - But assume that `m` is the naive definition which is the
      inertial mass. It's a property of the substance and has
      nothing to do with coordinate systems)

- So if we say the law has the same form between different
  frames, then it must be expressed by quantities whose
  _transformation property_ is encoded in the quantities
  themselves.

- In other words, the vector quantities `F` and `A` must carry
  a dictionary in their very meaning so if
    - you tell me how `S'` is situated with respect to `S`,
    - and give me a rule `F = mA`,
    - then I can predict `F'_x, F'_y, A'_x, A'_y` for you.

- In other words, if you want the physical law to be unchanged in
  form, (to be form invariant under coordinate transformations),
  you must express them in terms of quantities whose
  transformation law is known, prescribed, once and for all.

- Such quantities are called _covariant quantities_.

- This is such an important principle (its at the root of modern
  physics) that I am going to write it down:
    - $MY_IMAGES/physics/bala-fundamental/lec06-003.jpg
    > Physical laws are expressed in terms of covariant
    > quantities, so that they remain FORM invariant.

- Now, thats a lot of words for something straight-forward. It
  just says we believe more and more generally that our laws are
  observer independent.

- Modern physics is an effort to discover laws of nature in such
  a manner that they are completely independent of the observer's
  special status.

- For that we need to express laws in a form that doesn't change
  going from one observer to another. And to do that you need to
  express them in terms of quantities which carry their own
  transformation laws, their own dictionary. Now scalars and
  vectors and generalizations are such quantities under specific
  kinds of transformation. This is what I'd like to demonstrate
  today.

- I repeat... the reason to use scalars, vectors. In particular
  vectors you can write in 3 dimensions, you can write
  3 equations in one line and you write in terms of vector, it is
  not to save space. It's to make sure, you are expressing form
  invariant physical laws in a manner where you can translate
  from one frame to another or from one observer to another.

- In this case, if `f` and `a` are vectors in the definition I'm
  going to give, then you're guaranteed `F = ma` is
  form invariant under the transformations from one observer to
  another,  or one frame to another under which these quantities
  are vectors that set of transformations will be seen shortly
  and under that set of transformations you are then assure that
  this law will not change its form, numerically yes it will
  change.

- Because, any acceleration you measure in this frame, the
  numerical values you have for these components at any
  instance of time would be different from the numerical values
  you have in this frame as would the force components. 

- But, what you are assured is that if you give me `f` and `a`,
  the components of `f` and `a` in this frame and tell me how
  `o'` is related to `o`, how far is it move, how much it rotated
  then I'll predict for you that in this frame you will
