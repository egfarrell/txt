Representation
    - you can represent a group in more than one way
    - don't confuse the representation with the group itself.

    - you could always represent the objects of a group by other
      objects, and form a rule to go from an element in the group
      to that other object.

    - so this other set of objects is a representation of the
      group. It may be a _faithful_ representation of the group:
        - it may be a _one-to-one_ mapping between both sets
          (isomorphism) 
        - or a _one-to-many_ mapping between both sets
          (homomorphism)

### Representation of ℝ^1
    corresponding to every element a ∈ ℝ^1
    I associate a matrix L_a
    1 a
    0 1
    and replace addition in ℝ^1 with multiplication
    by these matrices

    a ∘ b = a + b
    `L_a L_b = L_(a+b)`

    Therefore this set of matrices forms a representation
    of the Lie Group ℝ^1

    - But its not the same as ℝ^1, these are different objects.
      Also the rule has been changed

    - but theres no problem, instead of ℝ^1 I could talk about
      this, and its a perfectly good Representation

    Techncially
        ℝ^1 ~ {L_a}
        The group ℝ^1 is isomorphic to the group {L_a}

        {L_a} is a 2x2 matrix representation
        of the real numbers

        its not the simplest representation (or even very
        useful), but everything you can do with real numbers
        you can do with {L_a}

### another ℝ^1 mapping
    to the S^1 sphere (its a circle!)
    `f:x -> e^(2πi x)`
    each revolution of 2π increments by another integer
    so you actually can many different integers represented
    by the same point on the S^1 circle
    This is a many-to-one relationship so is a homomorphism

    - but many different _integers_ on ℝ^1 are mapped to the
      identity element (identity where θ=0)

    - so all those integers (e.g 0, 1, -1) are called the
      "_kernel_" of the homomorphism i.e. the kernel of this S^1
      mapping is ℤ

    so ℝ^1/ℤ ~ S^1

    if you have a point on S^1, you can say where it came
    from in ℝ^1 modulo an integer. (you can always add an
    integer to it)

    _locally_ a segment on the circumference of s^1 looks
    like a bit of the line ℝ^1
    but _globally_ thats not true, because the curvature on
    S^1 takes over and has an impact

    so locally in a homomorphism you have a mapping that
    looks one-to-one, globally it isnt (and in this case
    theres a non trivial kernel)

generators and lie algebras
    - once I say the elements are _analytic functions_, I would
      like to write these functions down.
    - and very often they are exponential functions. and if they
      are derivable from the identity element in a specific way,
      then you can generate the entire set of elements by using
      a set of _infinitesimal generators_.

    - and these generators will obey the _lie algebra_,
      corresponding to the lie group.

    - so in a sense, you can replace the study of the group by
      the study of the lie algebra.

    - and this is the idea behind writing down things which are
      analytic functions.
