### Chapter 1 Falling-body Law – Vast & Excellent Science

- In the early Renaissance Thomas Aquinas and others moulded
  ideas taken from Greek thought (notably from Aristotle) into an
  all-encompassing world-view called `scholasticism`.
- The unifying principle of scholasticism was `teleology` – the
  study of the purposes of things.
- This is what Aristotle termed the “final cause” of anything.
  E.g. the “final cause” of a chair is “to be sat on”.
- But in natural science it acquires a religious significance
  – the philosopher observes nature to discern God’s will at
  work, thus providing a harmonious link between science and
  theology.

- Galileo’s approach was more sophisticated. His Aristotelian
  schooling taught him to respect the value of observation but he
  had even greater admiration for Plato who worshipped the
  abstract beauty of geometry.
    - We must look beyond the imperfect world revealed by our
      fallible senses to uncover a more perfect reality, the only
      fit object of study.
    - He found a happy fusion between the two approaches – he
      quested after hidden truth written in the deep language of
      mathematics but experience had shown him that reason
      unaided by the senses can be lead astray.
    - However passive observation is no better – Nature will not
      reveal her secrets to any fool. She must be tricked into
      showing her hand by human ingenuity and contrived
      situations (experiments) which emphasise the hidden
      reality.
    - But above all Galileo rejected teleology. It is labour
      enough to uncover the how of nature; the _why_ must remain
      forever beyond the methods of science.

- Aristotle saw motion as a contest between `propulsion` and
  `resistance`. He guessed the resulting speed would depend on
  the `ratio` between the two.
    - Double the effort, by adding rowers or sails, and a boat
      might well move twice as fast.
    - Cutting the resistance was another way to add speed, which
      was why the wheel was invented and why boat builders
      smoothed their hulls.
    - Though Aristotle lacked any means of putting his rule to
      a quantitative test, we will see in Chapter 5 he was not
      far from the mark. (really?!)

- However even Aristotle realised there was one `familiar motion`
  his scheme found hard to explain. Falling objects obviously
  pick up their speed as they drop.
    - But is the resistance decreasing or the propulsion growing?
    - Aristotelians debated the point but couldn’t offer a ready
      answer. Also a literal reading of Aristotle suggested that
      an objects speed of descent is fully proportional to its
      weight.
        - So shouldn't heavy objects fall faster than lighter
          ones? But experience shows light objects fall nearly as
          fast as heavy ones.

- Though Aristotle never thought of his science in terms of
  immutable laws, but simply as generalisations of experience
  that might allow exceptions, Galileo chose to attack these weak
  points by proposing:


1. In a medium totally devoid of resistance all bodies will fall
   at the same speed.
    - The words “devoid of resistance” signal a clear break from
      the Aristotelian formulation where zero resistance would
      imply infinite velocity.
    - They also suggest a vacuum, a concept prevailing scientific
      thought considered unnatural – “nature abhors a vacuum”.
    - Since a vacuum was then impossible in practice, Galileo
      proclaimed his refusal to take nature as he found it;
        - he was intent on describing a `perfect state` to which
          nature can only approximate.
    - However Galileo wasn’t totally cavalier: If he cannot make
      resistance go away, he will prove his point by making it
      worse!
        - “Have you not observed that two bodies which fall in
          water, one with a speed a hundred times greater than
          the other, will fall in air with speeds so nearly equal
          that one will not surpass the other by as much as
          a hundredth part?” (e.g. a marble egg and a hen’s egg)
        - I.e. if deviations from his law are far less in a thin
          medium than in a dense medium, is it not reasonable to
          suppose they would disappear if the medium were absent
          altogether?


2. During equal intervals of `time` a FALLING body receives
   equal `increments` of speed.

    - This was not particularly radical. Aristotle might well
      have applauded it as an extension of his style of physical
      thinking. But it illustrates two roles of mathematics in
      the “new science”.
    - It extends our ordinary language and also provides a means
      of producing quantitative predictions that are the acid
      test of new ideas.

    - If a car goes from `0` to `45` miles per hour in `5
      seconds` it experiences an (average) acceleration of `45/5
      = 9` miles per hour per second.
    - If this car experiences constant acceleration then it hits:
        - 9 mph after one second,
        - 18 mph at the end of two and
        - 90 mph after 10 seconds.

    - ... later ...

    - In SI units this is `4 meters per second` per second
      (m/s^2). What on earth is a “square second”?
    - The square merely signifies that to get from measurements
      of distance to acceleration you must divide by time twice.

    - `Distance / time` » `speed / time` » `Acceleration`
    - Or
    - `Acceleration (x time)` » `speed (x time)` » `Distance`

- If Galileo’s readers were familiar with this language he could
  have condensed his description of falling bodies to: (1) + (2):
    - In the absence of resistance, all bodies experience the
      same constant acceleration.

- However in Galileo’s day no instrument could directly measure
  either acceleration or speed and his law was stated in terms of
  something that couldn’t be directly observed.

- Now the second role of Mathematics comes in. Using simple
  algebra Galileo was able to convert his statement about
  constant acceleration into a relation between things he could
  measure: distance and time.
    - For an object uniformly accelerating from rest, Galileo
      derived the distance as proportional to the square of the
      time.
    - An object released from rest will drop 5m in the first
      second, 20m or four times as far in two seconds, 45m in
      three seconds and so on.

- Because heavy objects fall so quickly Galileo also had to
  `stretch` out time. He asserted, but could not actually prove,
  that rolling a ball down an inclined plane would “dilute” the
  motion without fundamentally altering its character.
    - Using a smooth board with a small tilt he studied motion
      that took 10 seconds to complete.
    - His timer was a water jar with a spigot.
    - The ball was released from various positions while the
      spigot was opened and water flowed into a cup. Afterwards
      the cup was weighed and the distance travelled found to be
      in proportion to the square of the weight of the water.
      This bore out Galileo’s prediction!

- The essence of Galileo’s experimental method: Start with an
  idealised description stated in terms of immeasurable
  quantities. Use mathematical skill to convert this into
  a statement about things you can measure.
- Slow Nature down in a way that you think changes nothing
  significant – a far cry from passive Aristotelian observation!
- But neither is it pure Platonism – da Vinci had searched for
  a mathematical rule for distances travelled in successive
  seconds and guessed it would follow a sequence of ordinary
  numbers, e.g. 1,2,3,4,… This was pure numerology but not quite
  right.
- Significantly Galileo had started knowing the correct relation
  of distance to time, having already discovered it
  experimentally! The principle of uniform acceleration was not
  his starting point (see Projectile Motion), but the conclusion
  of a long chain of discovery.


- Was Aristotle so wrong after all? If pure reason can lead
  a scientist astray, so too can unreasoned experimentation.
  Galileo’s incline experiments never encountered terminal
  velocity. If he had observed the fall of fairly light objects
  from great heights he would have seen a straight line graph of
  distance against time (i.e. constant speed), instead of
  a curved acceleration graph (i.e. proportional to `t^2`). Why?
- As a body speeds up, the resistance of air to its motion
  increases. Eventually a speed is reached where the force from
  the upward rush of air matches the force pulling the object
  down, and no further acceleration takes place.

- If one compares bodies of the same size and shape, their
  terminal velocities are proportional to their weights, a result
  compatible with Aristotelian physics(!!)
- In fact if Galileo had studied motion using a golf ball
  descending in water (reaches terminal velocity in a fraction of
  a second) instead of an inclined plane, he might have concluded
  that Aristotle was basically right after all. He could have
  ignored the period of the fall before the object reaches
  terminal velocity (i.e. constant acceleration) as a short term
  effect that soon goes away. Instead he chose to ignore the
  equally obvious small differences in the fall of heavy objects.

- Galileo made the right choice, but that is clear only in the
  light of subsequent scientific history. In some respects,
  Aristotle’s approach might have led to a better description of
  nature. The lesson here is there is nothing automatic about
  scientific progress.

- Suppose this was a current research problem. Modern PhD
  physicists might well receive grants to study falling objects
  of all possible combinations of shape, size, weight, medium
  etc, filling computer files with data and churning out
  scientific papers. Chances are that without a complete theory
  they would move towards an Aristotelian point of view to
  explain the data. They would miss Galileo’s insight altogether.

- Science is more than a mere attempt to describe nature as
  accurately as possible. Frequently the real message is well
  hidden, and a law that gives a poor approximation to nature can
  prove more significant that one that _works fairly well but
  misses an essential point_.

- The centrepiece of Galileo’s TWO NEW SCIENCES was a new,
  idealised mathematical description of the motion of falling
  bodies, a problem area for established Aristotelian physics.

- In the absence of resistance, all objects regardless of weight
  would fall at exactly the same speed and gain speed through
  uniform acceleration, where the change in speed is proportional
  to the time.

- To make this experimentally testable, he showed that it implies
  that the distance fallen is proportional to the square of the
  time. Even then he was obliged to test it not on freely falling
  objects but on a ball rolling down an inclined plane. This was
  an outstanding example of the distinction between experimental
  science and the passive observation of nature.

- Though Galileo’s description was inadequate for understanding
  the fall of real objects subject to resistance, it led to later
  progress by others and plays a central role in our
  understanding of gravity to this day.



### Chapter 2 Projectiles

- Galileo was forbidden to state the earth could be moving
  without our being aware of its motion, so he let projectiles
  make the argument for him.
    - His falling-body law (Chapter 1) is actually a by-product
      of this research. Galileo used two important physical
      principles which he did not invent:

- _Inertia_: An object moving on a level surface will continue to
  move in the same direction (in a straight line?!) at constant
  speed unless disturbed.

- _Superposition_: If an object is subjected to two separate
  influences, each producing a characteristic type of motion, it
  responds to each without modifying its response to the other.

- Both principles are needed to counter the argument that if the
  earth was moving you wouldn’t come down in the same place if
  you jumped in the air.
    - Inertia allows you to continue to share the motion of the
      earth (which is nearly a straight line) and superposition
      ensures that your jump will respond to gravity in the same
      way as if the earth were standing still.

- Inertia was a choice between two extreme ways of idealising
  phenomenon. Motion in the real world has a tendency to continue
  after whatever causes it is removed, but it persists for only
  a limited time.
    - Two extreme examples: a stone dragged across rough ground
      or a hockey puck on ice.
    - The Aristotelian approach concerned itself with the first
      case and dismissed the persistence of motion as a temporary
      condition.
    - By Galileo’s time however, there was support for the idea
      that motion has a natural tendency to continue unless
      something interferes.
    - Both Galileo’s and Aristotle’s approaches have intuitive
      appeal and there’s no obvious basis for a choice between
      them.
    - Like the falling-bodies situation, the most significant
      test was not which described a larger share of motions
      found in nature but which ultimately lead to a deeper
      understanding.

- Superposition can be demonstrated by firing a bullet
  horizontally and at the same instant dropping another bullet
  from the height of the muzzle: both will hit the ground at the
  same time.
    - In the absence of air resistance, the rapid horizontal
      motion has no effect on the vertical motion.

- Galileo experimented with a “ski jump” ramp in place of a gun.
  A ball rolled down the ramp and flew off the end horizontally.
  He found the vertical height through which the ball fell was
  proportional to the square of the horizontal travel.
    - To get the ball to land twice as far away, he had to raise
      the ramp not just two, but four times as high.
    - Galileo had now settled the argument over how to describe
      a projectile path – it follows a parabolic arc. (Graphs in
      the form `y=ax^2` are parabolas)

- This experiment also lead Galileo to the rule that the distance
  travelled (vertical or horizontal?) by a falling-body is
  proportional to the `square of the time`.
    - Here’s how: Guided by Superposition he analysed the
      horizontal and vertical motions separately.
    - The horizontal is simpler because by the Principle of
      Inertia, the ball must continue its horizontal motion at
      the same speed at which it left the incline.
    - With the Sun directly above, the shadow of the ball would
      move at constant speed. In this kind of motion, the
      distance covered is simply proportional to the time. So, he
      reasoned, the horizontal distance was a measure of the time
      of flight!

- Once he saw that, the conclusion was clear.
- For the vertical motion, which was that of a freely falling
  body, the height (distance) was proportional to the square of
  the time of descent.
    - This then was the origin of the falling-body result,
      through an experimental observation. Only later did he test
      the rule on an inclined plane, as a further check on his
      reasoning.
    - Only later still did he realise that this behaviour is
      a consequence of uniform acceleration.

### Chapter 5 Energy – The Romance

- When a car moves at constant speed on a level road, work is
  being done by the engine at a constant rate. Power is the
  `rate` of energy transfer, so:

    > power = work / time
    >       = F.x / time
    >       = F.v

- The power required is force times the velocity. Now at constant
  speed the force impelling a car is exactly equal to the force
  of resistance opposing it.  Making this substitution and
  solving for velocity we find that: Velocity is the ratio of
  power to resistance!

    > v = power / F_{resist}

- So it is power rather than Newtonian force that should be used
  to quantify propulsive effort in _Aristotle’s_ physics. 
    - There was some value after all in his approach to the study
      of motion, but don’t forget this only applies to the case
      of an object moving at _constant speed_ with the propulsive
      force balanced by resistance. It doesn’t have the
      generality of Newton’s laws.



### Chapter 6 Fields – Mechanist’s Missing Part!

- It must be emphasised that fields and action-at-a-distance have
  exactly the same observable consequences as long as the field
  is _constant in time_. 

- Thus while Faraday’s discoveries were admired, his fields was
  not taken very seriously until his work was refined and
  extended by Maxwell.
