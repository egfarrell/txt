
What is "Superposition"?

### Summary
    - A quantum state is a vector of `complex amplitudes` that
      encodes the `possible` outcomes of measurements.
    - A superposition is simply a linear combination of valid
      states. 
        - `(a)ψ_1 + (b)ψ_2` 
    - Physically, it means the system doesn't have definite
      values for the observable being considered.
    - The amplitudes in a superposition can `interfere`,
      producing measurable effects like diffraction patterns.
    - In a position superposition, the wavefunction is spread
      out, assigning a `probability amplitude` to multiple
      locations.
    - This doesn't mean a particle is a tiny object physically
      present in multiple places at once; location isn't definite
      until measured.
    - Different interpretations of QM give different stories, but
      they all agree on these operational facts.

### Basics
    - A quantum state is a complex-valued `function` (for
      position representation) or a `vector` in a Hilbert space
      (the abstract representation).
    - If a state is in a superposition, the physical meaning is
      that the state encodes the `possibility` of different
      measurement outcomes `simultaneously`, and that these
      possibilities can interfere with each other.
    - Formally, if `ψ_1` and `ψ_2` are valid state vectors, then
      any linear combination `(a)ψ_1 + (b)ψ_2` is also a valid
      state.
    - Superposition just means "adding allowed quantum states
      together with complex weights".

    - What does that mean physically? 
        - It means that before measurement, the quantum system
          doesn't have a definite value for the observable you
          are considering. 
        - A superposition is a state where, if you measure some
          property (like position or spin), the result isn't
          predetermined. 
        - The state encodes `amplitudes` for outcomes, which can
          interfere — like water waves overlapping.

### Multiple locations
    - Your 2nd question: does that mean a particle is literally
      in multiple locations? 
    - Here, language matters. If the superposition is of
      different positions, like `ψ = a*|left> + b*|right>`, you
      will indeed later detect the particle either left or right.
    - But before detection, the theory doesn't assign it
      a definite location. Saying "it is in both places" is
      shorthand, but not quite right physically — the particle
      isn't a tiny marble split in two. 
        - Instead, the wavefunction spreads out, giving nonzero
          amplitude in multiple locations. 
        - The superposition reflects a single particle with
          a spatially extended probability amplitude.

    - The strange part: in interference experiments (like the
      double slit), the wavefunction behaves as if the particle
      goes through multiple paths at once. 
    - If you ask "which slit?", the answer depends on the
      experimental setup: 
        - if you measure the path, interference vanishes; if you
          don’t, interference appears. Nature punishes the nosy.

### "normal Understanding"

    - Superposition is linear combination of states: yes.

    - Superposition means multiple potential outcomes coexist and
      can interfere: yes.

    - Saying the particle is “physically in many places” is
      a risky metaphor — a more careful statement is that
      position isn't a possessed property until measured.

    - Superposition reminds us that classical categories
      (location, path) only fully exist after measurement.

    - Continuing down this rabbit hole leads to interpretations:
        - Copenhagen, Many-Worlds, Bohmian mechanics, each giving
      a different story about what the wavefunction “really” is.

    - The fun in physics is that they all give the same
      predictions — but the underlying ontology is still an open
      playground.

### Implications for QG:

- A true philosophical-physics pressure point.

- GR treats spacetime and gravity as a smooth geometric field.
  Mass tells spacetime how to curve, curvature tells mass how to
  move. Elegant. Deterministic. Classical. A mass has a `definite
  position`, so the geometry around it is well-defined.

- QM replies: `position` isn’t even real until you look.

- Here’s the tension in plain terms:
    - In QM, a particle in a superposition of two positions has NO DEFINITE LOCATION.
    - In GR, the gravitational field depends on where mass is located.

- So what does spacetime “curve around” if the particle isn't
  anywhere in particular?
- Does a single particle produce a superposition of two different
  spacetime geometries?
- Do we need a gravitational field that's itself in
  superposition?

- That is the central dispute: 
    - whether gravity must become fully quantum or whether it can
      remain classical while matter is quantum.

- Some researchers argue:
    - Gravity must be quantized, because otherwise quantum
      superpositions of mass distributions would have undefined
      or contradictory gravitational effects.
    - The gravitational field should have its own quantum states
      — superpositions of curvature.

Others explore:
- Semi-classical models where gravity stays classical and
  responds to the expectation value of the quantum stress-energy
  tensor.
- This avoids QG… but it's internal consistency
  issues and likely conflicts with observations at some scale.

- And recently, experiments are appearing to test whether gravity
  can mediate quantum entanglement. If two tiny masses become
  entangled only through their gravitational interaction, that
  would strongly imply that the gravitational field itself can be
  in superposition. Entanglement is very picky: classical forces
  can’t do that trick.

The big picture:
    - Quantum superposition tells us nature doesn’t assign
      definite geometry to spacetime if matter isn’t definite.
    - Geometry either becomes fuzzy or branches or hides behind
      a deeper theory.
    - GR wasn’t built for fuzziness — which is why this frontier
      is so wild.

### Bohmian superposition?
    - Bohmian: particles are always somewhere, full stop. No
      existential crisis about position. But it pays for that
      certainty with a richer invisible machinery.

    - The key: in Bohmian mechanics, the wavefunction never stops
      being the full quantum wave we know from the standard
      theory. sUPERPOSITION LIVES THERE.
    - The particle rides inside that wave like a surfer on an
      ocean — the wave guides the particle’s motion.

- So what does superposition mean?

- The wavefunction can still be `a*psi_left + b*psi_right`.
- This means the pilot wave has two “branches” spreading through
  space, just like in standard QM. The particle itself, however,
  goes down exactly one path. But the _empty_ branch of the wave
  still influences the particle via the guidance equation.

For example, the double slit:
    - The particle has a definite trajectory through only one
      slit.
    - But the wave goes through both slits.
    - The wave’s overlapping branches generate the interference
      pattern.
    - The particle is steered by the resulting pattern into
      bright fringes.

- So Bohm’s answer:
    - Position is always definite, but the wavefunction has
      multiple possibilities encoded; those possibilities shape
      motion even if not realized.

- Reality has two layers:
    1. Particles, with definite positions and continuous
    trajectories.
    2. The pilot wave, evolving according to the full Schrödinger
    equation in configuration space, controlling particle motion.

- Philosophically, Bohmian mechanics preserves the ontology of
  a classical world (particles have locations) while keeping the
  mathematical structure of superposition and interference
  intact.

The trade-offs:
    - The wave lives in a very high-dimensional configuration
      space for many particles, which is hard to take as
      a literal physical field.
    - Nonlocality is built-in: what happens here depends
      instantaneously on the configuration elsewhere.

- But conceptually: Bohmian mechanics simply says superposition
  is a property of the guiding wave, not the particle. The
  particle’s “definiteness” is rescued, but only thanks to
  a shadowy pilot wave that never collapses and always knows the
  whole experiment’s layout.

Quantum weirdness doesn’t vanish — it just moves backstage.
