- 1.0 out of 5 stars
- Lubos Motl - 2016

- "Making Sense of Quantum Mechanics" is another pop-science text
  spreading falsehoods and confusion. Like other books by
  assorted quantum mystics and "philosophers", it eclectically
  and incoherently mixes several "QM interpretations":
    - ie, the misguided prejudices and confused philosophies
      attempting to replace QM since the 1920s, but especially
      since the 60s when "quantum flapdoodle" industry, as
      Gell-Mann called it, began to spread like wildfire.

- Bricmont isn't capable of critically analysing non-Copenhagen
  "interpretations" and makes the usual mistakes anti-quantum
  activists have done over the years.
    - In particular, he asserts that QM or Bell's theorem imply
      the existence of "`non-locality`", regardless of
      completeness of QM.
    - Sidney Coleman, Murray Gell-Mann, and numerous others have
      identified this falsehood as the most important point
      underlying the quantum superstitions.
    - In reality, the Bell-like experiments with entanglement DO
      NOT depend on nonlocality whatsoever.
    - Non-relativistic QM theories allow nonlocal interactions in
      principle but they typically decrease with the distance and
      become negligible at macroscopic separation, so they can't
      be responsible for any major features of the observations.

- In relativistic QM theories, i.e. QFTs and string theory (the
  latter if we assume a Minkowski background), `nonlocality` is
  strictly forbidden.
- In the QFT formalism, locality is guaranteed by the provable
  vanishing of the (graded) commutators of fields at the
  spacelike separation.
- No signals or influences can ever propagate faster than light
  and all correlations between distant, spacelike-separated
  experiments are always fully explained by the mutual
  interactions or co-existence of the objects (subsystems) in the
  past, i.e. by events in the intersection of the past light
  cones of the two measurements.
- Bell's inequalities fail to hold in Nature not because Nature
  is nonlocal but because nature is non-classical (i.e.
  "non-realist"). Nature is described by QM instead.

- QM is the correct theory/framework for describing Nature and as
  the founders in the 20s already realised, it is and must be,
  a set of rules for predicting the probabilities of observations
  by an OBSERVER.
    - These predictions are based on the observer’s own
      subjective knowledge of past observations.
- It can't be made independent of an observer and it can't be
  "objectified". Observations always influence the observed
  system and this influence can't be removed.
- All observations are always associated with `linear operators`
  on a `complex Hilbert space`.
- A priori possible results of a measurement are always given by
  the `spectrum` of the observable
    - (e.g. the eigenvalues of the operator).
- Every measurement always collapses the state vector (wave
  function) to an `eigenstate` of the measured operator
  corresponding to the measured eigenvalue, it always acts as
  a `projection`.
- The squared absolute values of the complex probability
  amplitudes always determine that the measurement produces
  a corresponding eigenvalue (Born's rule).
- The evolution in time (or other transformations) are always
  given by unitary operators.
- The space of allowed states are always elements of a linear
  vector space – complex superpositions of vectors `ψ`,`φ` are
  always as allowed as the vectors `ψ`,`φ` themselves.
- The superposition of two states `ψ`,`φ` always means "one OR
  the other", not "one AND the other", as Bricmont and others
  often incorrectly say.
- Composite systems are always described by Hilbert spaces that
  are tensor products of Hilbert spaces for the parts
  (subsystems).
- Most of the vectors in such Hilbert spaces (of the composite
  system) are always `entangled` i.e. they can't be written as
  tensor products of vectors from the two subsystems' spaces
  (only as a superposition of several terms of this type).

- The previous paragraph describes all the general postulates of
  QM that give us everything we need to know and that all QM
  theories share.
- The value of the above paragraph vastly exceeds the value of
  Bricmont's book because it's not only concise but also correct.

- Up to the freedom in the details of the presentation (one may
  talk about sequences of measurements instead and decoherent or
  consistent histories or one may choose the "Bayesian"
  terminology for the observer's probabilities etc.), it is the
  only correct "interpretation".

- These insights form the _core_ of QM, not something open to
  "reinterpretation".
    - They are the insights that Heisenberg, Born, and partly
      Dirac got their Nobel prizes for, and the reason we
      celebrate them. And also why a whole generation considered
      Bohr their guru, with the justification of QM being the
      greatest revolution in 20th century science.

- An intelligent reader may understand the rules by reading
  a single paragraph several times.
- To write hundreds of confused pages instead (pages that largely
  contradict the experimentally verified content of the previous
  paragraph) is plain wrong.
- The universal postulates of QM were first called "the
  Copenhagen spirit" by Heisenberg around 1930, and then "the
  Copenhagen interpretation" in his book sometime in the 1950s.
- Heisenberg was sorry about this phrase even before the book was
  released. He realized it would be misused by irrational critics
  of QM who would pretend that "other interpretations" were
  suddenly possible as well, something that Heisenberg and the
  other founders (the physics, not just people who contributed
  some mathematical insights, like Schrödinger who remained
  confused about the physical meaning of the mathematical objects
  all throughout his life) loudly realized to be nonsense.
- Heisenberg's worries turned out to be justified.
- Now, in 2016, we're literally drowning in the books and popular
  articles selling diverse "interpretations" – i.e. ways to deny
  the rules of QM (which are basically being dismissed as the
  "Copenhagen interpretation") and Bricmont's book is another one
  that makes the situation worse than before.

- The myth about `nonlocality` of QM – which has produced
  hundreds of stories and popular books promoting telepathy and
  other supernatural phenomena, among other flawed ideas – is one
  of the most prominent anti-quantum-mechanical myths.

- Bricmont praises almost all the wrong ideas about QM and offers
  all the indefensible criticisms of this framework which
  underlies modern physics – and criticisms of its founders,
  sometimes even personal criticisms.
