# Why the quantum state isn't (straightforwardly) probabilistic

In QM, we routinely talk about so-called "superposition states"
- both at the `microscopic` level and `macroscopic` level.
    - Microscopic: 
        - "the state of the ELECTRON is a superposition of
          spin-up and spin-down"
    - Macroscopic:
        - Wthe state of Schrodinger's CAT is a superposition of
          alive and dead"

A large part of the "measurement problem" is making sense of
these _superposition_ states, and there are basically two views.

1. "State as Physical": 
    - the state of a physical system tells us what that system is
      actually, physically, like, and from that point of view,
      Schrodinger's cat is seriously weird.

    - What does it even mean to say that the cat is both alive
      and dead? And, if cats can be alive and dead at the same
      time, how come when we look at them we only see
      definitely-alive cats or definitely-dead cats?

    - We could answer the definitely-alive-dead question by
      invoking a mysterious new dynamical process: 
        - the "collapse of the wave function" where the act of
          looking at the cat magically causes them to jump into
          alive-cat or dead-cat states. 

    - But a physical process depending for its action on
      "observations", "measurements", even "consciousness",
      doesn't seem scientifically reputable.

    - So people who accept the "state-as-physical" view generally
      try to make sense either of QM without collapses (that
      leads to something like Everett's many-worlds theory), or
      to modify QM to replace it with something less problematic.

2. "State as probability":
    - Here, Schrodinger's cat is totally unmysterious. 
    - Saying "the state of the cat is half alive, half dead",
      just means "it has 50% probability of being alive and 50%
      probability of being dead".

    - And the so-called collapse of the wavefunction just
      corresponds to us looking and finding out which it is. 
    - From this view-point, saying the cat is in a superposition
      of alive-and-dead is no more mysterious than saying Sean is
      50% likely to be in his office, and 50% likely to be at
      a conference.

- Now, to be sure, probability is a bit philosophically
  mysterious. What _exactly_ it means to say something is 50%
  likely can be controversial. But we have a number of ways of
  making sense of it, and for all of them, the cat stays
  unmysterious.

- For instance, perhaps we mean that if we run the experiment
  many times, we'll find that half the cats live, and half of
  them die. (Frequentist view)

- Or maybe we mean that we, personally, know that the cat is
  alive or dead but we don't know which, and the 50% is a way of
  quantifying our lack of knowledge. (This is the _Bayesian
  view_.) But on either view, the weirdness of the cat still goes
  away.

- So, it's tempting to say that we should just adopt the
  "state-as-probability" view, and get rid of the quantum
  weirdness.

- But this doesn't work, because just as
    - the "state-as-physical" view struggles to make sense of
      MACROSCOPIC superpositions,
    - the "state-as-probability" view founders on MICROSCOPIC
      superpositions.

- Consider, a simple interference experiment. 
    - We split a laser into two beams,  `Beam 1` and `Beam 2`,
      with a half-silvered mirror.

- We bring the beams back together at another such mirror and
  allow them to interfere. The resultant light ends up being
  split between say:
    - Output Path `A` and Output Path `B` and we see how much
      light ends up at each.

- It's well known that we can tune the two beams to get any
  result we like: 
    - e.g. all the light at `A`, 
    - all of it at `B`, 
    - or anything in between.

- It's also well known that if we block one of the beams, we
  always get the same result: half the light at `A`, half the
  light at `B`.

- And finally, it's well known that these results persist even if
  we turn the laser so low _ONLY ONE PHOTON_ at a time passes
  through .

- According to QM, we should represent the state of each photon,
  as it passes through the system, as a superposition of:
  `(photon in Beam 1) + (Photon in Beam 2) `

    - According to the "state as PHYSICAL" view,
        - the photon is in a strange kind of non-local state.

    - But with the "state as PROBABILITY" view,
        - it seems to be shorthand for "the photon is EITHER in
          beam 1 or beam 2, with equal probability of each.
        - --------------------------
        - And that CAN'T BE CORRECT!
        - --------------------------

- For if the photon is in _beam 1_ (therefore according to QM,
  described by a NON-SUPERPOSITION state, or at least not by
  a superposition of beam states) we know we get result `A` half
  the time, result `B` half the time.

- And if the photon is in _beam 2_, we also know that we get
  result `A` half the time, result `B` half the time.

- So whichever beam it's in, we should get result `A` half the
  time and result `B` half the time.

- And of course, we DON'T. 
    - (Ah because we can "tune" the beam so that interference
      makes it all at `A` or all at `B`, or anything inbetween.)

----------------------------------------------------------------
ASIDE:
    - Both beams open: 
        - Their waves RECOMBINE at the second mirror and
          interfere. Depending on their RELATIVE PHASE, the light
          can all go to A or all go to B.
    - One beam blocked: 
        - Only one beam reaches the second mirror. With no second
          beam to INTERFERE with, the mirror sends 50% towards
          A and 50% towards B.
    - The crucial distinction is that the second mirror doesn't
      always produce a 50/50 output. 
    - That happens when only one input path is illuminated. When
      both paths are illuminated, interference changes the
      distribution between the outputs.
    - And that's precisely the feature Wallace wants to draw
      attention to: opening the second path changes the outcome,
      even though it adds another possible route for the photon.
      ----------------------------------------------------------------

- So, just by elementary reasoning - I haven't even had to talk
  about probabilities - we seem to rule out the
  "state-as-probability" rule.

- Indeed, we seem to be able to see, pretty directly, that
  _something goes down each beam_. 
    - If I insert an appropriate phase factor into ONE of the
      beams (either one), I can change things from "every photon
      ends up at `A`" to "every photon ends up at `B`".

- In other words, things happening to either beam affect physical
  outcomes.

- It's hard to make sense of this unless BOTH beams are being
  probed by physical "stuff" on every run of the experiment. That
  seems pretty definitively to support the idea that the
  SUPERPOSITION is somehow PHYSICAL.

- There's an interesting way of getting around the problem. We
  could just say that my "elementary reasoning" doesn't actually
  apply to QM - it's a holdover of old, bad, classical ways of
  thinking about the world.

- We might say that "either-this-happens-or-that-does" reasoning
  I used above isn't applicable to quantum systems. (Tom Banks,
  in his post, pretty much says this)

- There are various ways of saying what's problematic with this,
  but here's a simple one. To make this kind of claim is to say
  the "probabilities" of QM don't obey all of the rules of
  probability.

- But in that case, what makes us think that they are
  probabilities?
    - They can't be `relative frequencies`, for instance:
        - it can't be that 50% of the photons go down the left
          branch and 50% go down the right branch.
    - Nor can they quantify our `ignorance` of which branch it
      goes down, because we don't need to know which branch it
      goes down to know what it'll do next.

- So to call the numbers in the superposition "probabilities" is
  question-begging. Better to give them their own name, and
  fortunately, QM has already given us one: "`AMPLITUDES`".
    - But once we make this move, we've lost everything
      distinctive about the "state-as-probability" view.

- Everyone agrees that according to QM, the photon has some
  amplitude of being in beam `A` and some amplitude of being in
  beam `B` and indeed, that the cat has some amplitude of being
  alive and some amplitude of being dead.

- The question is, _what does that mean?_

- The "state-as-probability" view was supposed to answer, simply:
  it means that we don't know everything about the photon's (or
  the cat's) state;
    - but that now seems to have been lost.
    - And the earlier argument that something goes down both
      beams remains unscathed.

- Now, I've considered only the most straightforward kind of
  state-as-probability view you can think of: a view which
  I think is pretty decisively refuted by the facts.

- It's possible to imagine subtler probabilistic theories; maybe
  the quantum state isn't about the probabilities of each term in
  the superposition, but it's still about the probabilities of
  something.

- But people's expectations have generally been that the
  _ubiquity of interference effects_ makes that hard to sustain,
  and a succession of mathematical theorems, from classic results
  like Bell-Kochen-Specker, to cutting-edge ones like PBR, have
  supported that expectation.

- In fact, theres only one current "state-as-probability" theory
  that seems half-way viable:
    - the probabilities aren't the probability of anything
      objective, they're just the probabilities of measurement
      outcomes.

- Quantum theory, in other words, isn't a theory that tells us
  about the world: it's just a tool to predict the results of
  experiment.

- Views like this, which philosophers call _instrumentalist_, are
  often adopted as fall-back positions by physicists defending
  'state-as-probability' takes on QM:
    - Tom Banks, for instance, does exactly this in his final
      paragraph.

- There's nothing particularly quantum-mechanical about
  `instrumentalism`. It has a long and rather sorry philosophical
  history: most contemporary philosophers regarding it as fairly
  conclusively refuted.

- But I think it's easier to see what's wrong with it just by
  noticing that real science isn't like this. According to
  instrumentalism, palaeontologists talk about dinosaurs so they
  can understand fossils, astrophysicists talk about stars so
  they can understand photoplates, virologists talk about viruses
  so they can understand NMR instruments, and particle physicists
  talk about the Higgs Boson so they can understand the LHC.

- In each case, it's quite clear that instrumentalism is the
  wrong way around. Science is not "about" experiments; science
  is about the world, and experiments are part of its toolkit.
