- Unpack this:
    > In the SM the Higgs mass squared receives large radiative
    > corrections (e.g., δM² ∼ (Λ²) when Λ is the cutoff) so to
    > keep the Higgs at the weak scale requires fine-tuning if
    > new physics sits at a high scale.

### Answer
- The Higgs boson has a mass, around 125 GeV.
    - That mass comes from a term in the Standard Model
      Lagrangian that looks like `m_H² |H|²`.

- When you include quantum effects — all the virtual particles
  popping in and out of existence in loops — the Higgs mass
  doesn’t stay fixed. Those loops shift the Higgs mass term. That
  shift is what we call a radiative correction.

- Here’s the problem:
    - For most particles, quantum corrections are modest. But for
      a scalar particle like the Higgs, the correction is
      quadratically divergent — meaning it grows like the square
      of whatever energy scale Λ you think new physics appears at
      (for instance, the Planck scale, 10¹⁹ GeV, or a grand
      unification scale, 10¹⁶ GeV).

- Symbolically:
`δM_H²` ∼ `(coupling constants / 16π²) × Λ²`

- Let’s plug in some rough intuition.

- If Λ were as high as, say, `10¹⁶ GeV`, then the correction term
  `δM_H²` would be enormous — on the order of
    - `(10¹⁶ GeV)² = 10³² GeV²`.
- But the actual observed Higgs mass squared is about
    - `(125 GeV)² ≈ 10⁴ GeV²`.

- To make those numbers compatible, the “bare” Higgs mass (the
  one you’d start with before including loops) must cancel out
  the enormous correction with almost ridiculous precision — to
  something like 1 part in 10²⁸. That’s the fine-tuning problem.

- So when physicists say:
    > “To keep the Higgs at the weak scale requires fine-tuning
    > if new physics sits at a high scale,”
- they mean:
    - Quantum loops naturally drag the Higgs mass up toward the
      highest energy scale in the theory.
    - But since we observe the Higgs mass to be small (~100 GeV),
      something must cancel those huge corrections almost
      perfectly.
    - Unless there’s some new physics (like supersymmetry,
      compositeness, etc.) that cuts off or balances the
      corrections, this looks extremely unnatural — as if the
      universe’s parameters were delicately adjusted just so.

- In short:
    - The Higgs mass is unstable under quantum corrections. If
      the universe has much higher energy physics above the weak
      scale, then keeping the Higgs light looks suspiciously like
      cosmic bookkeeping magic. That’s what’s called the
      hierarchy problem.
