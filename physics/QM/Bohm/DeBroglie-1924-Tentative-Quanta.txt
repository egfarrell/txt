### CLAUDE (binary symmetry)

- This 1924 paper is indeed distinct from his pilot wave work
  — it's more of an exploratory attempt to reconcile the photon
  picture with wave properties.

- On your question about "internal binary symmetry": de Broglie
  is referring to polarization as a two-state (binary) internal
  degree of freedom of the light quantum.

- Here's what he means, unpacked:

The symmetry of EM waves

A classical EM wave is transverse — its electric (and magnetic) field oscillates perpendicular to the direction of propagation. Crucially, there are exactly two independent polarization states (e.g., horizontal/vertical, or left-circular/right-circular). This is the "binary" character — not binary in the digital sense, but in the sense of a two-fold (Z₂) symmetry, a two-dimensional space of possibilities.

Translating this to the quantum

De Broglie is saying: if light quanta are real particles, they must somehow *carry* this polarization property internally. The wave's polarization axis must correspond to some internal orientation or spin-like axis attached to the quantum itself. So the "binary symmetry" is the two-valuedness of the polarization degree of freedom — the fact that any polarization state is a superposition of just two basis states.

Why "internal"?

Because polarization isn't about the *position* or *trajectory* of the quantum — it's a property that lives "inside" it, independent of its spatial motion. This is an early intuition toward what we'd now call an internal degree of freedom, which in modern QFT we understand as the photon having spin-1 with exactly two helicity states (±ℏ) — the left and right circular polarizations. The longitudinal mode is absent for a massless particle.

The subtle connection to his tiny mass idea

Note that this connects to his speculation about a tiny rest mass (that ~10⁻⁵⁰ g). A *massive* spin-1 particle would have three polarization states (the longitudinal one is restored). De Broglie's binary/two-state symmetry is actually more naturally consistent with a *massless* photon — which is a bit ironic given that he's simultaneously entertaining a small rest mass. This tension is physically interesting and wasn't fully resolved until much later.

So in short: "internal binary symmetry" = the two-dimensional polarization degree of freedom of the photon, which de Broglie is recognizing must be an intrinsic property of the quantum itself, not just a feature of the classical wave description.

### GEMINI (binary symmetry)

In this 1924 paper, Louis de Broglie is attempting to bridge the gap between the wave theory of light (Maxwell’s equations) and the corpuscular theory of light (Einstein’s light quanta).

When he speaks of "internal binary symmetry," he is referring to polarization.

Here is a breakdown of what he meant in the context of 1924 physics:

### 1. The Problem of Polarization
In classical electromagnetism, light is a transverse wave. This means the electric and magnetic field vectors oscillate perpendicular to the direction of travel. Crucially, there are two independent directions (degrees of freedom) for this oscillation—for example, horizontal and vertical polarization.

If you propose that light consists of particles (quanta), you have a problem: How does a single "point-like" particle carry the information of a transverse wave's orientation? A simple scalar particle (like a tiny billiard ball) has no "up" or "side" orientation.

### 2. Why "Binary"?
The term "binary" refers to the two-fold nature of polarization. De Broglie recognized that for a theory of light quanta to be valid, each quantum must possess an internal property that corresponds to these two states. 

In his mind, the light quantum wasn't just a point of energy; it had to have an internal "axis" or a "vibration" that could be oriented in two independent ways relative to its direction of motion. This internal state allowed the quanta to reproduce the interference and polarization effects seen in Maxwell's wave theory.

### 3. Precursor to Spin
While de Broglie didn't use the word "spin" (Goudsmit and Uhlenbeck wouldn't propose electron spin until 1925), he was essentially describing the spin of the photon. 

In modern quantum mechanics, we say the photon has a spin of $s=1$. Because the photon is (effectively) massless and moves at $c$, it only has two allowed helicity states (left-handed and right-handed circular polarization). De Broglie’s "internal binary symmetry" was a prophetic mechanical intuition for what we now call the discrete internal degrees of freedom of the photon.

### 4. The "Internal Axis"
Later in that same section of the paper, de Broglie suggests that because of this symmetry, the light quantum should be treated as a "vector" rather than a "scalar." 

He was trying to visualize the photon as a tiny observer carrying a "clock" (his famous idea of "phase harmony"). For the light quantum to account for polarization, that "clock" or internal vibration had to be a vector vibration occurring in the plane perpendicular to the velocity.

### Why he mentions tiny mass (10^{-50} g)
This is a crucial link to your quote. De Broglie believed that if the light quantum had a non-zero rest mass ($m_0$), it would travel at a speed slightly less than $c$. This allowed him to use Lorentz transformations to look at the "rest frame" of the light quantum. 

In that rest frame, the "internal binary symmetry" is even easier to visualize: it’s like a little dipole or a spinning top. If the mass were exactly zero, he couldn't mathematically "sit" on the photon to describe its internal symmetry using the standard mechanics of the time.

### Summary
In short, "internal binary symmetry" is de Broglie's 1924 term for the two polarization states of light. He was arguing that the photon is not a simple particle, but a complex entity with an internal orientation that matches the two-fold transverse nature of Maxwell's electromagnetic waves.
