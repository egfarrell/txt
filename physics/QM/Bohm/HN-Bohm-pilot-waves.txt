# Why don't more physicists subscribe to pilot wave theory?

### tzs

- Possible Analogy?
    - Would this kind of physics be equivalent to nonstandard
      analysis in mathematics?

- Nonstandard analysis (NSA) is an approach that essentially
  makes rigorous the vague notions of infinitesimals that Newton,
  Leibniz and other pioneers of calculus based their work on.
    - cf: $MY_NOTES/maths/analysis/infinitesimals.txt

- Newton's fluxions and Leibniz's differentials provided great
  guidance, and in many cases even worked and gave correct
  results, but they were not really rigorously defined.
    - Later mathematicians started running into serious problems
      with this approach. These were resolved with the approach
      we all know and love (loath?): limits and epsilon/deltas.

- NSA extends the real field to contain a new kind of number that
  is positive but is less than `1/N` for any integer `N`, giving
  a new field called the HYPERREALS.
    - You can then do things like define the derivative of `f` at
      `x` to be `(f(x+h) - f(x)) / h` where `h` is an
      infinitesimal, work out that using ordinary algebraic
      manipulations, and then just drop any terms that are
      infinitesimal.
    - NSA goes far beyond just calculus. You can use these ideas
      throughout all of analysis.

- Seems pretty cool, and a lot clearer than a limits approach. So
  why has it not taken over?

- I believe a big part of it is that it's been shown that NSA and
  standard analysis are equivalent. Anything you can prove in one
  can be proven in the other. Perhaps more important, one doesn't
  seem any better than the other at leading to deep insights.
    - If you can't solve a problem in standard analysis, you
      probably aren't going to figure out the solution if you use
      NSA, and vice versa.

- So while it may be nicer overall to use NSA if starting from
  scratch, instead of standard analysis everywhere, so much is
  already done in standard analysis that switching now would be
  too painful.

### naasking

- Pilot waves aren't made of anything, they are a description of
  configuration space. Think of it like this:
    - To describe particle motion, take the ordinary/classical
      equations of motion, and now add another term to account
      for quantum behaviour.
    - The force exerted by this term at time `t` is a function of
      the position of every other particle in the universe at
      time `t`, no matter the distance.

- That's pilot wave theory, where this term is sometimes called
  the "quantum potential" and is governed by Schroedinger's
  equation.
- As you can see, this extra term goes to zero when quantum
  effects become negligible and we recover classical mechanics
  straight away. The difficulty is the obvious nonlocality and
  what that means for SR.

### oceanofsolaris

- It is deterministic, but you still need to keep track of
  a field that covers your whole configuration space (basically
  you need all the information contained in a function R^n->C for
  n degrees of freedom).
    - Keeping track this high-dimensional field is what makes
      a quantum computer hard to simulate in the first place, not
      the non-determinism. So no, it doesn't make anything easier
      to simulate.

- This makes a lot of sense if you think of it that way:
    - Pilot wave theory gives exactly the same results as any
      other decent interpretation of QM.
    - So the work necessary to simulate it will also be exactly
      the same. Simulating QM is not hard because we don't
      understand it. It is hard because it is an inherently hard
      problem (otherwise, how could quantum computers be faster
      than classical computers?).
