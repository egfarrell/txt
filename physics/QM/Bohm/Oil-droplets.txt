- What can bouncing oil droplets tell us about QM?
- http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.12641

- cf: PILOT-WAVE HYDRODYNAMICS
    - JWM Bush: MIT Group
    - $MY_NOTES/physics/QM/Bohm/Pilot-Wave-Hydrodynamics.txt

### 1. Core idea

- Experiments with oil droplets bouncing on a vibrating fluid
  surface show behaviour closely resembling some quantum
  phenomena. These droplets, called “walkers,” behave like
  particles guided by waves they themselves create.

- This resembles “pilot wave theory” proposed by de Broglie,
  where quantum particles are guided by an associated wave. 
    - The article examines whether these experiments genuinely
      simulate QM or simply illustrate how similar behaviour
      could arise in a classical system.

Main conclusion:
    - The oil droplets do NOT simulate QM.
    - Instead, they provide an illustration of how wave-guided
      particle motion could work.
    - They help us understand how quantum-like behaviour is
      possible, but don't _prove_ QM works this way.

### 2. The experiment

- Experimental setup:
    - A shallow bath of oil is vibrated vertically.
    - A small droplet of oil is placed on the surface.
    - Instead of merging, it bounces repeatedly due to vibration.

- Key physical mechanism:
    - Each _bounce_ generates surface waves.

- These waves:
    - Persist for some time (“memory” effect)
    - Combine via superposition
    - Influence the droplet’s next motion

- This creates feedback:
    - droplet → generates wave
        - → wave influences droplet motion
            - → generates new waves

- Result: the droplet moves across the surface (“walks”).

- This creates a COUPLED wave-particle system entirely within
  classical fluid mechanics.

### 3. Why looks like QM

- The walkers reproduce several behaviours analogous to quantum
  phenomena:

- (A) Wave-particle duality
    - Quantum: particle guided by wavefunction
    - Walker: droplet guided by surface wave

- (B) Interference and diffraction
    - (single and double slit analogues)
    - The wave field affects where droplets can go, creating interference-like patterns.

- (C) Quantised orbits
    - Droplets in circular geometries settle into discrete
      allowed orbits, similar to quantised angular momentum.

- (D) Tunnelling-like behaviour
    - Droplets sometimes cross barriers they normally wouldn’t,
      similar to quantum tunnelling.

- (E) Probability distributions
    - If you track droplet positions over time, they form
      patterns similar to quantum probability densities.

- All of this emerges from classical physics alone.

### 4. Connection to pilot wave theory

- Pilot wave theory proposes:
    - A quantum system has two parts:
        - (1) Particle — has definite position
        - (2) Wave — guides the particle’s motion

- Key equation (conceptually):
    - particle velocity ∝ gradient of wave `phase`

- This differs from standard QM, where particles don't have definite trajectories.

- In the droplet system:
    - droplet ↔ particle
    - surface wave ↔ pilot wave

- Surface wave guides droplet motion just like pilot wave guides
  quantum particles.
- This makes the walker system a concrete example of
  pilot-wave-type dynamics.

### 5. But doesn't mean QM is classical

- Most important point:

- The walker system:
    - is classical
    - obeys classical fluid dynamics
    - doesn't reproduce all quantum phenomena

- In particular, it can't reproduce:
    - quantum entanglement
    - Bell inequality violations
    - true quantum `nonlocality`

- These require genuinely quantum physics.
- So the experiments don't show that QM is secretly classical.

### 6. Simulation vs illustration 

- (key conceptual distinction)

The authors distinguish two possibilities:

    - Analogue `simulation`:
        - One system genuinely models another
        - Results give direct evidence about the target system

    - Analogue `illustration`:
        - System demonstrates how similar behaviour could arise
        - Helps understanding, but doesn't confirm theory

- Conclusion:
    - The walker system is an analogue `illustration`, not
      a simulation.
    - It shows that wave-guided particle motion is possible in
      principle, but doesn't prove that real quantum particles
      work this way.

### 7. What we learn from the experiments

- Scientific value of the walker experiments:

- (1) Conceptual insight
    - They provide a concrete example of wave-particle duality in
      a classical system.

- (2) Support for pilot-wave intuition
    - They show that particle motion guided by waves is
      physically plausible.

- (3) Educational and heuristic value
    - They help physicists visualise alternative interpretations
      of QM.

- But importantly:
    - They do NOT confirm pilot wave theory is correct.
    - They do NOT replace QM.

### 8. Big picture

- QM:
    - Particle behaviour described by wavefunction.

- Walker system:
    - Classical droplet guided by surface wave behaves similarly.

- Key lesson:
    - Quantum-like behaviour can emerge from wave-particle
      feedback systems.

- But:
    - This does NOT mean QM is classical fluid dynamics.

- Instead, the experiments help us understand how wave-guided
  particle motion could work physically.


### 9. Simple conceptual analogy

- Standard QM view:
    - Particle is described only by wavefunction (probabilistic)

- Pilot wave view:
    - Particle is real and guided by wave

- Walker experiment:
    - Real particle (droplet) guided by real wave

- Thus the experiment shows a physical example of pilot-wave-type
  motion.


### 10. One-sentence summary

- Bouncing oil droplets show that classical particles guided by
  waves can reproduce many quantum-like behaviours, illustrating
  how pilot-wave interpretations could work physically, but they
  don't prove QM itself is classical.
