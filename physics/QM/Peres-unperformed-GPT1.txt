### What r_jα means, and why Peres modifies it

- In Bell-type experiments, `r_jα` denotes the result (`±1`, say)
  of measuring particle `j` along detector setting `α`. In
  a local theory, this result is assumed to depend only on the
  state of particle `j` and the local setting `α`.

- Peres’s key move: if you refuse to talk about unperformed
  experiments, but still want to reproduce Bell correlations,
  then you must give up the idea that measurement outcomes are
  INDEPENDENT of distant settings. So `r_jα` should really be
  written as `r_jα(βγδ…)`, meaning:

    - the result at `A` with setting `α` may depend on what
      measurement is performed at `B` (setting `β`), and even on
      other measurements `γ, δ,` … performed anywhere else in the
      universe that is correlated or entangled with particle `j`.

- This is explicit non-local contextuality:
    - outcomes are not properties of ISOLATED subsystems but of
      the entire experimental context (global).

### First average

- `(1/N) Σ_j r_jα(βγδ…) = 0`

- This says: if you fix `α` (measurement setting, direction along
  which Alice measures spin) and average the outcomes at `A` over
  many runs `j`, the mean result is zero.

- Physically, this corresponds to the fact that for a singlet
  state, each local measurement by itself looks completely
  random: half the time `+1`, half `−1`.
    - Importantly, this average is zero regardless of what is
      done elsewhere — regardless of `β`, `γ`, `δ`, etc.

- So even though INDIVIDUAL outcomes may depend non-locally on
  distant settings, the statistics at A alone do not reveal this
  dependence.

- This is why QM respects no-signalling: you cannot use these
  correlations to send messages.

### Second Average

- `(1/N) Σ_j r_jα(βγδ…) r_jβ(αγδ…) = − α · β`

- This is the Bell correlation itself.

- When you multiply the outcomes from `A` and `B` and average
  over runs, you recover the familiar quantum prediction:
    - the correlation is:
        - minus the dot product of measurement directions.

- Crucially, this correlation is also independent of `γ`, `δ`,
  etc. No matter what other measurements are performed elsewhere,
  the `A–B` correlation depends only on `α` and `β`.

### What is surprising?

- Not the formulas themselves — they are just the empirical
  quantum predictions.

- What's surprising is this COMBINATION of facts:
    - Individual outcomes are not properties of isolated systems;
      they depend on the GLOBAL experimental context.
    - Yet all observable averages behave AS IF systems were
      isolated.
    - Non-locality is present at the level of individual events,
      but completely hidden at the level of marginal statistics.

- Physicists are trained to think that if a system is influenced
  by distant events, that influence should show up statistically.

- As Peres emphasizes, Bell’s theorem shows that this intuition
  is wrong. Nature allows non-local dependence in SINGLE events
  while PERFECTLY CANCELING it in averages.

- That's why Peres says that separation is impossible for
  individual experiments, but still "holds" for averages.

### Why “beggars the imagination”?

- Our classical picture ties together three ideas:
    - isolation of systems
    - local causation
    - statistical independence

- Bell forces us to give up at least one of these.
    - Peres’s option is to give up ISOLATION at the level of
      INDIVIDUAL events, while keeping the appearance of
      isolation in observable statistics.

- That is deeply counterintuitive. The world behaves as if it
  were local and separable — but only when you look at AVERAGES.
  Under the hood, it is not.

- That tension, not the equations themselves, is what Peres finds astonishing.

### Notation

- Peres’s notation `r_jα(βγδ…)` means:

    - If, in this same run `j`, the distant setting `β` had been
      different, then the outcome at `A` might have been different.

    - That is a statement about how the outcome is generated, not
      about what you can read off from one datum.

- Why Bell forces dependence at the level of individual runs

- Bell’s theorem shows:
    - If outcomes at `A` are independent of distant settings `β`,

- Peres’s move is to let it fail here:
    - `r_jα` depends on `β`
    - `r_jβ` depends on `α`

- This dependence must exist run by run, because averages are
  built out of runs.

- But — and this is essential — the dependence is finely tuned so
  that it disappears when you AVERAGE locally.

- The quantity `−α·β` is not mysterious within QM. Once you

### Why clashes with classical worldview

- In a classical worldview, correlations arise from either:
    - 1. common causes in the past, or
    - 2. direct influences.

- Local hidden-variable models rely on the first: shared `λ`
  values carried from the source.

- But Bell proves that no such local common-cause story can

- That forces a choice:
    - abandon locality, or
    - abandon realism / outcome definiteness / statistical independence / etc.

- Peres’s option is to abandon locality at the level of
