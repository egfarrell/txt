- Paul A. M. Dirac

Key: there's some kind of `privileged time` in the Hamiltonian
formalism?

- I feel there will always be something missing which we can only
  get by working from a _Hamiltonian_, or maybe from some
  generalization of the concept of a Hamiltonian.

- That's to say, I assume there is an `action integral` which
  depends on the motion, such that, when one varies the motion,
  and puts down the conditions for the action integral to be
  stationary, one gets the equations of motion.

- You might wonder whether one could not take the Hamiltonian as
  the starting point and short-circuit this work of beginning
  with an action integral, getting a `Lagrangian` from it and
  passing from the Lagrangian to the `Hamiltonian`.
    - The objection to trying to make this short-circuit is that
      it's not easy to formulate the conditions for a theory to
      be relativistic in terms of the Hamiltonian.
    - In terms of the action integral, it's very easy to
      formulate the conditions for the theory to be relativistic:
        - simply require the _action integral_ be _invariant_.
    - One can easily construct examples of invariant action
      integrals. They'll automatically lead to equations of
      motion agreeing with relativity, and any developments from
      this action integral will therefore also be in agreement
      with relativity.

- ... to the non-linear electrodynamics which I mentioned
  previously, I don’t think one can short-circuit the route of
  starting with an action integral, getting a Lagrangian, passing
  from the Langrangian to the Hamiltonian, and then passing from
  the Hamiltonian to the quantum theory.

- You may be a little disturbed at this stage by the importance
  that the `time` variable plays in the formalism.
    - We have a time variable `t` occurring already as soon as we
      introduce the Lagrangian.
    - It occurs again in the velocities, and all the work of
      passing from Lagrangian to Hamiltonian involves one
      particular time variable.
    - From the relativistic point of view we are thus singling
      out one particular observer and making our whole formalism
      refer to _the time for this observer_.

- That, of course, is not really very pleasant to a relativist,
  who would like to treat all observers on the same footing.
    - However, it is a feature of the present formalism which
      I do not see how to avoid if one wants to keep to the
      generality of allowing the Lagrangian to be any function of
      the coordinates and velocities.

- We can be sure that the theory is relativistic, even though the
  form of the equations is not manifestly relativistic on account
  of the appearance of one _particular time_ in a dominant place
  in the theory.

- They are connected by the relations (1-4), which are called the
  primary constraints of the Hamiltonian formalism. This
  terminology is due to Bergmann, and I think it is a good one.

- It's convenient to introduce a certain formalism which enables
  one to write these equations briefly, namely the Poisson
  bracket formalism.

- We have the constraints (1-4), but must not use one of these
  constraints before working out a Poisson bracket.
    - If we did, we'd get a wrong result.
    - So we take it as a rule that Poisson brackets must all be
      worked out before we make use of the constraint equations.
