    2. Conventional Realism possible?:

    3. Open Realism:
        - Despite the challenges to conventional realism, the

    4. Kant's Influence:
        - The author introduces Kant’s philosophy, particularly

                - Scientific principles are chosen from

    6. Limits of Human Knowledge:

        - dEspagnat suggests this divide is unresolved but might

### QUANTUM PHYSIC’S ‘WEAK’ OBJECTIVITY
    - This section delves into the concept of "weak objectivity"
      in QM, contrasting it with the strong objectivity of
      classical physics.

    1. Classical Realism and Intuitive Concepts:
        - In classical physics, a realist perspective was
          reasonable because theories used intuitive concepts
          (like space, motion, force) that corresponded to an
          assumed objective reality. This extended to more
          abstract concepts in relativity, such as curved space.

    2. Challenges from QM:
        - QM complicates this realist perspective. While some
          quantum problems (like calculating eigenvalues) may
          resemble classical ones, key differences emerge in
          measurement outcomes.
        - The Born rule only provides `probabilities` of
          measurement results, which implies a reliance on an
          observer or measurement device.
        - This dependency on measurement for meaning diverges
          from conventional realism, where statements should
          refer to an `observer-independent` reality.

    3. Weak Objectivity:
        - The probabilistic statements in QM, though objective
          and universally valid, depend on the _context_ of
          observation and are thus “weakly objective.”
        - Unlike classical statements about a mind-independent
          reality, they involve the observer, which challenges
          the realist interpretation.

    4. Hidden Variables and Nonlocality:
        - Attempts to reintroduce realism through hidden
          variables (eg Bohmian mechanics) have faced challenges,
          especially in compatibility with relativity.
        - Bell's theorem demonstrated that assuming both
          `realism` and `locality` leads to measurable
          inequalities that are experimentally violated.
            - (Tim says there's only one assumption: locality)
            - This violation, confirmed in multiple particle
              systems, suggests that any viable realism must
              accept `nonlocality`, which conflicts with
              commonsense notions of `separability`.

    5. Structural Realism and Empirical Reality:
        - The limitations of knowledge in QM echo `structural
          realism`, which asserts we only know relationships
          (equations) between objects, not the intrinsic nature
          of objects themselves.
        - Thus, entities like electrons or atoms are not elements
          of mind-independent reality but part of empirical
          (observable) reality.

    6. Decoherence and Macroscopic Reality:
        - Decoherence explains why the world appears classical to
          us, despite quantum underpinnings. This challenges the
          notion that macroscopic objects have an independent
          absolute existence.

    7. Philosophical Implications – No “God’s Eye View”:
        - Being part of the universe means our perspective is
          inherently subjective. This idea resonates with Kantian
          perspectives, suggesting that objective knowledge is
          limited to phenomena (observable reality) and supports
          a `mentalist` view of scientific knowledge.

    8. QM as Operationalist:
        - The author concludes that QM should be viewed as an
          operationalist theory: it predicts outcomes rather than
          describing an independent reality.
        - Attempts to interpret it as a descriptive law lead to
          inconsistencies. Its utility is in predicting
          measurements without necessarily depicting an
          underlying reality.

    - This section argues that the advent of QM forces a shift
      away from strong objectivity, aligning more with a Kantian,
      `mentalist view` of knowledge. 
        - QM provides predictive rules rather than a complete
          description of reality, challenging the idea that we
          can know a mind-independent reality in a classical
          realist sense.

    - This section argues ** FOR ** the retention of the concept
      of a mind-independent reality (or thing-in-itself), even

    - While science itself doesn't demand this, the author
      presents 3 philosophical reasons supporting the continued
      relevance of mind-independent reality:

    1. Reason 1: Experimental Resistance

          withstand empirical testing. The resistance of science
          to purely theoretical constructs suggests the existence
          of something beyond human thought, hinting at a reality
          that is not mind-dependent.
        - This reason is likely the most persuasive for
          scientists due to their reliance on experimentation.

    2. Reason 2: Existential Insight
        - The "Cogito" argument ("I think, therefore I am") hints

        - This existence _in se_ (in itself) is deeper and more
          fundamental than perception.
        - The idea that such a mode of existence is confined
          solely to human consciousness seems implausible and
          presumptuous, implying instead that this form of
          existence could extend beyond the mind, to reality
          itself.

    3. Reason 3: Structural Realism and the Unknowable
        - Structural realism, as well as the "no God’s eye view"
          argument, acknowledges a reality independent of our
          knowledge, even if its nature remains forever

        - This implies that mind-independent reality exists, even

    4. Reason 4: Material Basis of Consciousness

        - While the author personally disagrees with this, he

    - Despite the challenges, particularly as summarized by H.

### TWO NOTIONS OF REALITY, OPEN REALISM
    - Here the author introduces the concept of "open realism" as
      an alternative to "conventional realism" in response to
      challenges posed by QM and philosophical considerations.
      Summary:

    1. Conventional Realism vs. Open Realism:
        - Conventional realism assumes the existence of
          a mind-independent reality that is _knowable_, at least
          in principle.
        - However, 'Open realism' suggests that
          a mind-independent reality exists but may be
          _fundamentally unknowable_.
        - This distinction allows for a version of realism that
          doesn’t assume human beings can fully comprehend this
          reality.

    2. Defining Mind-Independent Reality:
        - 'Open realism' contends that mind-independent reality
          has meaning without requiring reference to human
          perception or thought.
        - This approach avoids the `idealist` stance that only
          things perceivable by humans are meaningful, suggesting
          instead that mind-independent reality exists with
          inherent meaning, independent of human understanding.

    3. Empirical vs. Ultimate Reality:
        - 'Open realism' divides reality into two levels:
            - `(1) mind-independent reality`, which exists
              independently and may be unknowable, and
            - `(2) empirical reality`, the part of reality that
              is observable and describable through science and
              everyday experience. 
            - Empirical reality is what is generated in the mind
              and is therefore distinct from the ultimate,
              mind-independent reality.

    4. Knowledge Derived from Reality:
        - Though mind-independent reality may not be fully
          accessible, it may causally influence our knowledge.
        - E.g., Physics provides accurate predictions, suggesting
          there is some connection between mind-independent
          reality and human experience, even if this connection
          is indirect.

    5. Implications for Matter and Consciousness:
        - 'Open realism' shifts the definitions of matter and
          consciousness. Rather than seeing `matter` as the
          fundamental building block of reality, 'open realism'
          views matter as part of empirical reality; essentially
          phenomena or appearances in consciousness.
        - Consequently, the idea that consciousness arises from
          matter becomes problematic within this framework;
          instead, the author proposes that consciousness might
          emerge _directly_ from mind-independent reality.

    6. Intellectual and Emotional Insights into Reality:
        - Although empirical science cannot fully capture
          mind-independent reality, some human experiences like
          emotions from art or nature might provide “windows”
          into this deeper reality.
        - This suggests that aesthetic or emotional experiences
          could offer limited insight into the underlying,
          unknowable nature of reality.

    - In essence, the author presents `open realism`, which
      acknowledges an inaccessible mind-independent reality that
      shapes empirical knowledge without being directly knowable.

    - This perspective also challenges the conventional view that
      consciousness emerges from matter, proposing instead that
      it might emerge from a deeper, mind-independent reality
      beyond our intellectual reach.

### Addendum: Why is it 'open' realism?

- Openness to the Existence of Reality Beyond Human Knowledge

    - The key to understanding "_open_" realism lies in its
      openness to the possibility of a reality that exists beyond
      human perception and cognition. 
    - Unlike conventional realism, which assumes that everything
      important about reality is potentially knowable, open
      realism keeps an open stance toward a reality that:
        - Exists independently of us,
        - Has real causal effects (ie, influences our
          observations), and
        - May forever lie beyond our descriptive or conceptual
          reach.

    - Openness to Causal Interaction with Reality
        - While open realism accepts that we can't fully know
          mind-independent reality, it doesn't entirely "close
          off" this reality from us. The mind-independent world
          may still interact with us through
          causation—contributing to the patterns we observe and
          the predictive success of our theories. This is the
          "open" part: open realism is open to accepting a form
          of reality that affects us indirectly but that we can’t
          wholly explain or access.

    - Distinction from Conventional Realism
        - Open realism is, in essence, a loosening of
          conventional realism’s grip on the _knowability_ of
          objective reality. It’s “open” in the sense that it:
            - Welcomes mystery: 
                - It accepts that parts of reality might be
                  unknowable.
            - Acknowledges limitations: 
                - It doesn’t insist that science or human thought
                  can eventually map the totality of existence.
            - Accepts indirect knowledge: 
                - It allows for reality to be knowable only in
                  part—meaning, we may know only how it
                  influences us or creates appearances in our
                  minds, rather than understanding it in full.


- By using the term "open realism," the author highlights
  a philosophical openness toward a reality that exists
  independently, but we must accept may remain fundamentally
  elusive to direct knowledge.

- In short, open realism embraces an open-minded perspective
  toward the boundaries of knowledge itself. 
    - It proposes a reality that exists beyond human
      understanding but whose traces or effects we might still
      infer indirectly, accepting both reality's existence and
      our limited ability to fully comprehend it.
