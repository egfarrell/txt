## Did Einstein Reject the Idea of Entanglement?

- You might have heard that what Einstein referred to as “spooky
  action at a distance” is technically known as entanglement, and
  he insisted that entanglement couldn’t exist. Neither is
  true.

- The “spooky action” quote is a direct translation of the German
  phrase *spukhafte Fernwirkung*, which Einstein wrote in a 1947
  letter to Born. He was referring to an idea that had long
  intrigued him: how to interpret the `MEASUREMENT` process in
  QM, which he had earlier described as relying on a “peculiar
  mechanism of `action at a distance`” 
    - cf
    - G. Bacciagaluppi and A. Valentini
    - $MY_BOOKS/Physics/QM/Reconsidering-solvay.pdf

- Mathematically, the `measurement` process in QM is
  instantaneous. Say you want to measure the position of
  a particle. Before you do so, the equations allow the particle
  to be in several places at the same time. Observe or measure
  it, however, and suddenly it is in only one place.

- This issue of reality apparently suddenly materializing out of
  uncertainty when you observe it is known as the measurement
  problem. 
    - The update happens faster than light, seemingly violating
      Einstein’s SR, which says that no signal can exceed light
      speed. Of course, Einstein didn’t like it. 
    - That is why, together with physicists Podolsky and Rosen,
      Einstein argued in 1935 that QM must be an “incomplete”
      theory ("EPR" paper), in which measurement is just
      a probabilistic description of an underlying physical
      reality.

- That same year, Schrödinger coined the term *entanglement* to
  describe a `correlation` between two or more objects about
  which one has incomplete knowledge. 
    - For example, you could have two particles, one on the left
      and one on the right, that can each have a state (usually
      physicists consider the property of “spin”, but it could be
      something else, such as momentum) of either `+1` or `−1`,
      and both values must add up to 0. 
- So, either the left particle has spin `−1` and the one on the
  right spin `+1`, or the other way around.

- In an experiment, you can flip the spin of ONE PARTICLE, say
  the LEFT one, even without knowing what it is. 
- If it was `−1`, it is now `+1`; 
- if it was `+1`, it is now `−1`.
    - If you do that, what happens to the particle on the right
      side? 
    - Answer: NOTHING. 
        - (me: ????!!!! I thought this was the whole point of
          Bell's theorem etc. That some sort of 'influence'
          travels between the particles???? 
            - Otherwise Bell's inequality would not be breached
              by QM???)
    - The other particle itself has NOT CHANGED, and the two
      particles are still entangled — just the correlation
      between them has changed.
    - You have changed an entangled system into a different, also
      entangled system.
    - There is no “spooky action” in entanglement, no exchange of
      information that is faster than the speed of light.
        - (Me: ??! Then what's the whole point of Bell's theorem
          then??)

- I think the reason why even some physicists get this mixed up
  is that in their 1935 paper, EPR used what we now call
  ‘entangled particles’ to illustrate the problem with the
  instantaneous update of a system on measurement. 
- The two concepts – `measurement` and `entanglement` – became
  entangled, so to speak.

- Einstein never claimed that entanglement, or QM itself, is
  wrong. What he did was question the physical interpretation of
  the `MEASUREMENT`:
    - that a quantum system seems to exist in several possible
      superposed states but updates to a different state as soon
      as you OBSERVE it. That is an issue that still hasn’t been
      resolved.

- Sabine Hossenfelder is a physicist and member of the Munich
  Center for Mathematical Philosophy in Munich, Germany.
