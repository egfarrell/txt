- First, a bit of orientation.

- The paper is essentially a sharpened restatement of Bell’s
  theorem, but with a specific philosophical `TARGET`:
    - he's targetting the idea that unperformed measurements HAVE
      definite results.
    - Peres’ slogan “unperformed experiments have no results” is
      not a throwaway line; it is the logical ESCAPE HATCH from
      Bell’s contradiction.

- What the symbols mean (informally)
    - You have pairs of particles labeled by `j` = 1,…,N.
    - `r_jα` is the result (`±1`) of measuring particle `A` along
      direction `α`.
    - `r_jβ` is the result (`±1`) of measuring particle `B` along
      direction `β`.

- In a classical hidden-variable picture, you imagine that for
  each pair `j`, the particle already “KNOWS” what result it
  would give for every possible measurement direction.
    - Bell’s theorem shows you cannot assign all those
      hypothetical results consistently while reproducing quantum
      correlations.

- Peres then says: if you insist on imagining results of
  unperformed experiments, you must let them depend on what is
  done elsewhere. That is why he replaces `r_jα` by `r_jα(βγδ…)`.
- The result at `A` is allowed to depend on what measurement is
  chosen at `B`, and even on other correlated systems in the
  universe.
    - That is already a dramatic conceptual shift: no ISOLATED
      subsystems at the level of individual events.
- Now to the two equations at the end.

- First equation:
    - `(1/N) Σ_j r_jα(βγδ…) = 0`
    - This says: if you look only at one wing (say `A`), the
      outcomes are completely RANDOM on average. No matter what
      measurements are done elsewhere `(β, γ, δ, …)`, the average
      result is zero.
    - This is exactly what QM predicts for a singlet state:
        - each local measurement alone is random.
        - This guarantees no superluminal signaling. You cannot
          detect what `B` is doing by looking only at `A`.

- Second equation:
    - `(1/N) Σ_j r_jα(βγδ…) r_jβ(αγδ…) = − α · β`
    - This is the quantum correlation. The average product of the
      outcomes depends only on the relative angle between `α` and
      `β`, and equals `−cosθ = − α · β`.
    - This correlation violates Bell inequalities.
    - Now here is the key point, and this is where the “subtlety”
      comes in.

- Why this is shocking
    - Individually:
        - Each local result looks completely random.
        - Each local average is zero.
        - No dependence on distant choices is visible locally.
    - Jointly:
        - When you compare the results from `A` and `B`, they
          show a highly structured, ANGLE-DEPENDENT CORRELATION.
        - This structure is too strong to be explained by any
          assignment of PRE-EXISTING local values.
        - Yet it does not allow signaling and does not show up in
          any single-detector statistics.

- So yes, your intuition is on the right track, but the surprise
  is sharper than just “random locally, correlated jointly”.

- The real tension is this:
    - 1. If outcomes were predetermined and local, Bell
      inequalities must hold.
    - 2. If outcomes are not predetermined (unperformed
      experiments have no results), Bell’s contradiction
      disappears.
    - 3. If you insist that outcomes exist for unperformed
      measurements, you must allow them to depend on distant
      experimental choices.
    - 4. Even then, nature conspires so that this dependence
      cancels out in all local averages, while surviving
      perfectly in correlations.

- That last fact is what Peres finds almost unbelievable.

- Nature somehow manages to:
    - Be nonlocal at the level of individual events,
    - Hide this nonlocality completely from local statistics,
    - Reveal it only when results are compared afterward.

- Classically, correlations come from SHARED PROPERTIES carried
  by the particles. Quantum mechanically, the correlations exist
  without any underlying list of values, and without any locally
  observable trace.

- This is why he says physicists are used to isolated systems,
  “although it still holds for averages”. The separation
  principle survives statistically, but fails microscopically.

- Why Bell’s theorem is doing the real work here
    - Bell’s theorem tells you that you cannot have all three of
      the following at once:
        - Definite results for all possible measurements,
        - Locality (no dependence on distant choices),
        - Agreement with quantum correlations.
        - Peres’ ending says: pick your poison.

- Either:
    1. Don’t talk about results of unperformed experiments at all
      (his preferred view), or
    2. Accept a deeply nonclassical, holistic universe where even
      hypothetical results depend on everything else.

- The equations show that even if you choose the second route,
  nature still looks locally innocent while being globally
  impossible to imitate classically.
- That combination — strong nonclassical order emerging from
  local randomness without contradiction or signaling — is what
  “beggars the human imagination”.

### 1. Why “if outcomes are not predetermined, Bell’s contradiction disappears”

- What Bell’s contradiction actually assumes
- Bell’s theorem does not start from “quantum mechanics”. It
  starts from a very specific classical-looking assumption:
    - For each run `j`, and for each possible setting `α, β, γ,
      …,` there exists a definite value `r_jα`, `r_jβ`, `r_jγ`,
      … whether or not those measurements are performed.
- This is exactly what Peres calls “speculating about unperformed
  experiments”.

- Once you assume that, you can derive Bell inequalities using
  simple algebra on numbers `±1`. No quantum mechanics is needed
  at that stage.

- Where the contradiction comes from
    - Quantum mechanics predicts correlations like
    - `(1/N) Σ_j r_jα r_jβ = − α · β`
    - which violate Bell inequalities.

- So the contradiction is:
    - Bell inequalities must hold if all `r_jα`, `r_jβ`, … exist
      simultaneously.
    - Quantum correlations violate them.
    - That contradiction only exists because the `r`’s for
      unperformed measurements were assumed to exist.

- What happens if unperformed experiments have no results
    - If you drop the assumption that `r_jα` exists when `α` is
      not actually measured, then you cannot even write down the
      Bell inequalities.
    - Why? 
        - Because the inequalities require you to compare, in the
          SAME RUN `j`, values like `r_jα` and `r_jα′`, even
          though only one of `α` or `α′` can be measured.

- Peres’ key move is:
    - No value → no algebra → no inequality → no contradiction.

- How this relates to the two summation formulas
    - The summation formulas are written only for actually performed measurements:
    - 1. `(1/N) Σ_j r_jα(βγδ…) = 0`
    - 2. `(1/N) Σ_j r_jα(βγδ…) r_jβ(αγδ…) = − α · β`

- These formulas never refer to `r_jα` and `r_jα′`
  simultaneously. Each `r` appears only in a context where that
  measurement is actually made.

- So the formulas are consistent precisely because Peres has
  refused to assign context-independent values to unperformed
  measurements.

- In short:
    - Bell’s contradiction requires hypothetical, context-free
      `r_jα`.
    - Peres’ equations explicitly allow context dependence and
      avoid assigning values where no experiment is done.
    - That is why the contradiction disappears.

### 2. “The separation principle survives statistically, but fails microscopically”
- What “separation principle” means here
    - Peres is not talking about “no correlations at all”.
- He means the operational principle:
    - What happens at `A` cannot be influenced by what is done at
      `B`, in any observable way.
- In modern language: no signaling.
- Now let’s map this onto the two equations.

- Equation 1: statistical separation survives
    - `(1/N) Σ_j r_jα(βγδ…) = 0`
    - This says:
    - No matter what is done elsewhere `(β, γ, δ, …)`, the
      statistics at `A` alone are unchanged.

- This is exactly the separation principle at the level of
  observable statistics. You cannot infer anything about `B` by
  looking at `A`’s data alone.
- So separation survives statistically.

- Equation 2: why this does not contradict that
    - `(1/N) Σ_j r_jα(βγδ…) r_jβ(αγδ…) = − α · β`
    - This equation involves joint statistics — correlations after
  results from `A` and `B` are brought together and compared.

- The separation principle never forbids correlations. Classical
  systems can also be correlated.

- What Bell’s theorem shows is that quantum correlations are “too
  strong” to be explained by LOCAL PRE-EXISTING properties.

- So equation 2 violates local realism, not statistical
  separation in the no-signaling sense.

- Why Peres says separation fails microscopically
    - Microscopically means: at the level of individual runs j.
- Peres is saying:
    - If you insist on imagining that each individual result has
      a definite value even when unmeasured, then those values
      must depend on distant choices.

- That is why he writes `r_jα(βγδ…)`.
- So at the microscopic (single-event, counterfactual) level:
    - The outcome at `A` cannot be thought of as independent of
      what happens at `B`.

- But at the macroscopic (statistical, AVERAGED) level:
    - All such dependencies cancel out in equation 1.
    - No observable influence remains.
- That is the sense in which separation “fails microscopically
  but survives statistically”.

- Putting it all together in one sentence

- Equation 1 says:
    - no signaling, statistical independence of marginals.
- Equation 2 says:
    - NONCLASSICAL correlations in joint statistics.

- Bell’s theorem says: you cannot explain both with local,
  predetermined values.

- Peres’ conclusion says: therefore, unperformed experiments have
  no results — and nature only looks separable when you average.
