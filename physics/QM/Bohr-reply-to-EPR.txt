### Compressed logical outline

- QM is not incomplete.
    - The theory doesn't arbitrarily omit elements of physical
      reality. What looks like incompleteness arises from
      confusing different experimental contexts.

- Different experimental arrangements define different physical
  quantities.
    - Quantum phenomena require a rational discrimination between
      mutually exclusive experimental procedures.
    - Each arrangement permits only certain classical concepts to
      be applied unambiguously.

- The limitation isn't ignorance but principled indefinability.
    - In a given experimental setup, some quantities aren't
      merely unknown; they can't be defined at all without
      changing the experimental conditions.

- Complementarity, not hidden variables.
    - The issue is the use of complementary classical concepts
      (e.g. position vs momentum), each tied to a different
      experimental arrangement. These concepts can't be jointly
      applied.

- EPR’s “no disturbance” criterion is ambiguous.
    - Although there is no mechanical disturbance of the distant
      system, the choice of `measurement` affects the conditions
      that define what predictions are meaningful.

- Conditions of prediction are part of physical reality.
    - Since the experimental conditions determine what can be
      predicted, they are an essential part of the phenomenon and
      of what can be called physical reality.

- Therefore EPR’s conclusion does not follow.
    - Because their argument ignores the _CONTEXTUAL NATURE_ of
      prediction, it does not establish that QM is incomplete.

- Complementarity enables new physical laws.
    - Mutual exclusion of experimental procedures makes possible
      laws that would otherwise seem contradictory, without
      violating scientific principles.

- Classical description is unavoidable.
    - Any measurement outcome must be described using classical
      concepts, which requires a division between measuring
      apparatus and object.

- The cut is conventional but essential.
    - The placement of the quantum–classical distinction is
      a matter of convenience, but its existence is fundamental.

- Measurement interactions resist deeper causal analysis.
    - The interaction between system and apparatus has an
      irreducible individuality not analyzable in classical
      `causal` terms.

- Consequence: revision of classical realism.
    - Quantum theory requires abandoning strict classical
      `causality` and revising what is meant by physical reality.

### Modern language version

- Bohr’s reply to EPR isn't about technical details of
  measurement. Instead it's about what it means to describe
  a physical system at all.

- He argues that QM isn't missing information. The problem is
  different experimental setups define different physical
  quantities, and these setups can't all be realized at the same
  time.
    - You can't talk meaningfully about all quantities at once,
      because they aren't simultaneously well-defined.

- This isn't because we're ignorant of some hidden values.
  Rather, the values themselves don't exist independently of the
  experimental context. In a given setup, some quantities simply
  have no unambiguous meaning.

- EPR claim that if we can predict a quantity without disturbing
  the system, then that quantity corresponds to an element of
  reality.
    - Bohr responds that this notion of “without disturbing” is
      ambiguous. Even if no physical signal or force acts on the
      distant system, the choice of measurement changes what
      kinds of predictions are possible. That change matters.

- For Bohr, the conditions that make predictions possible are
  part of the physical phenomenon itself. Physical reality isn't
  just “what the system has”, but includes the experimental
  _context_ that gives meaning to statements about the system.

- Because EPR ignore this _contextual_ dependence, their argument
  for incompleteness fails.

- More generally, Bohr introduces complementarity: mutually
  exclusive experimental arrangements are required to reveal
  different aspects of quantum systems. The apparent
  contradictions only arise if one assumes that all classical
  concepts should apply simultaneously.

- Finally, Bohr insists that measurements must be described
  classically. This requires a division between the system
  studied and the measuring apparatus. While the placement of
  this division is flexible, the need for it is fundamental. The
  measurement interaction can't be fully analyzed in classical
  causal terms.

- The upshot is that quantum theory forces us to abandon the
  classical ideal of a fully causal, _observer-independent_
  description of reality, and replace it with a _contextual_
  notion of physical description tied to experimental conditions.


### Original language

- “My main purpose in wanting to repeat these simple, and in
  substance well-known, considerations, is to emphasize that in
  the phenomena concerned we're not dealing with an incomplete
  description.”

- “we're not dealing with an incomplete description characterized
  by the arbitrary picking out of different elements of physical
  reality at the cost of sacrificing other such elements—but with
  a rational discrimination between essentially different
  experimental arrangements and procedures.”

- “Any comparison between QM and ordinary statistical
  mechanics—however useful it may be for the formal presentation
  of the theory—is essentially irrelevant.”

- “In each experimental arrangement suited for the study of
  proper quantum phenomena, we have not merely to do with an
  ignorance of the value of certain physical quantities, but with
  the impossibility of defining these quantities in an
  unambiguous way.”

- “As in the simple case, in the special problem treated by
  Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen, we're just concerned with
  a discrimination between different experimental procedures
  which allow of the use of complementary classical concepts.”

- “We now see that the wording of the above-mentioned criterion
  of physical reality proposed by Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen
  contains an ambiguity as regards the meaning of the expression
  ‘without in any way disturbing a system.’”

- “Of course there is in a case like that just considered no
  question of a mechanical disturbance of the system under
  investigation during the last critical stage of the measuring
  procedure.”

- “But even at this stage there is essentially the question of an
  influence on the very conditions which define the possible
  types of predictions regarding the future behavior of the
  system.”

- “Since these conditions constitute an inherent element of the
  description of any phenomenon to which the term ‘physical
  reality’ can be properly attached, we see that the
  argumentation of the mentioned authors does not justify their
  conclusion that quantum-mechanical description is essentially
  incomplete.”

- “In fact, it is only the mutual exclusion of any two
  experimental procedures which provides room for new physical
  laws, the coexistence of which might at first appear
  irreconcilable with the basic principles of science.”

- “It is just this entirely new situation that the notion of
  complementarity aims at characterizing.”

- “We see throughout the necessity of discriminating, in each
  experimental arrangement, between those parts of the physical
  system which are to be treated as measuring instruments and
  those which constitute the objects under investigation.”

- “It is true that this choice is a matter of convenience.”

- “But it is of fundamental importance, because we must use
  classical concepts to interpret all quantum-mechanical
  measurements.”

- “It is impossible to make a closer analysis of the reactions
  between the particle and the measuring instrument.”

- “We have to do here with a feature of individuality completely
  foreign to classical physics.”

- “The very existence of the quantum entails the necessity of
  a final renunciation of the classical ideal of `causality` and
  a radical revision of our attitude toward the problem of
  physical reality.”
