- Modern QM resolves the momentum issue without abandoning
  conservation:
    - Momentum is conserved in EACH EMISSION EVENT, for the
      combined atom–radiation system.
    - The outcome can be probabilistic: QM predicts probabilities
      for different emission directions.
    - Statistical regularity emerges from many such events.


### Discussion

- Yes, an atom recoils when it emits a photon.
- Schrödinger's 1922 worry came from the classical picture of
  radiation, and the quantum picture removed it.

- Why the puzzle arose. In the classical wave picture, an excited
  atom radiates a spherical wave, symmetric in all directions.
- A perfectly symmetric emission carries away no net momentum, so
  the atom would have nothing to recoil against, even though
  Einstein had shown light carries momentum (`p = E/c`, or `h/λ`
  for a quantum).
- Momentum conservation seemed to fail for single atoms. The
  related Bohr-Kramers-Slater proposal of 1924 also gave up
  strict conservation for individual events, holding only on
  average.

### How it was resolved.

- Two things settled it:

1. Emission is directional per event.
    - An atom emits one photon in one particular direction, not
      a spherical wave of energy. The "spherical wave" describes
      the probability distribution over directions across many
      emissions, not the outcome of any single one. In each event
      the photon carries momentum p = h/λ, and the atom recoils
      with equal and opposite momentum. This is exactly the
      gun-and-bullet picture.

2. Experiments confirmed strict conservation.
    - The Compton effect (1923) showed photon-electron scattering
      obeys energy and momentum conservation event by event. The
      Bothe-Geiger and Compton-Simon coincidence experiments
      (1925) then killed the statistical-conservation idea.
      Schrödinger's doubts about causality at the atomic level
      turned out to be unnecessary on this point.

- The recoil is real and measurable. The recoil velocity is
`v = h/(mλ)`. It is tiny for ordinary atoms (for sodium emitting at
589 nm it's about 3 cm/s), but it matters in several settings:

- Recoil shift:
    - the emitted photon's energy is slightly less than the
      atomic transition energy, because the atom takes a bit of
      kinetic energy. The absorbing atom needs a slightly
      higher-energy photon, so emission and absorption lines are
      offset by twice the recoil energy.

- Mössbauer effect:
    - in a free nucleus, gamma-ray recoil shifts the line enough
      to prevent resonant absorption. Locking the nucleus in
      a crystal lattice lets the whole crystal absorb the recoil,
      which makes recoil-free resonance possible.

- Laser cooling and atom interferometry:
    - these rely directly on photon recoil. Each absorbed or
      emitted photon gives the atom a calibrated momentum kick,
      and the recoil velocity sets fundamental limits such as the
      "recoil temperature."

A subtlety Schrödinger would have enjoyed:
    - In QM the emitted photon's direction is genuinely random
      for a single event, with a distribution given by the
      radiation pattern.
    - The atom's recoil direction is therefore equally random.

- Chance really is at the root of the single-event outcome, as
  Exner and Schrödinger suspected, but conservation laws hold
  exactly in every individual event.

- Randomness in which outcome occurs doesn't mean the conserved
  quantities fail. That is roughly where Schrödinger ended up in
  his later view of quantum probabilities, even though he never
  liked the Copenhagen interpretation.
