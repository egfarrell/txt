### 0. Setup: what QM “measurement” means

- In QM, the state `|ψ⟩` is a complete description of the system.

- A measurement is not “reading off a property the particle already had”.

- Instead, it is an _interaction_ that FORCES the state to give
  a definite outcome. Mathematically, that forcing is encoded
  using operators and their eigen-structure.


### 1. Observable operator (eg: position x̂)

- Meaning:
    - An observable `operator` is the mathematical object that
      represents a measurable physical quantity.

- For position in 1D, the observable is the position operator `x̂`.

- What it does:
    - `x̂` acts on state vectors and tells you “what position
      would be registered *if* the system were in a state with
      a definite position”.

- In the x-representation, `x̂ ψ(x) = x ψ(x)`

- Significance:
- Operators encode how measurement devices interact with states.
- Each observable has its own operator because different
  measurements are physically different interactions.

- Crucially:
    - Different observables generally do not commute → they
      cannot share a common set of definite values.

### 2. Eigenvector (example: |x⟩)

- Meaning:
    - An eigenvector of an observable operator is a STATE that
      gives a DEFINITE OUTCOME when that observable is measured.

- For position:
    - `x̂|x⟩ = x|x⟩`

- Interpretation:
    - If the electron is in the state `|x⟩`, then a position
      measurement will *certainly* return the value `x`.

- No probabilities. No spread. Just `x`.

- Significance:
    - Eigenvectors represent “perfectly sharp” states for that
      observable.

- This is not optional:
    - QM needs states that correspond to definite measurement
      outcomes, otherwise the theory could never explain why
      measurements ever give single numbers.

### 3. Eigenvalue (example: x)

- Meaning:
    - An eigenvalue is the _numerical result_ that comes out of
      a measurement when the system is in the corresponding
      eigenvector.

- For position:
    - Eigenvalue `x` = the position value shown by the detector.

- Interpretation:
    - Eigenvalues are not abstract math numbers — they are
      literally the possible measurement readouts.

- Significance:
    - The spectrum of an operator tells you which values can
      ever be observed.

- For position, the spectrum is continuous.
- For energy in an atom, it’s discrete.
- For spin, it’s finite.

- That’s where quantization actually enters the theory.

### 4. Eigenbasis (example: {|x⟩ for all x})

- Meaning:
    - An eigenbasis is the complete set of all eigenvectors of an
      observable.

- For position:
    - The set of all `|x⟩` for x ∈ ℝ.

- Interpretation:
    - The eigenbasis is the “coordinate frame” associated with
      that measurement.

- Any state `|ψ⟩` can be expanded as:
    - `|ψ⟩ = ∫ ψ(x)|x⟩ dx`
    - Here ψ(x) = ⟨x|ψ⟩.

- Significance:
    - This expansion is what allows QM to make PROBABILISTIC
      predictions.

- The squared magnitude `|ψ(x)|²` gives the PROBABILITY density
  for measuring position `x`.

### Why eigenvectors/eigenvalues are NECESSARY

...and not just convenient.

- This is the key conceptual point.

- QM has three empirical facts it must explain:
    1. Measurements yield definite outcomes (single numbers)
    2. Outcomes are probabilistic
    3. Different measurements are incompatible (`x` vs `p`, etc.)

- The eigenstructure solves all three at once:
    - Definite outcomes → eigenvalues
    - States with certainty → eigenvectors
    - Probabilities → expansion coefficients in an eigenbasis
    - Incompatibility → different operators have different eigenbases

- There is no simpler mathematical structure that does all of this simultaneously.

- If we didn’t use eigenvectors, we:
    - couldn’t define “definite position”
    - couldn’t explain why measurements collapse to single values
    - couldn’t explain why measuring `x` destroys info about `p`

### Position measurement in one sentence

- Measuring position means:
    - “Expand the state `|ψ⟩` in the position eigenbasis `{|x⟩}`,
      then randomly select one eigenvalue `x` with probability
      `|⟨x|ψ⟩|²`, and project the state onto `|x⟩`.”

- That entire sentence collapses without eigenvectors and eigenvalues.

    - LATER: Better rewording to make it shaper:
        - "Measure the system's position. The measurement yields
          an outcome `x`, with probabilities governed by `|⟨x|ψ⟩|²`.
        - In the idealised measurement model, the state is then
          projected onto the corresponding position eigenstate
          `|x⟩`."
        - The key distinction: the mathematics describes the
          possible outcomes and their probabilities; the
          laboratory measurement produces the actual outcome. The
          scientist sets up the experiment, not the result.

    - LATER: more clarification
        - The measurement process yields one eigenvalue `x`, with
          probability governed by `|⟨x|ψ⟩|²`, and projects the
          state onto the corresponding eigenstate `|x⟩`.”

        - The original phrase “randomly select” is simply
          shorthand for the probabilistic nature of a quantum
          measurement. It doesn't mean the scientist chooses the
          outcome.

        - One small technical refinement: because position has
          a CONTINUOUS spectrum, `|⟨x|ψ⟩|²` is a probability
          DENSITY, rather than the probability of obtaining one
          exact value `x`.



### Deep intuition (important)

- Eigenvectors are not mathematical decoration.
- They are the only states that a measurement device recognizes
  as STABLE outcomes.

- Every real detector is built to respond to eigenstates of the
  observable it measures.

- Aside:
    - Also, the position eigenstates `|x⟩` are idealised
      mathematical states, not normalisable physical states that
      a real detector produces exactly. The simple projection
      picture assumes an IDEALISED measurement.
