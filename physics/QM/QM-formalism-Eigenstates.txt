- The key idea is: an eigenstate of an observable is a state with
  a definite value for that observable.

### QM examples

- Observable being measured   Example EIGENSTATE  Corresponding eigenvalue  
- ---                         ---                 ---                       
- Position, x̂                 |x₀⟩                Position x₀  
- Momentum, p̂                 |p₀⟩                Momentum p₀  
- Energy, Ĥ                   |Eₙ⟩                Energy Eₙ    
- Spin along z, Ŝᶻ            |↑z⟩                +ℏ/2         
- Spin along z, Ŝᶻ            |↓z⟩                −ℏ/2         
- Angular momentum, L̂ᶻ        |ℓ,m⟩               mℏ           
- Photon number, N̂            |n⟩                 n photons    


### 1. Position

- Observable: position.
- Eigenstate: `|x₀⟩`.
- Meaning: a state with definite position `x₀`.

### 2. Energy

- Observable: the Hamiltonian `Ĥ`.
- Eigenstate: `|Eₙ⟩`.
- Meaning: a state with definite energy `Eₙ`. In the absence of
  time-dependent changes, it evolves in time only by acquiring an
  overall phase.

### 3. Spin

- Observable: spin along the z-direction, `Ŝᶻ`.
- Eigenstates: `|↑z⟩` and `|↓z⟩`.
- Meaning: definite spin outcomes along z, either +ℏ/2 or −ℏ/2.

- Notice that one observable can have multiple eigenstates, one
  for each possible outcome (or, in more complicated cases,
  multiple independent eigenstates for the same outcome).

### Connecting this to your coordinate-frame analogy

Your notes' analogy maps onto these examples as follows:

- Hilbert space: the space containing the quantum states.
- Coordinate frame: choose an observable, such as spin along z.
- Coordinate axes: the eigenstates associated with that observable, such as |↑z⟩ and |↓z⟩.
- Coordinates: the amplitudes of a state when expanded in that eigenbasis.

- For example, a spin state can be written as:
    - `|ψ⟩ = a|↑z⟩ + b|↓z⟩`

- where a and b are probability amplitudes.

- One important qualification: position and momentum eigenstates
  are idealised _generalised eigenstates_, rather than
  normalisable vectors in the Hilbert space. 
- The analogy is therefore especially straightforward for
  observables with discrete, non-degenerate eigenstates.
