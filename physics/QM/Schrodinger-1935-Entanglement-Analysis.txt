- “Diskussion der Wahrscheinlichkeitsbeziehungen zwischen
  getrennten Systemen,”
    - Published: Cambridge Philosophical Society.

- “Discussion of Probability Relations Between Separated Systems”
  (1935).

### Summary
    - Schrödinger analyzes the probabilistic structure of QM for
      composite systems that have interacted and then separated,
      showing that QM generically assigns a definite `pure state`
      to the WHOLE while denying definite states to the PARTS.
    - The resulting correlations between spatially separated
      subsystems can't be interpreted as reflecting ignorance
      about pre-existing local properties, but arise from
      entanglement, a structural feature of the quantum
      formalism.
    - Schrödinger argues the `wavefunction` of a subsystem
      represents only a conditional statistical description, not
      an OBJECTIVE PHYSICAL STATE, and this description depends
      on measurements performed elsewhere.
    - `Measurement` therefore induces a nonlocal change in state
      assignment _without physical interaction_, creating tension
      with classical notions of separability and independent
      reality.
    - He concludes that QM compels either the abandonment of
      classical `realism` for individual systems or the view that
      the wavefunction is an INCOMPLETE description of physical
      reality, with entanglement as the central source of the
      difficulty.


### Discussion

- Schrödinger analyzes the consequences of QM for composite
  systems that have interacted and then separated. He shows that,
  according to the formalism, the total system can be in
  a definite `pure state` even though neither subsystem possesses
  an individual state of its own.
- Instead, each subsystem is described only by a statistical
  mixture derived from the joint wavefunction. This feature isn't
  incidental but generic:
    - after interaction, the subsystems become what he calls
      “entangled” (Verschränkung), and their properties are no
      longer independently specifiable.

- He emphasizes that the quantum state of a SUBSYSTEM generally
  can't be regarded as representing an OBJECTIVE PHYSICAL
  CONDITION of that subsystem alone.
    - The reduced description depends on how the composite system
      is partitioned and on what measurements are contemplated
      elsewhere.
    - Consequently, probability relations between distant systems
      arise that can't be interpreted as reflecting pre-existing
      local properties.

- Schrödinger contrasts this with classical probability, where
  correlations are understood as arising from ignorance about
  definite but unknown states. In QM, by contrast, the
  correlations encoded in the wavefunction are stronger and
  structurally different:
    - even complete knowledge of the WHOLE does not permit
      assignment of definite states to the parts.

- He discusses measurement explicitly: when an observable is
  `measured` on one subsystem, the state description of the
  distant subsystem changes discontinuously, despite the absence
  of PHYSICAL INTERACTION.
    - Schrödinger stresses that this change isn't a physical
      signal but a change in the theoretical description
      conditioned on the measurement outcome.
    - Nonetheless, he regards this as deeply problematic for any
      realist interpretation that treats the wavefunction as
      a complete description of reality.

- The paper concludes that QM forces a revision of classical
  ideas of separability and independent reality of spatially
  separated systems. Schrödinger leaves open whether this
  indicates an incompleteness of the theory or a need to abandon
  classical intuitions about physical states, but he is clear
  that entanglement is the central, unavoidable feature and it's
  responsible for the tension.
