
- I'd sharpen three points:
    - An observable doesn't always determine a unique basis;
    - An eigenvalue doesn't always pick out one eigenvector;
    - The coordinate-frame analogy is informal.

# Revised notes

### Hilbert space

- The Hilbert space `H`:
    - A mathematical space containing the state vectors `|ψ⟩`.
    - Has an inner product and is complete.
    - Strictly, a physical pure state corresponds to a ray in
      Hilbert space, rather than to one unique state vector.
        - State vector: 
            - one particular vector, with a specific length and
              direction.
        - Ray: 
            - all vectors pointing in that same direction, with
              any non-zero length.
    - In QM: 
        - multiplying a state vector by a NON-ZERO complex number
          doesn't change the ray. 
        - For normalised state vectors, this means `|ψ⟩` and
          `e^(iφ)|ψ⟩` represent the same physical pure state,
          regardless of global phase `φ`.
        - Aside:
            - Strictly, this scalar-multiple statement applies to
              NON-ZERO complex scalars; the `zero vector` is not
              a state vector representing a physical state.

- A coordinate frame:
    - Informally, corresponds to choosing an observable A
      and using its eigenstates as a basis.
    - More precisely, the observable determines the relevant
      eigenstates or eigenspaces, subject to degeneracy.

- A coordinate axis:
    - Informally, corresponds to one basis vector |aₙ⟩.
    - More precisely, the vector defines a one-dimensional
      subspace, analogous to a coordinate axis.


### Choosing an observable

- Choose a physical quantity (observable) A:
    - Position x, momentum p, energy H, spin S_z, etc.
    - A is represented by a self-adjoint operator.

- For an observable with a discrete, non-degenerate spectrum:
    - Its eigenstates {|aₙ⟩} form an orthonormal basis.
    - This basis is analogous to a coordinate FRAME.


### Choosing a measurement outcome

- Choose a particular eigenvalue aₙ:
    - If the eigenvalue is non-degenerate, it identifies
      one eigenstate |aₙ⟩.
    - That eigenstate corresponds, by analogy, to one
      coordinate AXIS.
    - If the eigenvalue is degenerate, it identifies an
      eigenspace, not one unique eigenstate.


### The hierarchy (as a useful analogy)

- Hilbert space
- → choose an observable A
- → obtain its eigenstates/eigenspaces
- → choose a measurement outcome aₙ
- → identify the corresponding eigenstate or eigenspace


# Two important caveats

### 1. Degeneracy

- If several independent eigenstates share the same eigenvalue,
  the measurement outcome does not distinguish between them. The
  outcome identifies an eigenspace, and you can choose different
  orthonormal bases within that eigenspace.

- So your original statement that choosing a value singles out
  one basis vector is correct only for a non-degenerate
  eigenvalue.

### 2. Position and momentum

- Position and momentum have continuous spectra. Their
  eigenstates, conventionally written |x⟩ and |p⟩, are
  generalised eigenstates: they are not ordinary, normalisable
  vectors in the Hilbert space. The simple picture of a countable
  orthonormal basis
