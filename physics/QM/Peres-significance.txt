## 1. What Peres actually does

Peres's paper has two main moves.&#x20;

First, he derives a Bell inequality without invoking hidden variables explicitly.

He compares correlations in a classical model — fragments of an exploding bomb carrying opposite angular momenta — with the quantum correlations of a spin-singlet pair.

The classical correlations cannot match the quantum correlations at every angle. His argument makes this contrast concrete without beginning by assuming a particular hidden-variable ontology.

Second, he examines the hypothetical measurements underlying the argument.

Suppose Alice measures along direction `a`, while Bob measures along direction `b`.

Now imagine that Bob had measured along `b′` instead.

If Alice's result is independent of Bob's choice of measurement, Alice would have obtained the same result either way. This is the crucial assumption connecting the actual experiment to the hypothetical alternative.

We can therefore try to construct a table containing the actual and hypothetical outcomes. Peres shows that no such table can consistently satisfy both the required locality condition and the quantum correlations.

That is the substance behind his famous conclusion: unperformed experiments have no results.

## 2. What is the genuinely interesting point?

It is not simply that an unperformed experiment has no recorded result. That is obvious.

The deeper issue is this:

Can we consistently assign hypothetical outcomes to alternative measurements while retaining the assumption that the distant measurement choice makes no difference to the local result?

Peres shows that the answer is no, if those assignments must also reproduce the quantum correlations.

This gives us a clear fork:

- Retain the local, predetermined-outcome picture, and the resulting assignments contradict the quantum statistics.
- Reject that picture, and abandon at least one of the assumptions that made those assignments possible.

Peres brings this tension into sharp focus. He also emphasises that classical correlations can satisfy the inequality, whereas quantum correlations violate it.&#x20;

## 3. But isn't this already in Bell?

Yes, substantially.

Bell's original 1964 argument uses perfect correlations and locality to establish predetermined outcomes for alternative settings. He then shows that those outcomes must satisfy an inequality contradicted by quantum mechanics.

Peres's argument is closely related. The main difference is one of presentation and emphasis: he foregrounds the construction of hypothetical outcomes and questions whether they can consistently be assigned.

There is, however, one important qualification. Later formulations of Bell's theorem also accommodate stochastic local models. They do not require us to assume, independently, that every possible measurement already has a definite outcome. Bell inequalities still follow from the relevant local-probability assumptions.&#x20;

Consequently, saying "unperformed experiments have no results" does not, by itself, evade Bell's theorem. It challenges one way of picturing the argument, not the full mathematical result.

## 4. So what should you take away from Peres?

I would put it this way:

Bell establishes the incompatibility between quantum correlations and the relevant assumptions of local causality.

Peres helps expose why a locally explicable world cannot be represented by a single consistent table of predetermined outcomes for all the alternative measurements.

That is a conceptual clarification, not an additional physical discovery. It is useful when discussing whether Bell assumes counterfactual definiteness, but it does not independently establish non-locality or improve the inequality itself.

The most important distinction is this:

Peres's slogan is not Bell's theorem. It is a way of expressing the failure of a particular classical picture of measurement outcomes.

And we should not interpret it as a general proof that hypothetical outcomes are never meaningful. In a deterministic local theory, counterfactual outcomes can be meaningful precisely because the theory specifies what would happen under alternative measurement settings. Bell's result is that such a local account cannot reproduce all the observed quantum correlations.&#x20;
