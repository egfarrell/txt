      Schrödinger went back to basics. Starting with the standard
      wave equation of classical mechanics, he used the de

      until it was pointed out.

### Picturing Quantum Processes
    - https://www.amazon.co.uk/Picturing-Quantum-Processes-Diagrammatic-Reasoning/dp/110710422X
    - Misleading title, but great book on `process theory`
    - Not a good first book on QM: the gentle introduction in
      Chapter 1 drops off sharply.
    - An excellent intro to *process theories*, a useful branch
      of category theory. A QM background helps, but isn't
      essential. The “pictures” are abstract diagrams, not
      visualisations of subatomic phenomena.
    - Process theory represents mathematical relationships as
      wires connecting boxes, replacing repeated symbols across
      equations with diagrammatic connections. It's a special
      case of category theory where connection order matters, but
      not how connections are bundled.
    - The authors show that some features associated with QM
      arise simply from cycles in these diagrams; even the
      no-cloning theorem requires little QM to demonstrate. 
    - For those with a QM background, the book offers a fresh
      perspective; for others, a useful intro to process theory.

### Lubos primer QM (also QM is local!)
    - $MY_NOTES/physics/QM/Lubos-review-Bricmont.txt
    - All observations are always associated with `linear
      operators` on a `complex Hilbert space`.
    - A priori possible results of a measurement are always given
      by the `spectrum` of the observable
        - (e.g. the eigenvalues of the operator).
    - Every measurement always collapses the state vector (wave
      function) to an `eigenstate` of the measured operator
      corresponding to the measured eigenvalue, it always acts as
      a `projection`.
    - The squared absolute values of the complex probability
      amplitudes always determine that the measurement produces
      a corresponding eigenvalue (Born's rule).
    - The evolution in time (or other transformations) are always
      given by UNITARY operators.
    - The space of allowed states are always elements of a linear
      vector space – complex superpositions of vectors `ψ`,`φ`
      are always as allowed as the vectors `ψ`,`φ` themselves.
    - The superposition of two states `ψ`,`φ` always means "one
      OR the other", not "one AND the other", as Bricmont and
      others often incorrectly say.
    - Composite systems are always described by Hilbert spaces
      that are tensor products of Hilbert spaces for the parts
      (subsystems).
    - Most of the vectors in such Hilbert spaces (of the
      composite system) are always `entangled` i.e. they can't be
      written as tensor products of vectors from the two
      subsystems' spaces (only as a superposition of several
      terms of this type).
     


### Sabine: entanglement measurement - nothing changes?
    - $MY_NOTES/physics/QM/Sabine-Entanglement.txt
    - In an experiment, you can flip the spin of one particle,
      say the left one, even without knowing what it is. If it
      was `−1`, it is now `+1`; if it was `+1`, it is now `−1`. 
        - If you do that, what happens to the particle on the
          right side? NOTHING. 
        - The other particle itself has not changed, and the two
          particles are still entangled — just the correlation
          between them has changed. 
        - You have changed an entangled system into a different,
          also entangled system. 
        - There is no “spooky action” in entanglement, no
          exchange of information that is faster than the speed
          of light.

### Heisenberg cut / "schnitt"
    - $MY_NOTES/physics/QM/Age-Entanglement-Louisa-Gilder.txt
    - A deterministic extension of QM would need to introduce
      additional variables or rules that restore causal
      evolution. Such additions would have to act at the
      interface between the quantum system and the classical
      measuring apparatus (the “cut/Schnitt”). 
    - On both sides of this interface, each domain’s laws hold
      precisely: quantum dynamics for the system, classical
      dynamics for the apparatus. Statistical behavior enters
      only at this boundary because measurement inevitably
      disturbs the system’s evolution.
    - However, the location of the cut isn't fixed. One can
      always treat a larger portion of the apparatus quantum
      mechanically, as long as some remaining part is described
      classically. If new deterministic variables were
      introduced, their behavior would need to remain consistent
      no matter where this boundary is placed. 
    - Because shifting the cut changes which components are
      governed by quantum versus classical descriptions,
      enforcing deterministic hidden properties across all
      possible placements leads to contradictions with the
      established probabilistic structure of quantum theory. Thus
      such deterministic “completions” are generally incompatible
      with the movable nature of the quantum–classical boundary.
    - Also: Von Neumann's incorrect `assumption`:
        - Von Neumann’s no-hidden-variables proof assumes that
          any deeper theory must preserve the quantum rule ⟨P
          + Q⟩ = ⟨P⟩ + ⟨Q⟩, but this linearity belongs to the
            Hilbert-space formalism itself and need not constrain
            unknown underlying variables. Because the assumption
            forbids alternative value-assignments from the start,
            the proof excludes hidden variables by stipulation
            rather than argument. The uncertainty relations show
            that exact position and momentum don't exist
            simultaneously as classical properties, not merely
            that we can't measure them precisely. Once that
            classical assumption is dropped, the possibility
            remains that some other, nonclassical characteristics
            could causally determine motion, and QM alone doesn't
            rule them out.

    - Colloquium: What's Wrong with QM, and How to Fix It 
    - Jacob Barandes, Harvard 
    - Does textbook quantum theory suffer from fundamental
      inconsistencies, like the measurement problem, or do
      phenomena like decoherence save the day? 
    - I'll make the case that the problems with the textbook
      theory are real and significant, and efforts to address
      them have produced many practical spin-offs, including
      decoherence itself. 
    - I'll lay out a minimal set of criteria that any consistent
      axiomatic formulation of quantum theory should satisfy.
    - I'll argue that none of the existing options, including the
      Many Worlds interpretation, clear this bar, so another
      approach to quantum foundations is urgently needed. 
    - I'll introduce one new such approach, based on a remarkably
      simple axiomatic picture in which every system has an
      old-fashioned configuration in physical space that evolves
      according to an “indivisible” stochastic process, and in
      which wave functions and superposition are no longer
      literal ingredients of reality. 
    - I'll also present a mathematical mapping between these
      stochastic processes and the usual Hilbert-space
      formulation of quantum theory. 
    - After describing this stochastic-quantum correspondence,
      and going through several concrete examples, I'll turn to
      a careful dissection of Bell's theorem, and show how
      `indivisible quantum theory` points to shortcomings in the
      theorem's claims about local causality. 
    - I will conclude with several intriguing possible
      applications to physics and to other areas of science.
    - References: https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.10778,
      https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.16935.

### Key: The elephant in the room
    - Lucien Hardy talk at Lee Smolin Fest     
        - https://pirsa.org/c25023
    - The measurement problem
    - According to unitary evolution we can have
        - `ψ  =  ψ_bigthing at A  +  ψ_bigthing at B`
    - If we believe this, we're on the horns of a dilemma.
      Either:
    - REALITY PROBLEM: 
        - there's a mismatch between the quantum description and
          way the world appears to be (as big objects appear to
          be in definite places), or
    - INCOMPLETENESS: 
        - The wavefunction isn't a complete description of
          reality. (Lee thinks this)
    - `The measurement problem` is the elephant in the room.
    1. Reality problem
        - Claim: The wavefunction evolves smoothly via unitary
          (Schrödinger) evolution into superpositions (e.g., `ψ
          = ψ_A + ψ_B`).
        - But: We never observe macroscopic superpositions (e.g.,
          a chair simultaneously here and there).
        - Implication: There's a mismatch between theory and
          observed reality.
        - Interpretation: 
            - Either the wavefunction doesn't correspond directly
              to reality, or something about observation
              "collapses" the state.
        - Examples: 
            - Schrödinger’s cat, macroscopic superpositions.
        - Responses: 
            - Collapse theories (e.g., GRW), objective collapse,
              or reinterpretation of the wavefunction.
    2. Incompleteness
        - Claim: The wavefunction doesn't give the full physical
          story.
        - Implication: QM is incomplete; hidden variables or
          additional structure may be needed.
        - Historical example: Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen (EPR)
          argued for hidden variables.
        - Modern outcomes: Bell’s theorem shows any hidden
          variables must be nonlocal.
        - Responses: Bohmian mechanics, Many Worlds (where the
          wavefunction is complete, but reality is richer than
          appearance), epistemic views of the wavefunction.

    - For those who feel weird about the whole "forbidden
      transitions being only possible with quantum tunneling"
      thing and want an alternative interpretation:
        - It's only true that the transitions are forbidden under
          a given simplified model of the atom. 
        - It is very much possible to calculate the transition
          probabilities under a more realistic model, and the
          previously "forbidden" transitions are now just regular
          transitions with lower probability.
        - In this case, the simplified model is that of the
          electric dipole approximation, where the atom is taken
          to be an electric dipole (reasonable when the
          wavelength of light emitted during an atomic transition
          is much larger than the size of the atom).
        - This means it interacts with EM radiation only through
          electric dipole interactions, which implies that energy
          transitions must change orbital angular momentum, hence
          the 21cm transition is "forbidden". 
    - But an atom isn't truly an `electric dipole`, and so the
      21cm transition is possible by the magnetic dipole
      interaction, just with low probability.
        - (This low probability is due to the relative strength
          of the magnetic interaction compared to the electric
          interaction).

### Measurement independence (important!)
    - Informal: Sabine
        - ./Sabine-measurement-independence.txt
        - This finally brings us to Bell's theorem. I'm not going
          to explain it because you've already watched 20 videos
          about it, and it didn't help. 
        - I'll just tell you what it says. 
        - Bell's theorem says if you WANT a local hidden
          variables model that reproduces QM correctly, then it
          needs to violate `measurement independence`.

    - Formal: Stanford
        - https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/bell-theorem/
        - Different experiments may be performed on each system.
          We will use a,a′ as variables ranging over possible
          experiments on 1, and b,b′ as variables that range over
          experiments on 2. 
        - It isn't assumed that these parameters capture the
          complete state of the experimental apparatus, which
          might have a range of microstates corresponding to each
          experimental setting.
        - It is assumed that the preparation probability
          distribution `ρ` is independent of the processes by
          which a choice of experiments to be performed is made. 
        - This assumption we call the Measurement Independence
          Assumption.

### Beam splitter: momentum conservation
    - https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HEWMwnCZHJY
    - Question: 
        - I always thought in QM that conservation laws apply to
          average values. If they truly hold for every single
          event, I find it hard to understand how the
          Mach–Zehnder interferometer would work. 
        - Wouldn’t the interaction with the mirrors destroy the
          interference pattern? 
            - After all, each part of the photon’s wavefunction
              is now in a superposition with the moving mirrors.
        - Furthermore: still considering a Mach–Zehnder
          setup—wouldn’t the action of the first beam splitter
          alone be enough to reduce the interference effect?
            - Since, in principle, one could measure its
              momentum, how is the interference preserved?
    - Sabine:
        - Yes, this is a very good question. The reason that the
          interaction with the beam splitter and/or mirror
          doesn't destroy the superposition is that you can't
          measure it and in fact don't measure it. 
        - You can imagine the mirror and/or splitter to be so
          small that they are another particle and the
          interaction causes a measurable change. 
            - If you then measure the splitter-particle, the
              superposition will collapse.
    - Further clarification:
        - Consider a single photon fired at a beam splitter. 
        - What Sabine means is that the photon only interact with
          a small nano-scale portion of the macro-scale beam
          splitter that you can see with your eyes. 
        - While that's technically false (since any small
          momentum change would propagate), the relevant thing is
          that the information about any momentum changes in the
          beam splitter that's caused by interaction with the
          photon is lost at the macro-scale scale, with all the
          background noise in whatever actual setup you have.
        - Even if the momentum of the macro-scale beam splitter
          is very precisely measured, its correlation with
          interactions with the photon is extremely low. 
            - This is in contrast with the photon's interaction
              with a detector, where the photon's signal is
              intentionally amplified, so that its presence is
              correlated with a macro-scaled event (such as an
              electric current) that we can measure.
        - If you then suggest to measure momentum changes in the
          beam splitter at nano-scale portions, then you are
          turning the beam splitter into a detector. 
            - This is similar to measuring which slit a particle
              passes through in a double-slit experiment

### Origin of Schrodinger Eqn
    - 1. Prior events. Schr¨odinger cultivated soil that had been
      prepared by others. Planck was led to write `E = hν` by his
      successful attempt (1900) to use the recently-established
      principles of statistical mechanics to account for the
      spectral distribution of thermalized electromagnetic
      radiation. 
    - It was soon appreciated that Planck’s energy/frequency
      relation was relevant to the understanding of optical
      phenomena that take place _far from thermal equilibrium_
      (photoelectric effect: Einstein 1905; atomic radiation:
      Bohr 1913). 
    - By 1916 it had become clear to Einstein that, while light
      is in some contexts well described as a Maxwellian wave, in
      other contexts it is more usefully thought of as a massless
      particle, with energy `E = hν` and momentum `p = h/λ`. 
        - The mechanical statement `E = cp`* and the wave
          relation `λν = c` become then alternative ways of
          saying the same thing. 
            - *: from the relativistic statement `E
              = c ∙ sqrt(p^2 + (mc)^2)` in the limit `m -> 0`.
        - Einstein had in effect “invented the photon” (which,
          however, did not acquire its name until 1926 from
          American chemist, Gilbert Lewis).

### GPT, Schrodinger and PHysical Space
    - You're absolutely correct in highlighting this distinction.
      The statement that "the wavefunction ψ(r,t)ψ(r,t) in
      non-relativistic QM is a function of both position rr
      (which represents a point in physical space) and time tt"
      applies accurately only to a single particle. For systems
      involving multiple particles, the wavefunction depends on
      a much larger space: configuration space.
    - Wavefunction in Configuration Space
        - Single Particle: For a single particle, the
          wavefunction ψ(r,t)ψ(r,t) is indeed a function of the
          particle's position in physical space rr and time tt.
        - Multiple Particles: For a system of NN particles, the
          wavefunction
          ψ(r1,r2,…,rN,t)ψ(r1​,r2​,…,rN​,t) is
          a function of the positions of all NN particles. Here,
          r1,r2,…,rNr1​,r2​,…,rN​ are the position
          vectors of the particles, each of which represents
          a point in 3-dimensional physical space. The
          wavefunction now resides in a configuration space,
          which is the Cartesian product of the physical spaces
          for all the particles. This configuration space has
          a dimension of 3N3N for NN particles.
    - Schrödinger's Original Interpretation and Lorentz's
      Correction
    - When Schrödinger first introduced his equation, he
      initially interpreted the wavefunction as describing a wave
      in physical space. For a single particle, this
      interpretation holds. However, Hendrik Lorentz pointed out
      that for systems of multiple particles, the wavefunction
      could no longer be interpreted as a simple wave in
      3-dimensional physical space but rather as a function
      defined in the higher-dimensional configuration space.
    - For example:
        - Two Particles: The wavefunction
          ψ(r1,r2,t)ψ(r1​,r2​,t) is a function of six
          spatial coordinates (three for each particle) plus
          time. It doesn't describe a wave in physical space but
          rather in the 6-dimensional configuration space.
        - Three Particles: The wavefunction
          ψ(r1,r2,r3,t)ψ(r1​,r2​,r3​,t) exists in
          a 9-dimensional configuration space.
    - Implications
        - Configuration Space: For more than one particle, the
          wavefunction doesn't have a simple interpretation in
          physical space. Instead, it describes the probability
          amplitude for the system as a whole, considering all
          the particles' positions simultaneously in
          configuration space.
        - Physical Space Misinterpretation: Schrödinger's initial
          belief that the wavefunction was a physical wave in
          ordinary 3-dimensional space was only valid for single
          particles. For many-particle systems, this
          interpretation had to be corrected, as Lorentz pointed
          out, leading to the understanding that the wavefunction
          lives in configuration space for such systems.
    - Conclusion
    - Your statement is correct: the wavefunction for a system of
      multiple particles can't be simply interpreted as
      a function over physical space. It must be understood as
      a function over configuration space, which grows in
      dimensionality with the number of particles in the system.
      This distinction is crucial in QM, as it affects how we
      interpret the nature of quantum states in more complex
      systems.

### Concepts Underlying QM
    - key: Classical Physics based on concepts of Material points
      and potential energy (forces)
    - $MY_BOOKS/Physics/QG/Einstein-Studies-02-Conceptual-Problems-QG.pdf
    - So Einstein felt that QM started out with a set of basic
      concepts that were outmoded.
    - Therefore if one could not advance further than quantum
      mechanics did on the basis of these concepts, it was not
      really so surprising to him.
    - On the contrary, as we will see in a moment, what he found
      surprising is that one could get so far with a set of
      concepts known to be inadequate. He outlined this
      inadequacy of the classical-mechanical starting point of
      QM:
        > I don't at all doubt that the contemporary quantum
        > theory ("QM") is the most complete theory compatible
        > with experience, as long as one bases the description
        > on the concepts of MATERIAL POINTS and POTENTIAL ENERGY
        > as fundamental concepts (Einstein ,1953; po 6;
        > translation cited from Stachel 1986a, ppo 375-376)0
    - Arthur Fine has noted that while the old Einstein is often
      considered a reactionary and the quantum mechanicians
      considered radicals, from Einstein's point of view, the
      situation was just the opposite (Fine 1986, Chapter 2).
    - The adherents of QM were clinging to the
      classical-mechanical concepts, trying to find some way to
      continue doing physics only using this outmoded conceptual
      basis, whereas he was searching for a _new conceptual basis
      for physics_, which he regarded as the radical step. To
      continue with Einstein:
        > [The difficulties of QM] are connected with the fact
        > that one retains the classical concept of FORCE or
        > POTENTIAL ENERGY and only replaces the LAW OF MOTION by
        > something entirely new. To me it seems, however, that
        > one will finally recognize that something must take the
        > place of forces acting or potential energ.y Something
        > that has an atomistic structure in the same sense as
        > the electron itself. "Weak fields" or forces as active
        > causes will then not occur at all, just as little as
        > mixed states (Einstein 1953, ppo 12, 14; translation
        > cited from Stachel1986a, p. 376)

### deBroglie movement
    - $MY_BOOKS/Physics/Maths/Math-Phy-Clifford-Geo-Algebra/QM-in-Clifford-Algebra.pdf
    - Einstein and deBroglie discovered the quantum world...

      material particle
        - I always thought it was the particle _itself_ that was
          a wave
    - The `mass` term of the Dirac equation is replaced by
      a slightly different `mass` term, and this is enough to
      solve the problem of unphysical negative energy.
    - The true frame allowing us to describe the Dirac wave is
      the Clifford algebra of 3-dimensional space

    - interesting: (Lagrangian)
        - One consequence of the greater invariance is a double
          link between the wave and its Lagrangian formalism.
        - Fermat was the first to understand that light moves in
          a way that minimizes the duration of its propagation.
        - Next, all laws of mechanics were understood as governed
          by such a principle of minimum.
        - The de Broglie wave was obtained by uniting these two
          principles of minimum.
        - Today all QM stems from such a mechanism, a Lagrangian
          density linked to the quantum wave.
        - Calculus of variations allows us to get the wave
          equation from this Lagrangian density.
        - The novelty is that when the wave equation is read in
          an invariant form, in Clifford algebra, the Lagrangian
          density is exactly the real scalar part of the wave
          equation.
        - This is true for the electron in the space algebra,
          true for the pair electron+neutrino in the space-time
          algebra, and true again for the extended wave
          electron+neutrino+quarks in the Clifford algebra of the
          extended space-time.
        - This approach also explains why there are three
          generations and four kinds of neutrinos, the last one
          only experiencing gravitational interactions.

      representation).

      `interfere` with each other.

### Contextual properties
    - Kochen (PIRSA)
    - QM systems have 'contextual' (extrinsic, relational)
      properties

    - property of a system:
    - classical mechanics: _intrinsic_ properties
        - eg. you measure the temperature of water
        - you assume that what you are measuring is a property
          the water _already has_
            - property: temperature
            - system: water

    - QM:
        - situation very different: _extrinsic_ (relational,
          contextual) properties
        - these appear for a system as a result of its
          interaciton with another system
        - eg. you want to measure the property of spin in
          a particular direction (Stern-Gerlach)
        - no-one now assumes the property of spin in a particular
          direction is an intrinsic property of the system
        - rather it is the _interaction_ of spin with the
          magnetic field which is inhomegenous in that direction
          which brings about the value of spin in that direction

    - mathematical differences: between intrinsic and extrinsic
      variables
    - discussion about kolmorogov's basic probablity rules:
      Boolean σ-algebra
    - kolmorogov main rule: probability is additive on disjoint
      events
    - thats the basis of all modern probability theory
    - and despite what many physicists think, the exact same
      probability theory applies to QM
    - Born rule: defines an ordinary Kolmorogov probability
      measure on the interaction algebra generated by the set of
      'projections'

### QM systems oscillate sinusoidally
    - book: Quirky Quantum concepts
    - SPACE:
        - Quantum systems oscillate _sinusoidally_ in space with
          a frequency proportional to momentum: `k = p/ħ`.
        - ie., quantum systems have a wavelength, given by de
          Broglie. `k` is the spatial frequency in (say) radians
          per meter, and it points in the direction of momentum.
        - This momentum-wavelength relation was known to be true
          for photons from simple interferometry (which means it
          is true relativistically, as well, since photons travel
          at light speed).

    - TIME:
        - Quantum systems oscillate sinusoidally in time with
          a frequency proportional to energy : ω = E/ħ.
        - This was known true for photons from the photoelectric
          effect (which again means it is true relativistically).
        - It is also experimentally confirmed for both
          electrostatic and gravitational potential energy [1,
          pp. 125–129].
        - Any temporal oscillation has a frequency, phase, and
          real amplitude.
        - The real amplitude and phase can be described
          mathematically by a phasor : a complex number that
          represents a sinusoid.
        - The frequency isn't given by the phasor, and must come
          from other information, in this case, the energy.
        - In QM, a phasor may be called a complex amplitude , or
          confusingly, often just “amplitude.”
        - Because quantum systems oscillate, and we represent
          those oscillations with complex-valued phasors, the
          wave-function is a complex-valued function of space (or
          a phasor-valued function of space).
        - Since energy can be positive or negative, the frequency
          of oscillation can be positive or negative. We describe
          phasors and negative frequency later.
        - [In a relativistic treatment, axioms (1) and (2)
          combine into a Lorentz covariant form that unifies the
          time and space pieces.]

### Maudlin - QM Labyrinth
    - $MY_BOOKS/Physics/QM/Labyrinyth-QM-Maudlin.pdf
    - Double Slit experiment: Individual electrons seem to
      somehow interfere with themselves.
    - For all its mathematical rigor, von Neumann’s scheme is
      unprofessionally vague, as Bell would say.
    - Surely a measurement is just some sort of _physical
      interaction_ between systems, just as otherwise occurs with
      the passage of time.
    - After all, what physical characteristic could distinguish
      measurements from other sorts of interactions,
      a characteristic so precise that its presence or absence
      makes the difference between smooth deterministic evolution
      and stochastic jumpy “collapses”.
    - This, in a nutshell is the measurement problem. 
    - The Putnam of 1965 was rightfully scandalized by the state
      of it. In 1968 Putnam returns to quantum theory with “The
      logic of QM”. 
        - As the title indicates, questioning—or at least
          discussing—logic itself is now on the main menu. 
        - Our quick review of the 1965 works have set the table:
          in those, Putnam rejects real physical collapses of the
          wavefunction, “hidden” variables, and the Copenhagen
          interpretation because none of them could deliver what
          he regarded as a plausible account of measurement as
          a purely physical interaction between physical systems. 
    - The question that we must keep in our minds is how the
      introduction of a new logic, or even more radically the
      replacement of classical logic with a new logic, could even
      in principle help to resolve this problem.
    - In special cases if you hand the operator a wavefunction it
      hands back the very same wavefunction multiplied by
      a number. Such a wavefunction is called an eigenfunction of
      the operator and the number is called its eigenvalue.
    - At this point, a key physical assumption is made. 
    - The “observable” operators are associated with physical
      magnitudes and the condition for a system to have
      a definite physical magnitude is that its wavefunction be
      an eigenfunction of the associated operator. The value of
      the physical magnitude is then its eigenvalue.
    - Any wavfunction that isn't an eigenfunction of the operator
      fails to have any value for the magnitude at all.
    - Thus, a wavefunction that isn't an eigenfunction of the
      position operator represents a system that fails to have
      any definite position, and a waverfunction that isn't an
      eigenfunction of the momentum operator represents a system
      that fails to have any definite momentum. 
        - And since no POSITION eigenfunction is a MOMENTUM
          eigenfunction, mathematics alone guarantees that no
          system can simultaneously have a definite position and
          a definite momentum.
    - Indeed, the closer a system comes to having a definite
      momentum, the further it is from having a definite position
      and vice versa. This is an instance of the uncertainty
      relation or complementarity.
    - So there are two key questions that the standard
      presentation of QM fails to answer:
        - 1) Is the wavefunction a complete physical description
          of a system or not?
        - 2) How does the "pre-interaction with a 'measuring'
          device" state of a system relate to its
          post-interaction state? 
            - What does the outcome of the measurement indicate
              about the system before it was measured? 
            - Without answers to these questions, physical
              answers, the truth value of many claims can't even
              be addressed.
