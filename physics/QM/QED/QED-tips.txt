


### History QED
    - Quantum Electrodynamics (QED) was the first _successful_
      theory to unify QM with EM. But it wasn’t the first
      attempt.

    - Before Feynman, Schwinger, and Tomonaga polished it into
      renormalized, self-consistent form, there were several
      valiant (and messy) attempts to quantize the EM field.

    - Lineage:
        - 1920s: The first quantization attempts. After
          Schrödinger and Heisenberg introduced QM (1925–26),
          theorists like Dirac, Jordan, and Heisenberg
          immediately wondered how to _apply it to light_. 
        - Dirac’s 1927 paper “The Quantum Theory of the Emission
          and Absorption of Radiation” was the first serious step
          — he introduced creation and annihilation operators for
          photons. That was, in hindsight, the birth of quantum
          field theory.

        - 1930s: Quantum electrodynamics, version 0.9 (buggy).
          The theory worked conceptually but gave nonsense
          infinities when you calculated self-energy corrections
          — like the infinite mass and charge of the electron.
          People like Pauli, Weisskopf, and Fermi tried various
          fixes. They could quantize fields, but they couldn’t
          make the math finite.

        - Late 1940s: QED 1.0 (fully debugged). Feynman,
          Schwinger, and Tomonaga independently found ways to
          systematically renormalize the infinities — absorb them
          into redefined mass and charge, leaving finite,
          predictive results. Dyson then unified their methods
          into a coherent framework, showing they were equivalent
          formulations of the same underlying theory.

        - This was the birth of modern QED — the first complete
          and consistent quantum field theory, and the first
          successful quantum description of electromagnetism.

        - So Feynman, Schwinger, and Tomonaga completed the
          project that began with Dirac’s quantization of the EM
          field. 
        - QED wasn’t the first attempt, but it was the first
          success — the point where quantum mechanics and
          Maxwell’s electromagnetism were finally welded into
          a predictive and experimentally perfect union.

        - It became the prototype for all later quantum field
          theories, including the electroweak theory and QCD. In
          a sense, every quantum field theory since has been
          trying to replicate QED’s combination of beauty, rigor,
          and experimental accuracy.


### QED incomplete.

    - However, QED is an inherently incomplete theory. 
    - It ignores many processes that must come into play at high
      energies or short distances.
        - In particular, it completely ignores the microstructure
          of space-time, and simply assumes that space-time can
          be approximated by the smooth continuum of Minkowski
          space at arbitrarily small distances.
        - Therefore, it can blame the divergence of its
          perturbation series on all these effects that lie
          outside its realm.

### Positronium Physics
    - ### Tri-electron
        - The term usually refers to a bound state made of one
          positron (the antimatter twin of the electron, same
          mass but positive charge) and two electrons. So:
        - `tri-electron = e+ + e- + e-`
        - This is a three-body system that’s electrically charged
          overall (net -1), and is held together by Coulomb
          attraction:
            - the positron attracts both electrons, while the
              electrons repel each other.
            - It’s like a very delicate molecular ion made out of
              pure matter-antimatter-electron glue.

    - ### Allen Mills:
        - ... is a physicist well known in the field of
          positronium physics. Positronium is a bound state of an
          electron and a positron (a sort of "atom" without
          a nucleus).
        - After positronium was discovered, theorists asked:
            - what other weird few-body states could exist?
        - Could you have more complex "atoms" made only of
          electrons and positrons?
        - Among these candidates are positronium negative ions
          (Ps-), which are exactly this tri-electron:
            - a positron plus two electrons.
        - They were predicted in the 1940s (by Ruark and others)
          and finally detected experimentally in the late 1980s
          by... Allen Mills.
        - The tri-electron (Ps-) is unstable. It only lives for
          a fraction of a nanosecond (about 0.5 nanoseconds).
          Then the positron annihilates with one of the
          electrons, releasing gamma photons.

    - So "tri-electron" is shorthand for the positronium negative
      ion, `Ps-`.
    - It’s one of those peculiar few-body solutions that tests
      our understanding of QED.
    - You can’t really bottle it;
        - it flashes in and out of existence too quickly. But
          proving it exists at all was a big deal in confirming
          QED’s predictions for multi-body exotic states.

    - To place it in a broader arc: it’s like the hydrogen
      negative ion (`H-`, which is one proton with two
      electrons), except you’ve swapped the proton for
      a positron.
        - A hydrogen negative ion is weakly bound and fragile;
          the positronium negative ion is even more fragile, but
          still real.

    - The fun part is that these tiny fleeting entities hint at
      how much stranger matter could be under different
      rules—whole "chemistries" made of electron-positron
      complexes instead of protons and neutrons.
