# Bell’s Theorem and the Locality vs. Realism Debate

Bell’s theorem (1964) shows that _no theory satisfying
a reasonable locality condition_ can reproduce the statistical
predictions of quantum mechanics. In practice, Bell-type
experiments have violated the resulting inequalities. What this
rules out, however, has been hotly debated. Some conclude that
_locality_ must be sacrificed (accept "quantum nonlocality"),
while others argue that the culprit is _realism/hidden
variables_. Often one hears that _"local realism"_ is impossible,
but precisely what is meant by _realism_ or _locality_ varies.
What follows is a review of key positions and arguments.

## 1. Background: Locality and (Hidden-Variable) Realism

Bell’s argument builds on the EPR paradox (1935) and Bohm’s 1952
hidden-variable model. EPR showed that, under _Einstein locality_
(no faster-than-light influence), quantum mechanics must be
incomplete or involve “spooky” effects. Bohm’s theory added
hidden variables, but was explicitly nonlocal.

In 1964, Bell proved an inequality under the assumption of _local
causality_ (sometimes called "factorizability"). The inequality
conflicts with quantum predictions and experiments.

Traditionally, this was taken to rule out _local hidden-variable
theories_. Clauser & Shimony (1978) coined the term _local
realism_ as shorthand for the conjunction of _locality_ and
_realism_ (an objective underlying reality).

But what exactly counts as realism? Mermin equates it with
_outcome determinism_ (OD), the idea that every measurement has
a definite pre-existing value. Others define it more broadly as
the existence of objective properties. Crucially, OD is stronger
than needed — Bell's inequality follows from factorizability
(Bell locality) alone.

- Modern summaries state:
    - Bell’s theorem shows that the _conjunction_ of Bell
      locality and a certain realist premise (e.g., hidden
      variables or counterfactual definiteness) is incompatible
      with quantum mechanics.

## 2. Bell’s Theorem Implies: Locality Must Go

### Authors emphasizing that locality is the key assumption:

- _Tim Maudlin (2014)_ argues Bell’s theorem rules out
  _locality_, not realism. Bell made no assumption of determinism
  or hidden variables. Thus, the world is nonlocal.

- _Federico Laudisa (2013)_ says Bell's theorem has _nothing to
  do_ with realism. Realism cannot be experimentally refuted;
  only locality is ruled out.

- _Travis Norsen (2007)_ claims that all attempts to find
  a “realism” assumption in Bell's derivation fail. The phrase
  _"local realism"_ should be abandoned.

- _Wayne Myrvold (2016)_ argues that nonlocality is inherent in
  _any_ theory reproducing quantum statistics, even if it's not
  "action at a distance."

These views hold that the violation of Bell's inequality forces
us to abandon locality — that is, some kind of nonlocal influence
must exist.

## 3. Bell’s Theorem Implies: Realism (Hidden Variables) Must Go

### Authors emphasizing realism (not locality) is the extra
assumption:

- _Zukowski & Brukner (2015)_ argue that _every_ Bell inequality
  derivation requires an extra assumption beyond locality
  — namely hidden variables or counterfactual definiteness.

- Therefore, Bell's theorem doesn't refute locality _per se_, but
  rules out _local hidden-variable theories_. One can keep
  locality if one gives up realism.

- This view supports an operational interpretation of quantum
  mechanics — no underlying variables, no counterfactuals, just
  correlations.

- Some recent models even propose recovering locality by denying
  realism (e.g. denying unmeasured observables have values).

The idea here is that Bell's theorem challenges _classical
realism_, and that if we accept the full quantum description,
locality (as no-signaling) can be preserved.

## 4. The Problem of Terminology: What Does "Local Realism" Mean?

- Many authors agree that _"local realism"_ is ambiguous.

- In some versions, realism means outcome determinism; in others,
  it's a general belief in objective properties.

- Bell's inequality does not _require_ determinism — so giving up
  determinism doesn't save locality.

- In modern terms, the key assumption violated by quantum
  mechanics is _factorizability_, or _Bell-locality_ — not
  necessarily realism.

- Therefore, the phrase _"local realism is ruled out"_ is often
  misleading, unless precisely defined.

## 5. Summary of Arguments

- _No realism assumption needed_: Bell, Maudlin, Laudisa, and
  Norsen all argue that Bell’s theorem uses _only_ locality. So,
  Bell violations prove nonlocality.

- _Realism was assumed_: Zukowski, Brukner, and many in quantum
  foundations argue that Bell inequalities always assume
  something like hidden variables or counterfactual definiteness.

- _Most physicists favor keeping locality_: So, in practice, many
  interpret Bell’s theorem as a refutation of _realism_
  (understood as hidden variables or predetermined values).

- _Terminology warning_: The term “local realism” is widely used
  but often vague or inconsistent. A more precise phrasing
  focuses on Bell-locality and whatever extra assumptions (e.g.,
  counterfactual definiteness) go into a particular derivation.

## 6. Comparative Table of Views

| Author                        | Interpretation                             | Summary

| Tim Maudlin (2014)           | Bell rules out _locality_                  | Bell’s derivation uses no hidden variables or OD.
| Federico Laudisa (2013)      | Realism cannot be refuted by experiment    | Only Bell-locality is constrained.
| Travis Norsen (2007)         | "Local realism" is misleading              | No separate realism premise in Bell’s derivation.

## 7. Conclusion

- Bell’s theorem definitively rules out the compatibility of
  quantum mechanics with _Bell-locality_ (local
  causality/factorizability).
    - What one gives up — locality or realism — depends on
      interpretation.

- Some argue: _Bell's theorem => nonlocality is real_.
- Others argue: _Bell's theorem => no local hidden-variable realism_.

- There is no universal agreement, but it is clear that _some
  combination of assumptions — including locality and realism
  — must be abandoned_ to reconcile quantum predictions with
  classical intuitions.
