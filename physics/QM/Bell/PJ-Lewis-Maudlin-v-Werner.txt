### Bell's theorem, realism, and locality (2014)
    - PJ Lewis: http://philsci-archive.pitt.edu/11372/
    - (Opinion on the Maudlin v Werner disagreement)

    - Locality is an explicit _assumption_ in the proof of Bell’s
      theorem, and it is uncontroversial that one can EVADE
      Bell’s conclusion by postulating _non-locality_ in the
      world.
    - Bohm’s theory does this, as does GRW (postulates non-local
      dynamical law)

    - What is controversial is Maudlin’s claim that the _ONLY_
      way to evade Bell’s theorem is via non-locality:
        - this is the content of his claim that Bell’s theorem
          rules out locality.

    - To establish this, we must convince ourselves there are no
      other ways around Bell’s theorem.

    - In particular, Maudlin claims that _DENYING REALISM_ is not
      an option here.

    - Against this, Werner argues one can construct a local QM
      theory if one is happy to violate an assumption he calls
      ‘classicality’ or ‘realism’: where
        > ‘realism’ is the mathematical assumption that: ‘The
        > state space is a simplex’
            - aside: 
            - 'Lost causes' sayes QM state space is not simplex?
            - $MY_NOTES/physics/QM/EPR-lost-causes-Physics.txt
            - It is curious that Penrose is nearly saved by the
              fact that the state space in QM is not a `simplex`,
              and that the decomposition of a mixed state into
              pure states is not unique. 



    - He argues that operational QM violates an assumption of
      classicality/realism, and therefore gives a way to
      construct a local QM theory.

    - If Werner is right, then Bell’s theorem has an equal claim
      to _challenging realism_ as it does to _challenging
      locality_. (!!)

    - Therefore I think that one can take _anti-realism_ to be
      a potential lesson of Bell’s theorem in just the same sense
      that one can take _non-locality_ to be a potential lesson.

    - That is, there are accounts of QM that evade Bell by
      appealing to non-local causal mechanisms, and there are
      also accounts that evade Bell by denying our physical
      theories describe the micro-world.

    - Admittedly, the explanation appeals to the global state of
      the system, but this arguably requires non-separability
      (holism) rather than non-locality (Wallace and Timpson
      2010).
        - (!!! Interesting: the difference between
            - 'holism' / 'non-separability' ... and ...
            - 'non-locality')

    - ... but this means there is no need for the vast conspiracy
      of the _common cause_ approach: the particles have to
      interact with the devices that measure them anyway, so
      there are no new causal links, just an unexpected direction
      for some of the links.

    - Admittedly, _backwards causation_ is conceptually
      problematic, and there is no well-developed theory along
      these lines in existence yet.

    - But if it can be made to work, the retrocausal model
      provides a clearly realist and clearly causally local
      account of quantum phenomena.
        - If it is tenable, the retrocausal approach raises the
          possibility that the lesson of Bell’s theorem is that:
        > effects can precede their causes.

    - So I think its too soon to say what the lesson of Bell’s
      theorem is. All the models of quantum phenomena presented
      here have their attractions, but also their weaknesses,
      weaknesses that may prove fatal.

    - In the end, it may be that the lesson of Bell’s theorem is
      that the world is causally non-local.
    - Or the lesson  may be that is that measurements have
      multiple equally real outcomes.
    - Or it may be that effects can come before their causes.
    - Or it may even be that no description of the quantum world
      can be given — although this latter conclusion seems to me
      to be a last resort.

    - In any event, the import of Bell’s theorem is far from
      a settled matter.
    - What Bell did is to demonstrate what QM cannot be:
        - it CANNOT be a theory that satisfies ALL the
          assumptions of his theorem.
        - Something has to give — but what precisely has to be
          given up will have to await future research. (!!)
