## 1. Your chain of logic, with the key correction


### Step 1: Locality + perfect anti-correlation → predetermined outcomes?

1a. Assume locality.

- Roughly, the measurement performed on one photon cannot
  influence the outcome of a distant measurement in the relevant
  way.

- For example, Alice measures her photon and Bob measures his
  photon. Locality requires Alice's outcome not to depend on
  Bob's freely chosen measurement setting, and vice versa. Bell's
  precise formulation is more technical than this, but this
  captures the central idea.

1b. We observe perfect anti-correlation for a singlet state.

If Alice and Bob measure spin along the same axis, their results are always opposite:

A = −B

For photon polarisation, the analogous statement applies to appropriately chosen polarisation measurements, although the singlet-state example is most naturally formulated using spin-½ particles.

1c. Does 1a + 1b imply hidden variables?

Not quite as stated. Here is the subtlety.

Suppose Alice and Bob measure along the same axis. If Alice obtains +1, Bob must obtain −1, and vice versa.

Now suppose Alice can choose between different measurement settings, and Bob can independently choose between those settings too. Assume perfect anti-correlation for each common setting and that locality holds.

Then, if we want to explain the perfect correlations through local causal influences, Bob's outcome cannot be caused by Alice's distant measurement choice or outcome. Likewise, Alice's outcome cannot be caused by Bob's distant measurement.

The natural conclusion is that the relevant outcomes must already be fixed by something in their shared past, or at least be determined by the local physical circumstances together with the measurement setting.

That is the idea behind Bell's argument for predetermined outcomes.

However, this conclusion relies on more than the bare statement of locality and anti-correlation. It involves assumptions about the independence of the measurement choices, the possible causal explanations of the correlations, and the framework in which outcomes are described.

So I would revise your 1c to:

Locality + perfect anti-correlation + suitable causal and measurement-independence assumptions → predetermined outcomes for the relevant measurements, within the framework Bell is considering.

This is the sense in which perfect correlations can force determinism upon a theory that is assumed to be local.

## 2. Step 2: Predetermined outcomes + locality → contradiction with quantum statistics

This is the heart of Bell's theorem.

Suppose we have a hidden variable, λ, that specifies the relevant physical state of the photon pair. The outcomes can be written as:

A = A(a, λ)

B = B(b, λ)

Here, a and b are Alice's and Bob's measurement settings.

Locality means Alice's outcome function does not depend on b, and Bob's outcome function does not depend on a.

For simplicity, let the outcomes be either +1 or −1.

For a particular λ, the model assigns outcomes to the possible settings. In this sense, the relevant outcomes are predetermined.

Now the crucial question is: can such a model reproduce the observed quantum correlations for different measurement angles?

Bell's answer is no, provided the model satisfies the relevant locality and measurement-independence assumptions.

## The logical structure of Bell's theorem

Local hidden-variable model

Outcomes determined locally by the setting and λ

Bell inequality

A mathematical constraint on the correlations any such model can produce

Quantum predictions and experiments violate the inequality

Conclusion: at least one assumption must fail

For example, one version of Bell's inequality is the CHSH inequality:

|S| ≤ 2

Quantum mechanics predicts values up to 2√2 for suitable measurement settings, and experiments observe violations of the local bound.

The contradiction is not that hidden variables are mathematically impossible. It is that local hidden-variable models satisfying the relevant assumptions cannot reproduce the quantum correlations.

A non-local hidden-variable theory, such as Bohmian mechanics, is possible. Standard quantum mechanics also reproduces the correlations without using predetermined outcomes for every possible measurement.

## 3. Does the contradiction show that locality is false?

This is where Maudlin's interpretation becomes important.

Your step 2c says:

> A contradiction somewhere along the line. (ie implies 1a or 1c is incorrect??)

That is essentially right, but it needs to be more precise.

Bell's theorem rules out the conjunction of the assumptions used to derive the inequality. In a common formulation, those include:

* Local causality, or Bell-locality.

* Measurement independence: the measurement settings are statistically independent of the relevant hidden variables.

* The usual assumptions about outcomes and probabilities, including the applicability of the experimental statistics.

Experiments then establish that the observed correlations violate the inequality.

Therefore, at least one of the assumptions must be abandoned, or the mathematical and empirical framework must be revised.

Maudlin argues that the appropriate conclusion is that nature is non-local. His reasoning is that we should take the experimental settings to be appropriately independent and accept the observed quantum statistics. Given those commitments, local causality must fail.

This is a serious and influential interpretation of Bell's theorem. But it is worth distinguishing two claims:

1. Bell's theorem proves that the quantum correlations cannot be explained by Bell-local theories satisfying the other assumptions.

2. Bell's theorem, by itself, proves that nature is non-local, independently of any interpretative or philosophical commitments.

The first is the standard mathematical result. The second is stronger and depends on how we interpret the assumptions and what alternatives we are prepared to reject.

Maudlin defends the stronger conclusion because he regards Bell-locality as the assumption that must give way. Other interpretations explore alternatives involving measurement independence, the causal structure of the theory, or other assumptions.

## 4. Are hidden variables, determinism and counterfactual definiteness equivalent?

Your note groups these together, but they are not quite interchangeable.

- HIDDEN VARIABLES            
    - Additional variables, λ, intended to specify or help
      explain the physical state and correlations.      
- DETERMINISM                 
    - Given the complete physical state and relevant conditions,
      the outcome is fixed.                       
- PREDETERMINED OUTCOMES      
    - The outcomes for the relevant measurements are fixed before
      those measurements occur.                  
- COUNTERFACTUAL DEFINITENESS 
    - Roughly, the idea that outcomes for measurements not
      actually performed can meaningfully be specified. 

These concepts overlap in some Bell arguments, but they are not synonymous.

In particular, a hidden-variable theory need not be deterministic. It could assign probabilities to outcomes even given λ.

And counterfactual definiteness is often used as shorthand in discussions of Bell's theorem, but it can be misleading. Bell's theorem does not simply start by assuming that every unperformed measurement has a definite result and then show that assumption is false.

The more careful argument is that, under Bell's locality assumptions and perfect correlations, the outcomes for the relevant settings can be shown to be predetermined in the local model. Bell inequalities then follow from the structure of that model.

This is why step 1c is important: determinism is not simply an independent assumption that Bell inserts at the start of his argument. In the perfect-correlation argument, it can be derived from locality and the correlations, given the additional assumptions.

## 5. The cleanest way to rewrite your chain

I would formulate it as follows:

1. Assume Bell-locality and measurement independence.

2. Assume the relevant perfect correlations observed for suitably chosen measurements.

3. Under these assumptions, the local model must assign predetermined outcomes to the relevant possible measurements.

4. Predetermined local outcomes obey Bell inequalities.

5. Quantum mechanics predicts, and experiments confirm, correlations that violate those inequalities.

6. Therefore, Bell-locality, measurement independence, and the other assumptions used in the argument cannot all be true.

7. If we retain measurement independence and the other standard assumptions, we must abandon Bell-locality.

That last step captures the central reasoning behind Maudlin's position.

One final nuance: Bell's theorem is not merely a result about classical physics being unable to explain quantum correlations. It is a result about a particular class of theories that satisfy a precise locality condition. The class includes deterministic and stochastic hidden-variable models. Quantum correlations violate the constraint imposed on that class.

The main correction to your original chain is therefore this: perfect anti-correlation helps derive predetermined outcomes within a local model; the varying-angle correlations then show that no such model can reproduce the full quantum statistics. The conclusion is not that hidden variables in general are impossible, but that the relevant local explanation fails.
