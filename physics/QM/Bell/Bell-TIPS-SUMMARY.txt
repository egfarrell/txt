### Determinism/CFD: an assumption? (important)

    - Bell did not view determinism / conterfactual definitness
      as an arbitrary POSTULATE; he believed he had DERIVED it
      using EPR's argument.
    - In the intro of the 1964 paper, Bell references EPR:
        1. QM predicts that if Alice & Bob choose the SAME axis
        (a⃗ = b⃗), outcomes are 100% anti-correlated: `(A = −B)`.
        2. Alice & Bob make their measurements at spacelike
        separation. Under causal locality, Alice's choice of
        setting cannot instantaneously affect Bob's particle.
        3. THEREFORE, Bob's result must have already been
        PREDETERMINED before Alice made her choice.
    - As Bell later put it in `Bertlmann's Socks...etc` (1981)
        > "It's important to realize that in these experiments,
        > we are not just ASSUMING determinism. EPR showed that
        > locality plus the quantum predictions for parallel
        > analyzers ENTAILS determinism."
        - See above!
            - "1.  Locality + empirical anti-correlation =>
              Hidden Variables (or CFD?!)"
    - Because Bell considered determinism/CFD a LOGICAL
      CONSEQUENCE of locality applied to the singlet state, he
      viewed the subsequent inequality as testing LOCALITY
      itself.

### "Local causality" is important
    - Why Maudlin thinks “realism” is a red herring
    - His argument (simplified) goes like this:
        - Take the EPR setup with perfect anti-correlations. If
          Alice measures spin and can predict Bob’s outcome with
          certainty, and if nothing Alice does can causally
          affect Bob (locality), then Bob’s outcome must already
          be fixed.
        - That conclusion does not come from realism — it comes
          from locality + the observed correlations.
        - Thus, for Maudlin, introducing λ is not an extra
          assumption. It’s just making explicit what locality
          already commits you to.
    - So when people say “drop realism”, Maudlin replies:
        - you can’t — locality already implies it in these
          circumstances.
    - Bell’s `local causality` supports Maudlin
        - Maudlin leans heavily on Bell’s later notion of local
          causality, which says:
        - Once you specify everything physically relevant in the
          past light cone of an event, spacelike-separated events
          should not further affect its probabilities.
        - This principle is purely causal, not metaphysical.
    - Given local causality, Bell-style factorization follows.
      Violations of Bell inequalities therefore mean local
      causality is false — full stop.
    - From this perspective, saying “quantum mechanics is local
      but non-realistic” is, for Maudlin, a verbal maneuver that
      avoids facing the causal violation.
    - The opposing camp (Jarrett, Shimony, Wiseman, etc.)
      responds:
        - Bell locality is stronger than relativistic locality.
        - QFT respects:
            - Lorentz invariance
            - microcausality
            - no superluminal signalling
        - Yet it violates Bell inequalities.
        - So if you identify “locality” with Bell’s
          factorization, you are building realism into the
          definition of locality. On that view, Maudlin is
          redefining locality rather than discovering its
          failure.
    - This camp says:
        - Nature is non-Bell-local, but not necessarily nonlocal
          in spacetime.
    - The real disagreement (the important part)
        - The dispute is not about the mathematics or
          experiments.
        - It’s about this question:
        - Does locality ALONE, properly understood, force outcome
          definiteness?
        - Maudlin: yes
        - Others: no: only a classical notion of completeness
          does
        - So when Maudlin says “Bell proved nonlocality”, he
          means `nonlocal causation`, not signalling, not
          relativistic violation, but real causal dependence
          across spacelike intervals.

            - so `c` would be

        - You are comparing two different formalisms!

### Maudlin: Quantum non-locality and Relativity
    - $MY_BOOKS/Physics/QM/Maudlin-quantum-nonlocality.pdf
    - (Write out page 19 - basically about locality being
      violated anyway, regardless of realism/non-realism)
    - 2023: A final remark on Bell’s theorem is needed.
        - Bell himself derived the result as part of an
          examination of 'local hidden-variables' theories.
    - Such theories attempt to eliminate the `stochastic element`
      of orthodox QM by adding _extra parameters_ to the usual
      quantum formalism, parameters whose values determine the
      results of the experiments.
    - Therefore Bell’s results are sometimes portrayed as proof
      that local deterministic hidden-variables theories are
      impossible.
    - This is a misleading claim.
        - It suggests the violation of the inequalities may be
          recovered if one just gives up determinism or hidden
          variables. [logic-link]
    - But as we have seen, the ONLY ASSUMPTION needed to derive
      the INEQUALITIES is that:

### The Assumptions of Bell’s Proof (2015)
    - Roderich Tumulka
        - $MY_BOOKS/Physics/papers/2015-Tumulka-Assumptions-Bells-Proof.pdf
    - It is widely agreed that Bell's theorem is an important
      result, but there is much disagreement about what exactly
      Bell's theorem shows.
    - It is agreed that Bell derived a contradiction with
      experimental facts from some list of assumptions, thus
      showing that at least one of the ASSUMPTIONS must be wrong;
      but there is disagreement about what the assumptions were
      that went into the argument.
    - In this paper, I make a few points in order to help clarify
      the situation. (Tumulka then defines 4 types of realism:
      R1 - R4)

    - NOTE: `R2` is: The outcome of every experiment is
      pre-determined by some (“hidden”) variable `λ`.

    - The first proof (1964), didnt assume `R2` either. Yet it
      can easily appear as if `R2` was being assumed, for the
      following reason. The proof has two parts:
        - part 1 derives `R2` from the assumption of locality
          (ah!)
        - and part 2 derives Bell’s inequality from `R2` and
          locality.

    - Any reader missing part one will conclude that Bell assumed
      both `R2` and locality. And part 1 may be easy to miss,
      because it is short, and because all the mathematical work
      goes into part 2. And because part 1 was actually developed
      by EPR in their 1935 paper, not by Bell.

    - Bell complained later that people missed part 1 of the
      proof:
        > My own first paper on this subject (ie [3]) starts with
        > a summary of the EPR argument FROM locality TO
        > deterministic hidden variables.

        > But the commentators have almost universally reported
        > that it begins with deterministic hidden variables.

    - If my claims are right, one should conclude there is NO
      ASSUMPTION OF REALISM that enters the proof of Bell’s
      theorem next to the assumption of locality, and therefore
      we dont have the CHOICE between the two options of
      abandoning realism and abandoning locality, but instead,
      that we must abandon locality.

    - One should further conclude that the widespread statement
      that “Bell’s theorem refutes local realism” is misleading,
      and Bell’s theorem simply refutes locality. More detailed
      discussions of this point can be found in [17, 11, 14]

    - Clearly, Bell’s proof makes use of the Kolmogorov axioms
      for deriving Bells inequality. Its been suggested that some
      of the Kolmogorov axioms might be violated in nature, so
      that locality may hold true even though the Bell inequality
      is violated.

    - Specifically, it has been suggested that probabilities can
      be negative , or that additivity (1) can fail.

    > our measuring instruments were distorted by motion in such

    > reference things do go faster than light…. Behind the

    > a deeper level which is not Lorentz invariant…

    > [This] pre-Einstein position of Lorentz and Poincare´,
    > Larmor and Fitzgerald, was perfectly coherent, and is not
    > inconsistent with relativity theory. The idea that there is

      seems to presuppose that we are dealing with theories
      positing exclusively local beables.50 It can be stretched

      philosophers, but by physicists interested in addressing

### Bricmont (Bell paper)
    - $MY_BOOKS/Physics/QM/Bricmont-Bell-Meaning-Wavefunction.pdf
    - What is the conclusion of all this? We started from one
      crucial assumption: absence of "communication" between the
      two persons once they are asked the questions. I will from
      now on revert to a less anthropomorphic language and call
      this assumption "locality" - assume that there is no causal
      connection whatsoever between the two wings of the
      experiment. Then, we are led to a contradiction, so this
      assumption must be dropped.

    - Its important to understand the logic of the argument: the
      perfect correlations plus the absence of communication
      (i.e. locality) between the two wings of the experiments,
      leads us to postulate the existence of the variables vi(α)
      (which are also "hidden" - see the discussion in section
      2).
    - However, merely assuming that those variables exist leads
      to a contradiction with the experimental results obtained
      when different questions are asked.
    - To put it simply: locality plus perfect (anti)correlation
      implies hidden variables; However, the latter plus
      statistics when different angles are measured implies
      a contradiction.
    - Both the perfect correlations and the statistics for
      different angles are empirical results; the theorem is
      a theorem, namely a logical deduction; the only assumption
      was the lack of "communication", or locality. Hence,
      locality has to be given up, period.

    - It is not, as is often assumed, a concern for realism, or
      for determinism or for hidden variables that is the source
      of the problem.
    - The reason for this misunderstanding is probably
      historical: the first part of the argument (locality
      implies hidden variables) goes back essentially to
      Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen in 1935. But they did not put
      it this way and were certainly not interested in showing
      that the world is not local.
    - On the contrary, they assumed (as if it was obvious) that
      the world is local and concluded that QM is incomplete,
      namely that hidden variables must exist.
      - As a logical reasoning, it was perfect.

    - But Bell's theorem was proven only in 1964, almost thirty
      years later. It showed that the hidden variables, whose
      existence was implied by the "obvious" assumption of
      locality made by EPR, led to a logical contradiction.
    - However, between 1935 and 1964, the majority of the physics
      community became convinced that Bohr had satisfactorily
      answered Einstein. I shall not discuss here what Bohr
      really had in mind, and how the majority of physicists
      actually read him. That is a fascinating problem for
      historians of science.

    - The upshot is that Bell (who of course knew and understood
      the EPR argument) took it as his starting point and
      PROCEEDED TO DISPROVE THE EXISTENCE OF THOSE HIDDEN
      VARIABLES, HENCE OF LOCALITY.
    - But most of his readers did not understand the full logic
      of his argument and thought that Bell had merely refuted
      the existence of local hidden variables; since the EPR
      argument had simply been forgotten and since all concern
      for hidden variables were by then regarded as proof of
      a "lamentable addiction to metaphysics", Bell's result
      looked neither particularly spectacular nor particularly
      disturbing.

    - That is, in a nutshell, why the majority of the physics
      community underestimated the significance of one of the
      most startling results in the history of science.

### Bell Inequality
    - In Bell's derivation, he posits a presumptive hidden
      variable, `λ(x,t)`.
    - In this view, one member of the twin particles has position
      `+x` at time `t`, while the other has `-x` at time `t`.
    - But note that Bell blithely adopts a common master clock,
      `t`, so that `λ(x,t)` can be algebraically canceled out by
      `λ(-x,t)`, regardless of the function `λ(•)`.
    - If you appreciate that a gravitational gradient perturbs
      timekeeping so that the particles speeding off in opposite
      directions age at their own idiosyncratic rates, then one
      can no longer algebraically cancel out `λ`.  That is, the
      derivation of Bell's Inequality breaks down; the
      presumptive hidden variable λ(x,t) remains present.
    - Indeed one can say THE HIDDEN VARIABLE IS TIME ITSELF. That
      is, each particle in reality ages according to its own
      local clock, rather than being governed by a common master
      clock.
    - In Aspect's experiment, `λ(x,t)` could be Maxwell's

      rotating vectors.
      - But recall that photons traversing a gravitational
        gradient gain or lose energy and thus change their

      - The two photons are thus represented by sinusoids which

      - They will have a residual nonzero "beat frequency" which

        cancellation of `λ(x,t)` midway through his derivation.

### Last lecture comment by John Bell:
    - we need something where the correlations are "natural"
      _WITHOUT_ implying determinism (cos implying determinism
      leads you back to non-locality via Bells theorem (??))
    - If you're only interested in a limited part of the world,
      in QM you use a so-called desnity matrix, which kind of
      averages over the rest of the world. And then the density
      matrix has an equation evolving for itself and not
      mentioning the rest of the world.
    - But Einstein gave us a way of "switching off" the rest of
      the world outside the light cone. You could say that yes,
      the rest of the world is going to have an effect, but that
      effect will not arrive before light could propagate. So
      that was a way of dividing the world into bits that are
      relevant and bits that are not relevant. And that we don't
      have anymore (with QM)
    - Interesting!
        - Questioner: Does it mean that you say that Relativity
          and QM aren't compatible?
        - John Bell: No... no I can't say that because I think
          somebody will find a way of seeing they are compatible.
          But I haven't seen it yet. For me it's very hard to put
          them togoether. But I think somebody will put them
          together, and will just see my imagination was too
          limited.
    - Hidden Variables:
        - Questioner: ... there are no hidden variables?
        - John Bell: Well I don't know that there are no hidden
          variables. What we said there was that _hidden
          variables cannot restore locality_. But theres still
          the consideration I began with, that the classical
          physicists still thinks that if repeating the
          experiment gives different answers he has not quite
          repeated the experiment. And... you can have
          _non-local_ determinisim. Non-local hidden variables.
          So I could imagine a situation where we do have hidden
          variables, we do have again determinism, but not
          predictability. Because we wont be smart enough to get
          things with sufficient precision. But we'd again have
          determinism.. but it'd be non-local. We'd see that in
          some way distant things were connected, but not in
          a way that you could get hold of to send messages.
    - John: I believe QM is compatible with determinism and
      indeterminism. With both(!) But I think we're stuck with
      non-locality. I don't know any version of locality that
      works with QM. Whether we're stuck with determinism or
      indeterminism is another question. I know that if you
      didn't worry about Lorentz invariance, you can make
      explicit deterministic models which agree with all the
      experiments. But they're not Lorentz invariant, they have
      an ether. And that's hard to swallow. _It may be that
      Lorentz invariance with QM is incompatbile with
      determinism_. But I don't know that. It's a possibility.
