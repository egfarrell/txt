- There's another feature emerging clearly from these physics
  axioms and it appears from the repeated use of the word
  `measurement`. 

- From this you may think that physics has become restricted to
  trifling laboratory operations. 
    - Why all this interest in the laboratory? 
    - What about the big world outside the laboratory? Are
      measurements going on there? 
    - If not, we have nothing to say about what is happening
      there. Moreover, this concept of measurement becomes
      slippery on reflection.

- ... this approach involves the division of the world into
  a quantum world on one hand, and a classical world on the
  other, a division which is far from clear, which I term the
  "shifty split".

- The root of the problem that led to the _retreat from realism_
  is the _SUPERPOSITION_ principle of QM.

- If `A` and `B` are two possible states of a quantum system then
  you can form a whole family of states `αA + βB` where `α` and
  `β` are complex numbers. 
    - Now this combination is not a trivial one; in particular
      it's NOT the sort of combination you meet in probability
      theory where you can combine two probabilities into
      a single probability distribution. 
    - It does not mean either `A` or `B`; in some sense it means
      BOTH `A` and `B` at the same time and that is evidenced
      from the existence of INTERFERENCE phenomena.

- If one of those states is a plane wave in one direction and the
  other a plane wave in another direction, then such
  a superposition has _interference minima_; 
    - because particles that could arrive with either `A` or `B`
      cannot(? can) arrive with `αA + βB`. 
    - In some sense the two states exist simultaneously and not
      in a trivial way, because of interference.

- But superposition is absolutely essential in atoms.

- What could it possibly mean, when `A` and `B` are large systems
  such as tables or chairs.

- ... he coupled this into an apparatus of some kind which checks
  if the decay of atoms has not occurred, and that apparatus is
    - thrown into a state `T` associated with `A` 
    - or state `F` associated with `B`: 
        - `αAF + βBT`. 
    - `T` and `F` are two different states of the apparatus, and
      in fact they are Thin and Fat states of Schroodinger's cat.
    - This apparatus contains Schroodinger's cat and in such
      a way that it will be fed or hungry according to whether
      a certain atom has or has not decayed yet.

- The QM analysis of this situation depends on where you put the
  "shifty split". 
    - If the split is put here (1) with the cat "on our side", so
      to speak, there is no particular problem: 
        - the cat is like a classical one that is thin or fat;
    - but if the split is put here (2), 
        - this state is described by the superposition of the
          thin cat and fat cat; 
        - both are present in this case, although the cat knows
          very well whether it is either thin or fat. 
        - From the cat's point of view we have either 
            - this (AT) _OR_ that (BF), 
            - whereas from the wavefunction point of view we have 
            - this (AT) _AND_ that (BF), 
        - and the step from ‘and' to ‘or' is a non-trivial one
          because interference phenomena disappeared.

- ... so-called electron

- Imagine an experiment in which a so-called "electron" is passed
  through a small aperture and falls upon some kind of detector,
  say a photographic plate. 
    - Now, we know that the dynamics of this situation simply
      cannot be described in terms of classical particles moving
      along classical trajectories; we have to solve here
      Schrodinger's wave equation. 
    - But when this wave impinges upon this detector, what we see
      is not in the least like a wave: we see isolated spots, one
      at a time, so this picture builds up over the course of
      time, ... as the experiment is repeated. 
    - We see spots not waves, and here you see this terrible
      division of the world into a wavy part and a spotty part,
      and this awkward shifty division of the world has to be
      made. 

- So this is the key problem of QM, the ambiguity: 
    - Nobody knows what QM says exactly about any situation, for
      nobody knows where the boundary really is between wavy
      quantum systems and the world of particular events. 
        - This is the key problem and, as we know, it does not
          matter at all in practice.  (???)

- I think that what QM needs is not philosophy, but the _missing
  equation_, which defines mathematically where the split is or
  replaces the need for a split.

- I'm not going to describe these second level difficulties.
    - They arise when trying to make a relativistic QM. 
    - You find you're obliged not to start with a particle,
      a classical particle, but with a classical field. 
        - A bit like what Flournoy said about QFT... everything
          in it is a "field", no particles as such...
    - This classical field has infinitely many degrees of
      freedom.
    - And you find when doing physical calculations, the result
      is often given by an infinite sum, and the trouble is that
      this infinite sum often diverges, so the result seems to be
      infinite. 
    - Physicists have developed a way of delicately balancing
      these infinities against each another in order to extract
      finite results for experimentally observable quantities.
      This is called the _renormalization_ scheme. 

- Before Einstein, people had devised a way of understanding why
  you can't detect the motion of the ether, e.g. the Michelson
  Morley experiment. 
    - Nevertheless the theory these people had envisaged an ether
      in its fundamental construction. 
    - And of course all coordinate systems were not equal, there
      was one which was in some sense fundamental. 
    - And in this theory there would be such a fundamental frame
      because when you define an _average_ (?) over a certain
      region of space, you pick out a certain frame as
      a preferred frame.

- Moreover, if you try to make the theory invariant by making
  this averaging over the region go to a point, then you find
  that in QFT, operators at a point are terrible. 
- In particular, the quantum fluctuations would be infinite. 
    - And what does it mean to narrow down the fluctuations of
      something which is infinitely fluctuating? 
- When you do that, you must feel that you are doing rather
  serious damage to the system, not just treating it rather
  gently. 

- So one can anticipate trouble when you try to make this theory
  Lorentz invariant, not just for all practical purposes but
  deeply, in the sense of Einstein, eliminating entirely any
  privileged reference system from the theory.
