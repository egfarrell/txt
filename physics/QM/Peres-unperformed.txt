### Abstract
    - This paper discusses correlations between the results of
      measurements performed on physical systems which are widely
      separated, but have interacted in the past. It is shown
      that quantum correlations are stronger than classical
      correlations. 
    - This property leads to the following paradox, known as
      Bell’s theorem: Let us assume that the outcome of an
      experiment performed on one of the systems is independent
      of the choice of the experiment performed on the other.
    - Now, let us try to imagine the results of alternative
      measurements, which could have been performed on the same
      systems instead of the actual measurements. 
    - Then there is no way of contriving these hypothetical
      results so that they will satisfy all the quantum
      correlations with the results of the actual measurements.
      However, the weaker classical correlations can be
      satisfied.


# Summary of below
    - Bell's theorem identifies an impossible trio: 
        - you cannot simultaneously have locality (distant
          outcomes are independent), counterfactual definiteness
          (unperformed measurements have pre-existing values),
          and agreement with quantum predictions. Experiment
          confirms QM, so one of the first two must go.
    - The "contradiction" lives outside QM, not inside it. 
        - QM is internally consistent. The tension only appears
          when you try to *explain* its correlations using
          classical assumptions about pre-existing hidden values
          — that's where Bell inequalities get derived, and then
          violated.
    - Peres' core move is identifying which assumption to cut.
        - The step "imagine the results of measurements that
          weren't taken" is where the trouble sneaks in. He
          argues this assumption — counterfactual definiteness
          — is simply illegitimate, not forced on us by logic or
          physics.
    - The quantum surprise is that local randomness and global
      structure coexist. 
        - Each detector locally produces a completely random `±1`
          outcome, yet the joint correlations across both
          detectors follow the precise, smooth function `−α·β`. 
        - Classically, randomness and strong structure trade off
          against each other — quantum mechanics refuses that
          bargain.
    - Peres vs. Bell/Bohmians is a genuine fork, not a mistake by
      either side. 
        - Drop counterfactual definiteness → keep locality, no
          paradox (Peres). 
        - Keep realism → drop locality, accept a nonlocal world
          (Bohm/Maudlin). Both are internally consistent; the
          disagreement is philosophical, about what a physical
          explanation *must* provide.
    - The title's irony is the whole point. 
        - Peres uses counterfactual reasoning throughout — he
          just refuses to treat counterfactual values as *real*.
        - The distinction is between using "what would have
          happened" as an analytical tool versus assuming those
          hypothetical outcomes physically exist. The former is
          fine; the latter is where Bell's contradiction is born.

## User Questions

- Is Peres just restating Bell, or adding something new?
- What exactly is the “contradiction” in Bell/Peres?
- Why do the two averages (local randomness + strong correlation) feel surprising?
- Can Bell’s result be resolved by dropping counterfactual definiteness instead of locality?

## 1. What Bell’s Theorem Actually Shows

Bell proves that you cannot have all three:

1. Locality  
   - Outcomes at A do not depend on settings at B

2. Counterfactual definiteness (CFD)  
   - All possible measurement outcomes exist (even unperformed ones)

3. Agreement with quantum predictions  
   - Correlations: E(α, β) = − α · β

→ These three together lead to a contradiction (Bell inequality violation)

## 2. What the “Contradiction” Is

The contradiction is NOT inside quantum mechanics.

It is:

- Assume:
  - Locality
  - Pre-existing values for all measurements

- Then:
  - You derive Bell inequalities

- But:
  - Quantum mechanics (and experiment) violate those inequalities

→ So:
  The same system must both satisfy and violate Bell inequalities

## 3. Where the Contradiction Comes From

The crucial step is:

> “Let us imagine the results of alternative measurements…”

This assumes:

- For each run j:
  - r_j(α), r_j(α′), r_j(β), r_j(β′) all exist simultaneously

This is counterfactual definiteness.

## 4. Peres’ Key Move

Peres identifies that step as the problem.

He says:

> The contradiction only arises because we assume unperformed measurements have results.

So he rejects:

- Counterfactual definiteness

→ Conclusion:
  “Unperformed experiments have no results”

## 5. The Two Key Equations

### (1) Local average

- `(1/N) Σ_j r_jα(βγδ…) = 0`
    - Each side alone is random
    - Independent of distant settings
    - Implies no signalling

### (2) Correlation

- `(1/N) Σ_j r_jα(βγδ…) r_jβ(αγδ…) = − α · β`
    - Strong quantum correlation
    - Violates Bell inequalities

## 6. Why This Is Surprising

- Surprising because both are true at once:
    - Local outcomes → completely random  
    - Joint outcomes → highly STRUCTURED

- Classically, you expect:
    - Random → weak correlation  
    - Strong correlation → underlying structure

- Quantum mechanics gives:
    - Random locally  
    - Strong correlation globally  

→ This combination is impossible in classical local theories

## 7. Correlation vs Dependence

- Important distinction:
    - Correlation = statistical (many runs)
    - Dependence = structural (how outcomes are generated)

- Single run:
    - gives `±1` at `A` and `B`
    - shows no correlation by itself

- But:
    - Outcome at `A` may depend on `β` (distant setting)
    → Nonlocality is about dependence, not visible single-run correlation

## 8. Peres vs Bell/Maudlin

### Bell/Maudlin view

- Counterfactual definiteness is necessary
    - Bell ⇒ locality must fail

→ Conclusion:
    - The world is nonlocal

### Peres view

- Counterfactual definiteness is optional
    - Reject assigning values to unperformed measurements

→ Conclusion:
    - No contradiction, no need for nonlocal realism

## 9. Two Possible “Resolutions”

- Bell forces a choice:
    - Option A — Peres
        - Drop: Counterfactual definiteness
        - Keep: Locality (operationally, no signalling)

    - Option B — Bohmian / your suggestion
        - Keep: Realism (values exist)
        - Drop: Locality 
        - → Explicit nonlocal theory

## 10. Why the Disagreement Exists

- Key question:
    > Do unperformed measurements need definite values?

- Bell/Maudlin: YES (required for explanation)
- Peres: NO (illegitimate assumption)

## 11. The Role of −α · β

- This is the crucial empirical fact:
    - Smooth angular dependence
    - Violates Bell inequalities

- Classical models can produce correlations, but NOT this
  function.

## 12. Important Clarification

- The two equations:
    - Are NOT contradictory
    - Are both empirical facts

- The contradiction arises only when you try to explain them using:
    - local + predefined values

## 13. The “Irony” in Peres’ Title

- Title:
    > “Unperformed experiments have no results”
    - But he still:
    - talks about unperformed experiments

- Resolution:
    - He distinguishes:
    - Using counterfactuals as a _TOOL_ ✔  
    - Treating them as _real physical values_ ✘  

- So:
    > You can think about them, but they don’t correspond to
    > reality

## 14. Deep Insight

- Peres’ position:
    - Counterfactual reasoning = allowed  
    - Counterfactual VALUES = not real  

## 15. Final Summary

- Bell shows:
    - You cannot have:
        - locality
        - predefined outcomes
        - quantum correlations

- Peres concludes:
    > The mistake is assuming predefined outcomes

- Alternative view:
    > The mistake is assuming locality

- Both are logically consistent — the difference is philosophical.

## 16. One-Line Takeaway

- The tension in QM is not in the equations, but in:
    > whether unperformed measurements should be treated as
    > having real outcomes
