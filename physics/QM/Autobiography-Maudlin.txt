- In the fall of 1979 I was a senior in college. Like all science
  buffs before the internet, I had a subscription to Scientific
  American, and would read it cover to cover.
- That was prehistoric compared to now, but it meant I kept up
  with a wide range of scientific disciplines. But physics was
  the most important to me.

- That fall, they published "Quantum Theory and Reality" by
  Bernard d'Espagnat:
    - $MY_NOTES/physics/QM/dEspagnat-article-Scientific-American.txt
    - $MY_BOOKS/Physics/QM/Maudlin-autobiography-and-dEspagnat.pdf

- When I started reading I was astonished and shaken. I was
  living in a dorm room with 2 roommates, and if you were on the
  street outside you could see through the windows, and my
  roommates saw me holding this magazine and pacing around and
  around completely absorbed, pacing around the little coffee
  table.

- And they could tell, from outside, that something very weird
  was going on, just from my concentration and pacing. That was
  the day I learned of Bell's theorem.

- It's no exaggeration to say that article shaped the course of
  my entire intellectual life.
- (That issue of Scientific American had an illustration of some
  dung beetles rolling a rather large ball of dung, a detail
  I think many of us who found out about Bell's theorem in this
  way tend to recall.)

- No faculty at Yale at the time did philosophy of physics. The
  philosopher of science, Nancy Maull, did not specialize in
  physics, so I was on my own.

- By the next year I was doing graduate work in the History and
  Philosophy of Science at the University of Pittsburgh (largely
  because that was the only place that offered me full financial
  support).

- Pitt had some splendid philosophers of science and excellent
  historians of science, and top-notch philosophers of physics,
  but as it happened the philosophers of physics all specialized
  in spacetime theory rather than QM.Carl Hempel was still.
  A delightful gnome in his 80s straight from the Vienna circle
  who would tell us stories of Carnap and Schlick and Neurath. He
  was a very sweet shrunken wrinkled man with a gleam in his eye,
  like a pre-Yoda Yoda.

- Back in 1980 the distinct sub-specialty that would become
  philosophy of physics was in its infancy, and most philosophers
  of physics came there by way of first learning logic and other
  formal methods and then GR, which is a conceptually clear
  theory that has a sharp mathematical formulation. Quantum
  theory was not much studied, and when it was, it was a complete
  mess.

- If you compare Hilary Putnam’s work on quantum theory at the
  time with David Malament’s, Clark Glymour’s, John Earman’s, or
  Larry Sklar’s work on space-time theory and GR,
  the contrast is stark. Malament, Glymour, Earman, and Sklar
  knew GR at the same technical level as the best physicists and
  worked directly with Bob Wald, Bob Geroch, and Bill Unruh. When
  we graduate students pressed Glymour, my dissertation advisor,
  to teach space-time theory, he arranged a full-year course. The
  first half was pure mathematics—tensor analysis on
  manifolds—and the second half covered GR itself. We read parts
  of Hawking and Ellis and all of Michael Friedman’s Foundations
  of Space-Time Theory, the first philosophy of physics book to
  use coordinate-free methods. That book, together with Geroch’s
  GR from A to B, which I still use when
  teaching, and Sklar’s Space, Time and Space-Time, eventually
  grew into my Philosophy of Physics: Space and Time. After
  decades of teaching and steadily removing coordinate-dependent
  exposition, no coordinates remained, and special relativity
  emerged as pure coordinate-free geometry.

- As young philosophers of physics, we learned GR with a rigor
  and mathematical clarity comparable to any physics department
  course, though we spent no time solving problems. As one
  quickly learns in foundations, problem solving—the “calculate”
  in “Shut Up and Calculate”—contributes little to genuine
  understanding. Physicists, by contrast, have calculation
  drilled into them and devote almost all their time to technique
  rather than foundations. This leads them to think that
  understanding comes only from years of calculation, like
  a baker learning by practice. Eventually she produces excellent
  bread even if she can't articulate exactly why it works.

- If a field adopts “Shut Up and Calculate,” it is unsurprising
  that the best people are the best calculators. Ed Witten,
  a brilliant mathematician who deserved the Fields Medal, has
  been called “the greatest mathematical physicist since Newton,”
  despite having produced no empirically verified physics.
  Philosophers of physics in the New York area once spent several
  hours with him discussing foundations, including Schrödinger’s
  cat. He was open and engaged, but concluded by saying: “I
  haven’t thought about this for 30 years. My job is to calculate
  wave functions.”

- Returning to my main story: when we pressed Glymour to teach
  quantum theory, the result was very different. Instead of
  a full-year course using advanced physics texts, it was
  a single semester based on philosophy journal articles. We read
  papers like “Is the Sum Rule Well Confirmed?”, studied
  Reichenbach’s common cause principle, and encountered some
  quantum logic.

- We learned Gleason’s theorem, non-distributive lattices, and
  the then-fashionable proposal to solve the measurement problem
  via superselection rules, promoted by Danieri, Loinger, and
  Prosperi, names that have since faded.

- I heard of “Bohm’s theory” but never saw an equation. I was
  told it worked but was adhoc, implausible, and relied on
  a contrived “quantum potential” to recover the right
  predictions using Newtonian dynamics. No one suggested it could
  be taken seriously, since it rejected indeterminism, like
  Einstein. We read von Neumann’s axiomatization, but not his
  no–hidden variables proof, and no one noted that Bohm’s theory,
  however ad hoc, was a concrete counterexample.

- Everyone assumed the key conceptual innovation of QM was
  fundamental indeterminism, and that classical deterministic
  thinking had to be replaced by new quantum concepts. This
  produced, for example, a large literature on “probabilistic
  causation.”

- In short, my intro to the foundations of QM was an
  incoherent mixture of disparate ideas, forming an impenetrable
  fog compared to the logical and mathematical clarity of GR.

- It is no surprise that most philosophers of physics focused on
  GR instead. Around that time, David Malament visited the Center
  for the Philosophy of Science and remarked, almost casually,
  that all he had left to do in GR was work out some details
  about rotating coordinate systems. Then he would be finished
  and would need something new to study.

- Meanwhile, Bell’s theorem remained lodged in my mind, and none
  of this helped clarify it.

- Around then, David Mermin’s articles on Bell appeared,
  including “Quantum Mysteries for Anyone” in the Journal of
  Philosophy. They stunned me again, as d’Espagnat’s had. Bell
  had uncovered what seemed to me the single most important
  physical fact in physics, yet no one had figured out how to
  come to terms with it. Was it rooted in indeterminism? If
  non-locality was physically real, how could it be reconciled
  with relativity? Was this, as d’Espagnat suggested, a proof of
  the unreality of the physical world?

- That was my situation throughout graduate school in the early
  1980s. Everything I found on QM was a confusing mix of sharp
  mathematics—Gleason’s theorem, non-distributive lattices—and
  obscure physics, none offering a clear solution to the
  measurement problem. Standing above this confusion was Bell’s
  theorem, which did not even mention QM. It proved, as far as
  I could see with no acceptable escape, that the world is
  non-local, that there's genuine action at a distance, and that
  Einstein was right to see a prima facie conflict with
  relativity. Conspiracy theories were logically possible but
  physically unacceptable. Something had to give. And that is
  essentially how things stood throughout graduate school.

- In 1985 Nick Herbert’s book Quantum Reality appeared, and
  I still have my copy. It is written at a wonderfully
  non-technical but clear level. Herbert presents eight different
  “quantum realities”:

- Quantum Reality #1:
    - The Copenhagen interpretation, part 1 (there's no deep
      reality).

- Quantum Reality #2:
    - The Copenhagen interpretation, part 2 (reality is created
      by observation).

- Quantum Reality #3:
    - Reality as an undivided wholeness. This is loosely Bohmian,
      though it reflects the quasi-mystical Bohm popular at the
      time rather than the 1952 Bohm that awakened Bell from his
      dogmatic slumbers. It also involves taking the wavefunction
      seriously as representing part of physical reality and
      emphasizes the need for contextuality.

- Quantum Reality #4:
    - Many worlds.

- Quantum Reality #5:
    - Quantum logic.

- Quantum Reality #6:
    - Neorealism (the world is made of ordinary objects).
    - The idea here is that since a classical reality somehow
      emerges from QM, as everyday experience suggests, there's
      no reason to think the world isn't classical all the way
      down. This is where Bohm 1952 is actually mentioned, since
      its particles are ordinary point particles with definite
      positions following continuous trajectories.

- Quantum Reality #7:
    - Consciousness creates reality, essentially Wigner’s version
      of von Neumann, or perhaps von Neumann’s version of von
      Neumann.

- Quantum Reality #8:
    - Werner Heisenberg’s duplex world, which interprets quantum
      theory using Aristotelian potentialities and actualities.
    - Wavefunction collapse is presented as a transition from
      physically real potentiality to actuality.
    - The claim is that classical physics, from Galileo through
      Maxwell and Einstein, emerged by overthrowing Aristotle’s
      influence during the Scientific Revolution, which was
      largely a good thing, but that something valuable was lost
      along the way.
    - Aristotle’s notion of powers or potentia was discarded too
      quickly, mocked through examples like the virtus dormitiva.
    - We now need to reintroduce physical powers into physics.
    - This idea resurfaces periodically. If you are a Greek
      scholar, you can enjoy revisiting Aristotle’s distinctions
      between first and second potentialities and actualities.
    - And if you happen to be Nancy Cartwright, you can even
      argue for abandoning the idea of a single unified physical
      theory in favor of interacting “capacities” that produce
      a dappled world with a patchwork of laws. Don’t ask.

- Toward the end of the book Herbert lays out all eight quantum
  realities and discusses the pros and cons of each. It reads
  like a calm, impartial assessment, and he doesn't advocate any
  particular view.

- The overall impression is a “let eight flowers bloom” attitude:
    - there are many legitimate ways to understand what QM is
      telling us. (Herbert, incidentally, is one of the “Hippies
      Who Saved Physics.”)

- But then something odd happens.

- Only after all eight quantum realities have been presented,
  does Herbert introduce the EPR argument, in chapter 11,
  followed by Bell’s argument in chapter 12. This seems
  deliberate: the book is meant to culminate in Bell’s
  extraordinary discovery.

- Herbert explains both EPR and Bell extremely well, at least as
  clearly as d’Espagnat and Mermin, and cites “Quantum Mysteries
  for Anyone” and “Quantum Theory and Reality” among further
  readings.

- And once again, for anyone paying attention, the point was
  unavoidable. Bell had proved something unprecedentedly
  important, using only a few lines of algebra. He had blown the
  lid off everything, and you did not even need to “believe in”
  QM. You could simply go into the lab and check, as Clauser,
  Aspect, Zeilinger, and many others did.

- This structure was emotionally satisfying but logically
  frustrating. Once you understand what Bell proved, and what the
  experimental violation of his inequality for `spacelike
  separated events` means your perspective on the eight quantum
  realities must change. (Regardless of your views about QM, and
  even if QM had never been invented)

- Take `neorealism`. It claims that because a classical world
  emerges from QM, the world must be classical all the way down.
  But Bell showed that a normal classical, i.e. local, world
  doesn't emerge from QM at all.
    - What emerges is a deeply non-classical world, directly
      revealed by results from Clauser’s and Aspect’s
      laboratories. This is precisely the weirdness that led
      Einstein to reject Copenhagen, now confirmed not by
      speculative philosophy but by simple calculations and hard
      experimental work. After EPR and Bell, `neorealism` is
      dead. One quantum reality down.

- None of this prevented Murray Gell-Mann from dismissing quantum
  non-locality and spooky action at a distance as “flapdoodle” in
  'The Quark and the Jaguar'. 
    - In doing so, he took Bell’s example of Bertlmann’s socks
      and turned it upside down, claiming that quantum
      correlations are no more puzzling than mismatched socks.
      Bell introduced this for exactly the opposite reason:
        - to show that quantum correlations can't be explained in
          that way. As Bell himself said, misunderstanding could
          hardly be more complete.

- Without reviewing all the remaining quantum realities one by
  one, the central point is this. Since 1952, physicists should
  have known that quantum phenomena could be explained by
  a deterministic theory of ordinary particles guided by
  a non-local dynamics involving the wavefunction.

- They might not have liked the theory. Because of its
  non-locality, they might have sided with Einstein and kept
  searching for an alternative. But what they would have said is
  this: 
    - the theory restores determinism, has no measurement
      problem, has a clear ontology, involves no oddities of
      logic or probability, and—using Bell’s phrase—“neither
      needs nor is embarrassed by an observer.”

- The mathematics is as simple as one could reasonably want. But
  the theory is non-local. And that, they would have said, is
  intolerable.

- If history had unfolded that way, every physics textbook would
  now be Bohmian. If, in 1952, the virtues of Bohmian mechanics
  had been acknowledged and the only complaint had been its
  non-locality—the 'spukhafte Fernwirkung'—then that position
  would have been perfectly defensible, as it was for Einstein.

- At the time there was not a shred of empirical evidence for
  non-locality, so accepting a non-local theory, however
  virtuous, would have meant embracing something deeply strange
  without strong experimental reason. In that situation, I might
  well have sided with Einstein.

- But had that been the state of play in 1952, all hell would
  have broken loose in 1964. Once Bell showed that the
  predictions of QM—the very theory Einstein had criticized for
  decades for entailing indeterminism and thus non-locality—could
  not possibly be reproduced by any local theory (assuming
  statistical independence), and that these predictions were
  easily testable, the world would have waited breathlessly for
  the experiments.

- When non-locality was confirmed, Bohm, de Broglie, and above
  all Bell would have been hoisted onto shoulders and carried
  straight to Oslo, Nobel rules hastily rewritten.

- Or something like that.

- In reality, Bell published his epochal paper in about the most
  obscure venue imaginable: the inaugural issue of the
  quasi-journal Physics, which limped along for a decade and then
  folded. His result barely avoided complete obscurity because
  Abner Shimony, a philosopher, grasped its significance and
  effectively handed it to Clauser as a homework problem to test.

- Otherwise Bell might have continued contentedly at CERN, doing
  foundations work as a weekend hobby while correcting
  colleagues’ confusions over lunch. By then Bohm had already
  been unjustly ridiculed for over a decade, and the Big Shots
  were never going to admit how indefensible that treatment had
  been.

- De Broglie, bullied in 1927 by Pauli and Bohr’s acolyte Kramers
  into abandoning his pilot-wave theory, had settled into the
  role of a historical footnote—important, but safely contained.
    - (It is impossible to study the history of QM without asking
      such counterfactual questions; Jim Cushing did so seriously
      in QM: Historical Contingency and the Copenhagen
      Interpretation.)

- Back to Herbert. The main shortcoming of his otherwise
  excellent book, is that this logical situation never comes
  through clearly enough, at least not to me.

- He presents the eight quantum realities, then introduces EPR
  and Bell, and then asks what Bell’s result implies—fully
  understanding its significance.
- He even summarizes it correctly: Bell assumes a local reality,
  shows with a bit of arithmetic that it contradicts experiment,
  and concludes that reality must be non-local.
- But instead of revisiting the eight quantum realities and
  noticing that one of them—Bohm’s—already stood out precisely
  because of its explicit non-locality, Herbert treats
  non-locality as something that could simply be sprinkled onto
  any of the views as needed.
- What had been regarded as Bohmian mechanics’ fatal flaw had
  suddenly become its defining virtue, but Herbert doesn't
  emphasize this.

- For example, when discussing “Consciousness creates reality,”
  Herbert notes that Bell’s theorem implies that if consciousness
  collapses the wavefunction, this process can't be purely local:
  a decision here must affect an outcome there, across vast
  distances.

- True enough. But this is a far less satisfactory situation than
  in Bohmian mechanics, which already had action at a distance,
  long before it was fashionable. (Herbert’s attention to
  consciousness-induced collapse, implausible as it seems, is
  partly explained by the fact that the GRW spontaneous collapse
  theory appeared only a year after Quantum Reality.)

- Herbert also makes a more serious mistake by asserting that
  Bell’s theorem relies on what he calls the “Counterfactual
  Definiteness” assumption. He loosely associates this claim with
  John Wheeler, though it is more often traced to Henry Stapp.

- This red herring has plagued discussions of Bell’s theorem ever
  since. If Bell’s result depended on counterfactual
  definiteness, one could evade it simply by denying that
  assumption.

- Since counterfactual definiteness asserts that there's a fact
  of the matter about what would have happened had an experiment
  been performed, rejecting it seems trivial. Indeed, every
  fundamentally indeterministic theory denies it by definition.
  So on this view, Bell would have been guilty of assuming
  determinism from the outset. What a blunder.

- According to this wildly false narrative, Bell’s theorem can be
  neutralized by rejecting determinism and embracing
  indeterminism—exactly what Copenhagen had supposedly done all
  along. The only holdout, on this telling, was Einstein: an
  aging reactionary who could not accept the new quantum wisdom.
  This caricature has proven remarkably resilient, but it rests
  on a complete misunderstanding of Bell’s work.

- Did I mention that the account given above is wildly false?

- Here is Bell himself, in his usual trenchant style, from
  “Bertlmann’s socks and the nature of reality”:

> "It is important to note that to the limited degree that
> determinism plays a role in the EPR argument, it isn't assumed
> but inferred. What is held sacred is the principle of "local
> causality" or "no action at a distance". Of course, mere
> correlation between distant events doesn't itself imply action
> at a distance, but only correlation between the signals
> reaching the two places. These signals, in the idealized
> example of Bohm, must be sufficient to determine whether the
> particles would go up or down. For any residual undeterminism
> could only spoil the perfect correlation."

- It is remarkably difficult to get this point across:
    - determinism isn't a presupposition of the argument.

- There remains a widespread but mistaken conviction that
  determinism was Einstein’s central principle. The quotability
  of “God does not play dice” has only reinforced this error.
  Even Born struggled to understand Einstein’s position. Pauli
  tried to correct him in a 1954 letter:

> ...I was unable to recognize Einstein whenever you talked about
> him in either your letter or your manuscript. It seemed to me
> as if you had erected some dummy Einstein for yourself, which
> you then knocked down with great pomp. In particular, Einstein
> doesn't consider the concept of 'determinism' to be as
> fundamental as it is frequently held to be (as he told me
> emphatically many times)...he disputes that he uses as
> a criterion of admissibility of a theory "Is it rigorously
> deterministic?"...he was not at all annoyed with you, but only
> said you were a person who will not listen.

- Born had particular difficulty with the Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen
  argument. Much later, when editing the Born–Einstein
  correspondence, he summarized the disagreement as follows:

> The root of the difference between Einstein and me was the
> axiom that events which happen in different places A and B are
> independent of one another, in the sense that an observation on
> the state of affairs at B can't teach us anything about the
> state of affairs at A.

- Misunderstanding could hardly be more complete. Einstein
  readily accepted `correlations` between distant events. What he
  rejected was the idea that an intervention at one location
  could IMMEDIATELY influence physical reality at another.

- There's another historical sidelight worth noting. The claim
  that Bell assumed determinism, also known as counterfactual
  definiteness, has been reinforced by repeated citation of the
  title of a paper by Asher Peres. There are two papers in 20th
  century physics whose titles have exerted influence far beyond
  their contents, often among people who clearly never read them.

- The all-time winner is Eugene Wigner’s “The Unreasonable
  Effectiveness of Mathematics in the Natural Sciences” (1960).
    - The title itself poses an interesting puzzle, even if many
      who cite it seem unaware of Wigner’s own discussion.
    - (One wonders how many physicists who quote it know the
      paper opens with a line from Bertrand Russell.)

- Second place goes to Asher Peres’s 4-page paper “Unperformed
  experiments have no results” (1978).
    - From the title alone, one might infer that Peres is
      accusing Bell of assuming unperformed experiments have
      definite outcomes, and is criticizing this assumption by
      pointing out that obviously unperformed experiments have no
      results. It would seem to follow that Bell’s theorem
      doesn't apply to QM, since it is indeterministic.

- This is a perfectly plausible inference—and completely wrong.
  Yet Peres’s title is very often invoked to suggest exactly this
  mistaken conclusion.

- Curiously, Peres himself doesn't deploy the argument this way.
  He has no quarrel with Bell or with his reasoning. At the end
  of this concise paper, Peres presents the reader with two stark
  choices. Here is the entirety of the final section, titled
  “Discussion and Outlook”:

> There are two possible attitudes in the face of these results.
> One is to say that it is illegitimate to speculate about
> unperformed experiments. In brief “Thou shalt not think.”
> Physics is then free from many epistemological difficulties.
> For instance, it isn't possible to formulate the EPR paradox.
> [this last part is, BTW, false]

> Alternatively, for those who can't refrain from thinking, we can
> abandon the assumption that the results of measurements by A are
> independent of what is being done by B. In other words, we should
> not have written r_jα, but rather r_jα(βγδ…), where the additional
> subscripts γ, δ, … refer to possible experiments on anything else
> in the world that has been correlated (read: entangled) with the
> history of the j-th particle.

> The conclusion is surprising. Physicists are used to thinking
> in terms of isolated systems whose behavior is independent of
> what happens in the rest of the world. Bell’s theorem tells us
> that such a separation is impossible for individual
> experiments, although it still holds for averages:

`(1/N) Σ_j r_jα(βγδ…) = 0`

> and

`(1/N) Σ_j r_jα(βγδ…) r_jβ(αγδ…) = − α · β`

> independently of γ, δ, etc. As it often happens, the subtlety
> of nature beggars the human imagination.


- There's one final twist to this story. When I was in grad
  school, there was a broad consensus that the unintuitive
  character of QM was rooted in its `indeterminism`.

- On this view, our “classical” intuitions had been shaped by
  fully deterministic theories, so it was no surprise they misled
  us when confronted with indeterminism.
    - This diagnosis was reinforced by the fact that experimental
      violations of Bell’s inequality involve probabilities
      strictly between `0` and `1`.

- As a result, the violations don't follow by logical implication
  from the data and instead emerge only GRADUALLY from
  statistical analysis, giving them an unmistakably ghostly or
  spooky character.

- And this, at last, seems like a fitting place to end the tale.

- “As it often happens, the subtlety of nature beggars the human
  imagination.”
