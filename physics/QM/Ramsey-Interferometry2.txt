# Superposition, Relative Phase, and Ramsey Clocks

- Short answer: Yes. The "abstract" superposition has directly
  measurable physical consequences.

## What's actually oscillating

- If an atom is in a superposition of two energy eigenstates,

- `|ψ(t)⟩ = a·e^(-iE₁t/ħ)|1⟩ + b·e^(-iE₂t/ħ)|2⟩`

- each component rotates in phase at a rate set by its energy.
  Only the *relative* phase matters, and it advances at the
  angular frequency

- `ω = (E₂ − E₁)/ħ`

- That's the "natural frequency" in Weinberg's article. Nothing
  moves through space; what oscillates is the quantum state
  itself, and with it the probabilities of outcomes for certain
  observables (e.g., the atom's dipole moment oscillates, which
  is how an atom in superposition can radiate).

## Why it's measurable: interference

- A global phase on a quantum state is unobservable. A *relative*
  phase between two components of a superposition is observable,
  because it determines how those components interfere when
  recombined. This is the same logic as the double-slit
  experiment, with "two energy states" instead of "two paths."

## Ramsey's method

1. The first microwave pulse puts the atom into an equal
   superposition of |1⟩ and |2⟩.
2. During the free evolution time T, the relative phase
   accumulates at the atom's natural frequency, while the
   microwave source (the "local oscillator") advances its own
   phase at its own frequency.
3. The second pulse recombines the components. Whether the atom
   ends up in |1⟩ or |2⟩ depends on the *difference* between the
   accumulated atomic phase and the microwave's phase.
4. Counting atoms in each state gives a fringe pattern,
   P ∝ cos²(Δ·T/2), where Δ is the detuning between the microwave
   frequency and the atomic frequency.

- Tune the microwave until the fringes show zero detuning, and
  the microwave frequency now equals the atomic frequency. The
  longer T is, the narrower the fringes, which is why Ramsey
  spectroscopy is so precise.

## Is it "physical"?

- The superposition isn't a mathematical decoration on top of the
  real atom; the state vector *is* our best description of the
  atom, and the relative phase is a real feature of it. Atomic
  clocks, which define the second, depend on it. Cesium clocks
  literally count cycles of this phase evolution.

- Caveat on the GPT answer: "an oscillation in an observable" is
  right, but the eigenstate populations themselves don't
  oscillate during free evolution. Only the phase does, and it's
  invisible until the second pulse converts phase into
  population.

## Weinberg's point

- Clocks are a sensitive probe: if quantum mechanics were
  slightly nonlinear or otherwise modified, the phase evolution
  would deviate subtly from ΔE/ħ, and a good enough clock could
  in principle detect it.
