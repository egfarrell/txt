## What Peres's Paper Actually Does
    - The paper is best understood as sitting at the intersection
      of three things: it *presents* Bell's theorem, it offers
      a *distinctive framing* of what the theorem means, and it
      coins a *philosophical slogan* that became influential in
      its own right. Let me untangle these.
    - It's not simply a rederivation of Bell's inequalities. Bell
      (1964) derived his inequalities by assuming local hidden
      variables and showing quantum mechanics violates them.
      Peres's approach is subtly different in emphasis. Rather
      than primarily asking "can a local hidden variable theory
      reproduce quantum predictions?", Peres focuses on what he
      calls the *counterfactual* structure of the argument: what
      happens when you try to assign definite outcomes to
      measurements that were *never actually performed*?
    - The core move is this: take a pair of entangled particles
      and an actual set of measurements performed on them. Now
      try to *retroactively imagine* what the results would have
      been had you chosen different measurement settings instead.
      Peres shows that there is no consistent way to assign such
      hypothetical outcomes that satisfies all the
      quantum-mechanical correlations simultaneously — whereas
      classical correlations *can* be accommodated this way. The
      paradox, he argues, doesn't arise from nonlocality per se,
      but from the illegitimacy of assigning definite values to
      unperformed experiments.
    - This framing connects Bell's theorem tightly to an older
      result: the Kochen-Specker theorem (1967), which showed
      that quantum observables cannot all be assigned
      pre-existing definite values simultaneously, even without
      any spatial separation. Peres's phrase became a widely
      recognized slogan for quantum contextuality — the idea that
      measurement outcomes cannot be thought of as revealing
      pre-existing properties, because those properties depend on
      the entire measurement context. In later decades, Peres
      went on to construct an elegant, simplified proof of
      contextuality (the "Peres-Mermin magic square") that is now
      standard in the field.
    - So the paper's *philosophical* contribution is sharper than
      its technical one: it identifies that what's really wrong
      with hidden variable reasoning is the assumption of
      counterfactual definiteness — the idea that it is
      meaningful to speak of the results of experiments you
      didn't do.

## How It Fits Relative to Bell
    - Bell's 1964 paper derived an *inequality* that any local
      hidden variable theory must satisfy, and showed quantum
      mechanics violates it. Peres's 1978 paper reframes the
      lesson: the violation occurs precisely because quantum
      mechanics refuses to assign outcomes to unperformed
      measurements. Peres argued explicitly in his later textbook
      that "Bell's theorem is not a property of quantum theory"
      — meaning the paradox emerges only when you import the
      classical assumption that counterfactual results are
      meaningful, an assumption quantum mechanics flatly rejects.
    - This is a genuine conceptual addition, not just
      a restatement. Bell derived a mathematical constraint;
      Peres diagnosed *why* the constraint is violated and gave
      it a name.

## Impact and Reception
    - The paper's impact operated on two levels:
    - As a slogan.
        - The Copenhagen interpretation of quantum mechanics
          rejects counterfactual definiteness — it does not
          ascribe any value to a measurement that was not
          performed, and Peres's phrase became the canonical way
          to express this. The title became one of the most
          quoted one-liners in quantum foundations.
    - As a contribution to contextuality.
        - Later discussions of quantum contextuality
          — particularly the rapid growth of research into the
          Kochen-Specker theorem and its implications
          — repeatedly cited Peres as having "sloganized" the key
          idea. His 1978 paper is a standard citation in the
          contextuality literature, appearing alongside
          Kochen-Specker and Bell in foundational reference
          lists.

### Engagement and criticism.
    - The paper attracted ongoing philosophical debate,
      particularly from people skeptical that the "no results"
      claim dissolves rather than solves the paradox. Karl Hess
      and Walter Philipp, for example, argued that all versions
      of Bell's theorem (including Peres's presentation in his
      later textbook) were fundamentally flawed. N. David Mermin
      wrote a short paper directly defending Peres's formulation
      against this attack, as a birthday tribute, noting the
      criticism was "easily settled" — consistent with Peres's
      own attitude of not bothering to refute criticisms he found
      obviously wrong.
    - More broadly, the paper's framing fed into ongoing debates
      about what Bell's theorem actually *proves*: is it about
      nonlocality, about realism, about counterfactual reasoning,
      or about all three? Peres's answer was firmly in the
      counterfactual camp, and that position continues to be
      debated in philosophy of physics.
    - In short: The paper is neither a pure rederivation nor an
      entirely new theorem. It is a conceptually reorienting
      paper that takes Bell's result and argues its real target
      is the hidden assumption that unperformed experiments have
      definite outcomes — an assumption quantum mechanics
      categorically rejects. The phrase itself became
      a touchstone in discussions of contextuality, measurement,
      and the interpretation of quantum mechanics.
