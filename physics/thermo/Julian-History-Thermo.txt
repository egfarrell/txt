### Clausius
    - "If for the entire universe we conceive the same magnitude
      to be determined, consistently and with due regard to all
      circumstances, which for a single body I have called
      _entropy_, and if at the same time we introduce the other
      and simpler conception of energy, we may express in the
      following manner the fundamental laws of the universe which
      correspond to the two fundamental theorems of the
      mechanical theory of heat."
        1. The _energy_ of the universe is constant
        2. The _entropy_ of the universe tends to a maximum
    - Simple English
        - Imagine we measure the same quantity, which I call
          "entropy," for the entire universe, just as I did for
          a single object.
        - If we also consider the simpler idea of "energy," we
          can state the universe's basic rules, which are like
          the two main ideas in the study of heat.
        - 1. The total energy in the universe stays the same.
        - 2. The total entropy in the universe always increases
          to its highest possible level.


### State functions
    - If you walk from any point `A` on the Earth’s surface to
      any other point `B`, your ALTITUDE above sea level at `B`
      will be the same, whatever route you take.
    - The same is true of the difference of altitudes.
    - In fact, the _difference_ is physically more significant
      since altitude could just as well be measured relative to
      Mount Everest’s peak as to sea level.
    - Quantities that are _route independent_ are very important
      in physics. In thermodynamics they're called STATE
      FUNCTIONS.
    - We already met them as the pressure, volume and temperature
      of gases in equilibrium.
    - But other important qualities are _route dependent_.
        - E.g. driving to a town that has 3 different toll roads,
          each with differing amounts to pay. Then your journey
          cost is route dependent.
    - To determine the difference in heights above sea level at
      the end of a journey, all you need to know are the start
      and end points.
        - But if you have to pay tolls and there are different
          routes, you cannot determine the cost of the journey
          unless you specify the _route_.

### Two kinds of work: Interior / Exterior Work
    - In his 1850 paper, Clausius recognized the full
      significance of Joule’s work when he observed that TWO
      different kinds of work can be performed in
      a thermodynamics system.
    - One is _interior work_
        - (route independent)
        - eg the work needed “to overcome the mutual attraction
          of the particles” of water. (Joule had spoken of the
          molecules of a compressed spring being “forced
          asunder”.)
    - Now the same amount of heat will need to be expended on the
      interior work whenever and however it happens, for that
      depends solely on the nature of the molecules.
        - It’s route independent and analogous to altitude; all
          you need to know are the intial and final states.
    - The other is _exterior work_
        - (route dependent)
        - E.g. done by successive EXPANSIONS and compressions of
          a gas. If the original condition of the gas is again
          established at the end of the process, there is no
          guarantee that the entire amount of WORK PRODUCED will
          equal the amount expended.
        - This would be the case in a Carnot heat engine, Fig. 1,
          taken along `abc` and then simply reverse back to `a`
          along `cba`. Then no net work would be done.
        - But with the route along `cda` back to `a` the maximum
          possible work is done.
        - Thus, exterior work is a ROUTE-DEPENDENT quantity.
        - One has to know the _succession of states_ through
          which the system is carried by the application of heat
          or pressure from without (outside?).

### Motions of molecules, dof's
    - However, in a decisive advance, Clausius argues that “this
      is not the only motion present”.
        - Because molecules are composed of atoms and conceived
          to be extended, they must be capable of _rotational
          motion_ which can be increased or decreased whenever
          two molecules collide.
    - There can also surely be _vibrational motion_ corresponding
      to motion of the atoms inside a molecule relative to each
      other.
    - Although the term was only coined later by Maxwell, we can
      introduce the notion of mechanical `degrees of freedom`
      here.
    - The dof's come in two kinds:
        - one related to `position`,
        - the other to `translation` from place to place as
          expressed by velocity (or more precisely momentum).
    - If we consider a point particle, its `position` will be
      defined by 3 coordinates.
        - These are the 3 positional degrees of freedom.
    - Also there are 3 `momentum` degrees of freedom associated
      with motion along the 3 coordinate directions.
    - However, if a molecule is made up of two or more atoms,
      there are even more degrees of freedom.
    - For example, a diatomic molecule will be like a dumbell
      that can _rotate_ and _vibrate_, so there are further
      possible motions.
    - In the final third of his paper, Clausius translates these
      qualitative ideas into concrete calculations for gases and
      obtains important numerial results...

### Maxwell peers through the microscopic window
    - Keys: remarkable viscosity result
    - He notes that two basically equivalent models can be
      adopted for the ‘minute parts’ of matter.
    - The parts can be represented either as:
        - `centres of force`  (? Like Faraday)
        - or as hard elastic bodies,
    - He says it's evident the same results will be obtained.
    - Maxwell opts for the second model and considers two
      possibilities:
        - the elastic bodies are exactly _spherical_ or
        - have a bounding surface that is not spherical.
    - Besides reproducing results that had already been obtained,
      Maxwell’s paper contained a result of fundamental
      conceptual significance as well as a prediction that was so
      counterintuitive he initially thought it _disproved_ the
      kinetic theory of gases.
    - However, he later confirmed the prediction (concerning the
      viscosity of gases) in a famous experiment with his wife.
    - This can be illustrated by a pendulum. If it is set
      swinging in air, its initial amplitude slowly decreases
      until it eventually comes to rest.
        - This happens partly because of friction at its point of
          suspension and partly because the pendulum bob has to
          ‘push aside’ the air it encounters during its swing.
    - Air’s viscosity slows motion, like how walking through
      water takes effort. So Maxwell thought a pendulum in denser
      air would stop faster.
        - To his great surprise, Maxwell's calculations showed
          that changing the `density` had very little effect.
        - With his wife, he performed an experiment that
          confirmed the prediction.
    - This boosted confidence in the theory, which by 1865 gained
      widespread acceptance.

### Mean Free Path
    - Indeed, a striking by-product of Maxwell’s viscosity
      calculations was his ability to estimate Clausius’s mean
      free path.
    - Taking the mean velocity of air molecules to be 1,505
      ft/sec, he found the mean distance travelled by a molecule
      between collisions is 1/447000th of an inch.
    - ∴ Each molecule makes 8,077,200,000 collisions per sec(!)
    - The colossal numbers involved in the atomic hypothesis were
      beginning to emerge.
    - However, this didn't yield an estimate of the diameter of
      an individual molecule yet, or the number of atoms in your
      little finger.
    - A further idea would be needed to get at that.

    - 'Further idea 1'
        - The *further idea* hinted at above is *the concept of
          molecular diameter and number density* (i.e. the number
          of molecules per unit volume), which—combined with the
          mean free path—can be used to estimate molecular size.
        - Specifically, the key idea is:
            - Using the kinetic theory relation for mean free path:
            - `λ = 1 / (√2 * π * d^2 * n)`
        - where:
            - `λ` = mean free path
            - `d` = molecular diameter
            - `n` = number density (molecules per unit volume)
        - From this, knowing `λ` and estimating `n` (from gas
          laws), one can solve for `d`.
        - This development was later made more precise by
          *Boltzmann* and *Loschmidt*.

    - "Further idea 2"
        - The "further idea" needed to estimate the diameter of
          individual molecules or the number of atoms in a given
          volume (like a little finger) relates to comparing the
          _volume of a gas_ to the actual volume occupied by the
          molecules when they are in a _liquid state_.
        - Here's why:
        - Mean Free Path provides a distance:
            - Maxwell's calculations on mean free path told them
              the *average distance* a molecule travels before
              hitting another. This gives an idea of how far
              apart molecules are in a gas.
        - Need for Molecular Size:
            - To get the *actual size* of the molecules
              themselves, you need a way to relate the empty
              space in a gas to the space the molecules
              physically occupy.
        - The "Condensation Coefficient": 
            - (Loschmidt's contribution)
            - This "further idea" was largely developed by Johann
              Josef Loschmidt. He recognized that if you condense
              a gas into a liquid, the molecules are packed much
              more closely together. By comparing the volume of
              a gas at standard conditions to the volume of the
              same substance in its liquid form, and knowing the
              mean free path, he could estimate the actual volume
              (and thus diameter) of the individual molecules. He
              essentially used the ratio of the volume occupied
              by the gas to the volume occupied by the liquid to
              infer the space taken up by the molecules
              themselves.

        - So, in essence, the "further idea" involved:
            - Understanding the relationship between the gas and
              liquid phases.
            - Using the concept of a "condensation coefficient"
              (or packing density) to relate the macroscopic
              volume of a liquid to the microscopic volume of its
              constituent molecules.

        - This allowed for a more direct estimation of molecular
          dimensions and, consequently, the number of molecules
          in a given volume (which led to the concept of the
          Loschmidt constant, closely related to Avogadro's
          number).

    - Number Density v Packing density
        - Yes, the number density is related to the concept
          underlying Loschmidt's packing density (or condensation
          coefficient), though they describe different aspects:
        - Number density (n):
            - Number of molecules per unit volume (e.g. molecules
              per cm^3).
            - Central to kinetic theory and used to estimate mean
              free path, molecular diameter, etc.
        - Condensation coefficient / packing density (as
          discussed by Loschmidt):
            - A speculative estimate of how closely gas molecules
              would pack if condensed into a liquid or solid.
            - Typically assumes molecules are hard spheres
              touching each other, and looks at how many fit into
              a given volume—giving an effective volume per
              molecule.
        - Relation:
            - By estimating the volume a single molecule occupies
              in a condensed state, Loschmidt could infer:
                - Total number of molecules in a known gas volume
                  ⇒ number density.
                - Hence, link microscopic properties (molecular
                  size) with macroscopic (volume, pressure).
        - In summary:
            - Yes, Loschmidt’s packing density idea helped
              estimate number density by imagining gas as
              a diluted version of a densely packed molecular
              arrangement.

### H-Theorem
    - $MY_NOTES/physics/thermo/Boltzman-H-function.txt
    - Boltzmann’s equation was not his only innovation. 
    - In one of his most important contributions, he introduced
      a quantity later called the `H function`.
    - He proved a result this function's behaviour that became
      known as the _H theorem_.
    - Basically, the `H function` measures the deviation of:
        - the current energy distribution
        - from the Maxwell distibution
        - ... for the same total energy of the system.
    - To get a picture of this in concrete terms, suppose the
      curve of the current distribution is superimposed on the
      Maxwell distribution.
    - The two curves will not overlap, there will in general be
      closed areas between the two curves. The value of the `H
      function` is a measure of the sum of these areas.
        - It will tend to zero as the current distribution gets
          closer and closer to the Maxwell distribution.
    - Boltzmann interpreted such an effect as a _microscopic
      mechanical explanation_ of the way the entropy of an
      isolated system tends to a maximum.
        - The actual relationship between the 'H function' and
          entropy will be an important part of chapter 12.

### Loschmidt's Reversibility paradox
    - Given any possible state of a system of particles, defined
      by their masses, positions and velocities, there will
      always be a state paired with it, where the positions are
      all the same, but all the velocities are exactly reversed.
    - This manifest symmetry might lead one to expect a decrease
      in entropy as often as an increase. But one never does.
    - That's the paradox.

### Importance of initial states
    - Thus a proof cannot be given that after a certain time "the
      spheres must necessarily be mixed uniformly" whatever the
      initial state.
        - He says this is a consequence of probability theory:
          "because any non-uniform distribution, no matter how
          improbable it may be, is still not absolutely
          impossible "
        - (This is a fact about probabilities.E.g. in Lotto, you
          have the exact same chance of a winning sequence
          1,2,3,4,5 as any other five number sequence.)
    - In a critical passage, Boltzmann says:
        - It is only because there are many more _uniform
          distributions_ than non-uniform ones that the
          distribution of states will become uniform in the
          course of time.
        - Therefore one cannot prove that, whatever the starting
          positions and velocities of the spheres are, the
          distribution MUST become uniform after a long time;
        - Instead one can only prove that infinitely many more
          initial states will lead to a uniform one than
          a non-uniform one, after a definite length of time,

### Counting states
    - Boltzmann makes one of the most important observations in
      the history of Physics:
        - One could even calculate their probabilities, from the
          relative numbers of the different state distributions,
          which might lead to an interesting method for the
          calculation of thermal equilibrium.
    - He went on to do just that, utilising a simple method.
    - Any collection of particles has two distinct kinds of
      distribution:
        - one of positions in space
        - the other of kinetic energies
    - But they need to be handled in different ways.
    - In 1877, Boltzmann's main interest was the kinetic
      energies. The prize he was after was the Maxwell
      distribution and why it must be established eventually if
      the energy distribution is initially non-Maxwellian.
    - (Jullian gives an example with discrete quantities and
      a chessboard.)

### Chapter 13: Boltzmann tussle with Zermelo
    - For 15 years after Boltzmann's 1877 insight, the majority
      of German physicists surprisingly turned against atomism
      and the statistical-mechanical interpretation of
      Thermodynamics.
    - Why?
        - Failure to explain the ratio of specific heats: the
          theory suggested energy should be present in internal
          vibrational degrees of freedom but it refused to
          manifest itself.
        - Also: the spectral lines of radiation emitted and
          absorbed by atoms were very hard to interpret
          theoretically.
        - Also the rise of positivism.
        - (But classical mechanics had no chance of solving these
          problems. They needed the discovery of QM)
