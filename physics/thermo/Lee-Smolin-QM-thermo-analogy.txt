### Basic Idea

- Smolin proposes a “real ensemble” view of QM:
  the quantum state represents a real, physically existing
  ensemble of systems distributed across the universe, not just
  a probability distribution.
- He introduces a parameter `N`, the number of similar systems in
  the universe:
  * Large N →
        - standard QM works well (e.g., water
          molecules).
  * Small N →
        - QM may break down, requiring a deeper
          theory (e.g., unique systems like humans).
- In this picture, QM is like `thermodynamics`: it
  emerges as an approximation when averaging over many systems,
  analogous to how temperature arises from many molecules.
- For quantum computing: if we create unique entangled states,
  N might be small, and deviations from standard quantum
  predictions could occur—potentially measurable experimentally.
- Strengths of the analogy:
    * Provides a conceptual “knob” (N) marking where quantum
      mechanics applies.
    * Connects statistical realism with foundational questions in
      quantum theory.

### - Weaknesses / limitations:

  * Defining “sameness” for complex systems (like humans) is
    unclear.
  * Macroscopic uniqueness (N = 1 for humans) doesn’t correspond
    to meaningful quantum ensembles.
  * No experimental evidence that QM fails for
    large or unique systems so far.
  * Risk of circular reasoning: defining N in terms of where
    QM works may not yield predictive power.

- Overall, Smolin’s idea is a `thought-provoking analogy` between
  QM and thermodynamics, highlighting a potential
  path toward a deeper theory, but the N = 1 step is speculative
  and philosophically tricky.

### Full discussion:

    - This is one of Lee Smolin’s most characteristically
      Smolinian ideas: trying to make QM `realist`
      and `relational` at once.

### What Smolin is proposing

- He’s imagining that the quantum state (the wavefunction) is not
  just a mathematical description of possibilities, but
  a reflection of real things that actually exist elsewhere in
  the universe.

He suggests that every system—say a water molecule—doesn’t exist in isolation. Instead, it belongs to a real ensemble: a huge collection of similar systems scattered throughout the universe, all subtly linked in a nonlocal way.

This idea comes from his “real ensemble interpretation” of QM.

The parameter N

He introduces a parameter N, which represents how many copies of a given system exist in the universe.

For ordinary particles or molecules, N is huge (there are many identical systems).
In that case, standard QM emerges as a very good approximation.

But for unique systems, like a conscious observer or perhaps a specific quantum computer arrangement, N = 1 — there’s only one such thing in the universe.
In that limit, QM breaks down, and you’d need a deeper theory that replaces it.

Why this matters for quantum computing

He’s responding to Zierler’s question about whether quantum computers could replace physical experiments.

Smolin’s “wild” take is that QM might not hold indefinitely for every system. If we build more complex, unique quantum setups (like entangled ion traps), we might reach a regime where the assumptions of QM fail—because there aren’t enough identical systems elsewhere in the universe to sustain the usual quantum behavior.

He even claims this leads to testable predictions: if we measure certain deviations from quantum predictions in such systems, it would signal the boundary where QM gives way to a deeper, more realistic, nonlocal theory.

In short

Smolin is saying:

The wavefunction represents real physical ensembles across the universe, not just probabilities.

QM works when N (the number of similar systems) is large.

For unique systems (N small), QM should eventually fail.

That failure could be observable—perhaps even in advanced quantum computing experiments.

So his “final answer” is less about quantum computers replacing experiments, and more about how quantum computers might themselves become the key experiments—pushing us to the edge where quantum theory breaks down.

Let’s take it apart carefully.

1. What Smolin’s doing conceptually

He’s trying to generalize QM so it looks like an approximation to a deeper, statistical theory—a bit like how thermodynamics arises from the behavior of many molecules.
In that analogy, standard QM is valid when you’re averaging over a huge ensemble (large N).
So far, that’s clever and not crazy.

But then he says: For some systems—like conscious beings—N = 1. Meaning: there are no other identical copies of “you” in the universe, so the ensemble picture breaks down.

2. The problem

That leap is philosophically and physically murky.

Defining "sameness":
What counts as “the same system”? A water molecule is clearly identical to another water molecule in structure and energy levels. But a human being is not well-defined as a “quantum system” in the same sense—our macroscopic individuality, biological complexity, and environmental entanglement make it meaningless to talk about “copies” in quantum terms.

Level of description mismatch:
QM applies to specific degrees of freedom (like the electrons in an ion trap, or the spins in a lattice), not to “persons.” Mixing the quantum-mechanical ensemble idea with macroscopic identity confuses categories.

No known quantum breakdowns:
Experiments have tested quantum superpositions for larger and larger systems—C60 molecules, superconducting circuits, micromechanical resonators—and QM still works. There’s no empirical sign yet that the theory fails for systems that are “unique.”

Circularity risk:
If you define N by “how many similar systems exist in the universe,” and then claim QM works when N is large, you haven’t explained anything—you’ve just re-labeled when QM works. It risks being a tautology rather than a predictive principle.

3. What makes it interesting anyway

Smolin’s intuition—there might be a continuum between standard QM and a deeper, more local or realist theory—is worth exploring. He’s trying to identify a physical knob (like N) that marks the transition point. That’s bold, even if the specific “N = 1 for humans” example doesn’t hold up.

It’s the same spirit as decoherence theory or objective-collapse models (like GRW or Penrose’s gravity-induced collapse): something physical sets a boundary for when quantum behavior ends. Smolin’s just assigning that boundary to a cosmic statistical parameter rather than, say, mass or gravitational self-energy.

4. The philosophical hitch

His framing invites a kind of anthropocentrism: that conscious or unique systems are special in how QM applies. That’s risky territory—because the quantum formalism itself is universal, and we have no experimental reason to think consciousness changes the rules.

In short: the weakness is that “N = 1 for humans” isn’t a meaningful quantum statement. It confuses uniqueness in a biological or informational sense with uniqueness in a quantum ensemble sense.

It’s still an intriguing idea—a halfway house between statistical realism and relational ontology—but that N=1 move is where the scaffolding starts to creak.

The broader challenge is still open, though: If QM isn’t the final theory, what parameter measures its limits? Smolin’s N is a speculative candidate for that, even if it’s not yet coherent.
