Yes—you’ve zeroed in on one of the deepest points of tension in the whole
entropy/arrow-of-time debate, and Penrose is absolutely the standard-bearer for
this criticism.


1. The "past hypothesis"
    - To explain why entropy increases, statistical mechanics
      says: you need an improbable low-entropy starting point.
      Once you’ve got that, the rest follows
      automatically—entropy almost certainly goes up from there.
      So cosmologists (like Sean Carroll, David Albert, et al.)
      say: just posit that the early universe had incredibly low
      entropy.


2. Penrose’s critique Penrose points out that this is not an
   explanation but a placeholder. The low entropy of the early
   universe is itself an extraordinary fact crying out for
   explanation.
     - If the laws of physics don’t prefer one state over
       another, the early universe should have been *high
       entropy*—which in gravitational systems means a mess of
       black holes (because gravity clumps things).
        -  Sept 2025: Ah didnt the JWT see loads of tiny black
           holes at very early times? That'd disprove the past
           hypothesis.
    - Instead, the big bang gave us a *smooth*, almost uniform
      distribution of matter and radiation.
    - SMOOTHNESS IS LOW-ENTROPY FOR GRAVITY.
    - Penrose even tried to quantify how "special" this
      smoothness was: something like 1 in 10^(10^123) possible
      microstates. That number is so absurdly tiny it makes your
      head spin.

    - So Penrose says: you can’t just shrug and say "the universe
      started low-entropy." That’s the very thing that needs
      explaining.


3. Is this a contradiction? Not a logical contradiction, but a *deep puzzle*.
The "past hypothesis" is consistent, but unsatisfying: it moves the mystery into
the initial condition without explaining why such an improbable state was
selected.

Penrose’s worry: if entropy increase depends on such a fantastically unlikely
beginning, then our "explanation" of the arrow of time is resting on the very
improbability it was supposed to make sense of. It’s like explaining why dice
rolls look random by saying "they started in a really improbable alignment."


4. Penrose’s alternative idea
    - This is where Penrose’s own theories come in. He suggests
      there must be a physical principle forcing the early
      universe into this special low-entropy state.
    - His favorite is the Weyl curvature hypothesis:
        - at the big bang, the gravitational degrees of freedom
          (measured by the Weyl tensor in GR) were exactly zero,
          forcing the smoothness. Later, as the universe evolves,
          those degrees of freedom unlock and entropy can
          increase.

- So instead of a brute "past hypothesis," Penrose is hunting for
  a lawlike constraint that explains why the early universe was
  special.


So the past hypothesis is a neat patch, but it punts the real
question: why was the entropy so low at the beginning? Penrose’s
point is that any *real* explanation of the arrow of time has to
wrestle with that improbability, not just declare it.

- Here’s a strange angle to think about: in a universe with
  gravity, "low entropy" means SMOOTHNESS, but in almost any
  other system, "low entropy" means clumping. That’s a peculiar
  twist of gravity that makes this problem uniquely cosmic.
