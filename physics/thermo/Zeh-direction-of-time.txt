- It has proven useful to divide the dynamical description of
  Nature into
    - 1. LAWS
    - 2. INITIAL CONDITIONS.
- Wigner (1972), in his Nobel Prize lecture, called this
  conceptual distinction Newton’s greatest discovery, since it
  demonstrates that laws by themselves are far from determining
  Nature.
- The creation of these two pieces of dynamical description
  requires that appropriate kinematical concepts have already
  been defined on empirical grounds. (e.g. formal states or
  configurations `z`, which allow the unique 'mapping' or
  'representation' of all possible states of physical systems.)
  

- The laws of Nature, thus refined to their purely dynamical
  content, describe the time dependence of physical states,
  `z(t)`, in a general form – usually by means of differential
  equations.
- They are called 'deterministic' if they uniquely determine the
  state at time `t` (and possibly its time derivative) from that
  at any earlier or later time. That is, from an appropriate
  initial or final condition.
- This symmetric dynamical determinism is much more rigorous than
  the traditional concept of causality, which requires that every
  event in Nature must possess a specific cause (in its past),
  while not necessarily a specific effect (in its future).
- The Principle of Sufficient Reason (or at least its 'causal
  root') can be understood in this asymmetric causal sense that
  would define an absolute direction of time.
