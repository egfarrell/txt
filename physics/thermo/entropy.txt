### Types of entropy
    - $MY_NOTES/physics/QG/BH-Info-Loss-Sabine-Maudlin-BHG.txt
    - See also: "# Different entropies?" below
    - Thermodynamic entropy
        - original 19th-century concept (Clausius) describing
          heat and irreversibility.
    - Statistical mechanical entropy
        - Boltzmann’s and Gibbs’s microscopic explanation of
          thermodynamic entropy.
    - Shannon entropy
        - mid-20th century abstraction by Shannon, reframing
          entropy as information uncertainty.
    - Von Neumann entropy
        - quantum generalization of Shannon’s formula, applying
          to density matrices.
    - Entanglement entropy
        - modern offspring, using von Neumann entropy to quantify
          quantum correlations between subsystems.

    - Thermodynamic entropy
        - is the macroscopic measure of disorder or energy
          dispersal in a system. Defined through `dS = δQ_rev
          / T`, it increases irreversibly when energy spreads
          into more microscopic degrees of freedom. 
        - It governs the arrow of time via the second law:
          isolated systems evolve toward higher entropy. 
        - Unlike microscopic entropies, it doesn’t count states
          directly but emerges as a coarse-grained, observable
          quantity. It connects heat, work, and the statistical
          underpinnings of irreversibility.
    - Statistical mechanical entropy
        - counts how many microscopic configurations
          (microstates) correspond to a given macroscopic state.
          Boltzmann gave it as `S = k * ln W`, where `W` is the
          number of accessible microstates and `k` is Boltzmann’s
          constant. 
        - It links probability with thermodynamics: 
            - more microstates mean higher entropy. 
            - In quantum statistical mechanics, `W` becomes the
              dimension of the relevant Hilbert subspace. 
            - It’s the bridge between microscopic physics and
              macroscopic irreversibility.
    - Shannon entropy
        - measures average uncertainty in a set of possible
          messages with probabilities {p_i}. Defined as H = -∑
          p_i log p_i, it quantifies how much information is
          gained on average when you learn the outcome. High
          entropy means high unpredictability; low entropy means
          redundancy or certainty. It’s the bedrock of
          information theory and data compression. In spirit,
          it’s the same as statistical entropy—just applied to
          abstract information rather than physical states.
     - Von Neumann entropy
        - generalizes classical entropy to quantum states,
          quantifying uncertainty or mixedness of a density
          matrix `ρ`.
        - It’s given by `S = -Tr(ρ log ρ)`.
        - For pure states, `S = 0` since there’s no uncertainty;
          for mixed states, it’s positive. It tells how much
          information is missing about which pure state the
          system’s in. It’s the fundamental entropy concept
          underlying quantum information theory.
    - Entanglement entropy
        - measures how much quantum information is shared between
          two subsystems of an entangled state.
        - If you take a pure quantum state and trace out part of
          it, the remaining subsystem looks mixed—the
          entanglement entropy quantifies that mixedness.
        - It’s defined as `S = -Tr(ρ_A log ρ_A)`, where `ρ_A` is
          the reduced density matrix.
            - Zero entanglement entropy means the subsystems are
              unentangled; maximal entropy means they’re deeply
              correlated. It captures the “quantum glue” tying
              parts of a system together.

# Different entropies?
    - you’ve just stumbled into one of the most fascinating
      threads in the tapestry of physics: how *entropy* evolved
      from thermodynamics to information theory, weaving through
      Gibbs, von Neumann, and Shannon. It’s the story of how
      “disorder” became “information,” and how the density matrix
      sits right at the crossroads.
    - Let’s walk through it chronologically — each thinker added
      a new layer of meaning.


### 1. Gibbs and the classical statistical world

- In the late 1800s, J. Willard Gibbs developed *statistical
  mechanics* — a way to describe macroscopic systems (like gases)
  in terms of probabilities over microscopic states.

- He realized you can’t know every molecule’s position and
  momentum, so you describe the system by a probability
  distribution {p_i} over all possible microstates. He defined
  the Gibbs entropy as:
    - `S = -k * Σ p_i * ln(p_i)`
    - where `k` is Boltzmann’s constant.

- This formula quantifies uncertainty: if one state has
  probability `1` (you know everything), `S = 0`.
- If probabilities are spread out, S increases.

- So Gibbs entropy = *ignorance about microstates*.

- It was the first time entropy was framed statistically rather
  than mechanically.


### 2. Von Neumann: the quantum leap

- Jump to 1932. John von Neumann, building on both Gibbs and
  quantum theory, asked:

- “Okay, but what if the world is quantum, not classical?
  Probabilities are replaced by density matrices. What’s entropy
  then?”

- He defined quantum entropy as:
    - `S = -k * Tr(ρ * ln(ρ))`
This is the von Neumann entropy.

- It’s the *direct quantum analogue* of Gibbs’s formula, but
  using the density matrix `ρ` instead of classical probabilities.

- If `ρ` represents a *pure state*
    - (ρ^2 = ρ), then S = 0 → no uncertainty.
- If `ρ` is *mixed*,
    - S > 0 → there’s missing information, either because of
      classical ignorance or entanglement with something else.

- That’s the key: quantum entropy can measure entanglement — the
  “hidden” correlations between systems.

- Von Neumann didn’t use that language yet, but he saw that
  ρ could describe subsystems of a larger pure state, and that
  tracing out part of the system increased entropy.

- He had, without quite realizing it, described *entanglement
  entropy* decades before anyone used that term.


### 3. Shannon: the information twist

- In 1948, Claude Shannon published *A Mathematical Theory of
  Communication*, and introduced the Shannon entropy:
    - `H = -Σ p_i * log_2(p_i)`

- This measures *information content* — how many bits you need on
  average to describe an outcome drawn from probabilities {p_i}.

- It’s formally identical to Gibbs’s formula, just with different
  units (bits instead of joules per kelvin).

- Shannon probably didn’t know that von Neumann had already
  written the same functional form for quantum systems. Legend
  has it that when Shannon asked von Neumann what to call his new
  measure, von Neumann said: “Call it *entropy*, because nobody
  knows what entropy really is, so in a debate you’ll always have
  the advantage.” (It’s probably apocryphal, but delightful.)


### 4. The modern view: one idea, many faces

So, the three are part of one evolutionary line:

- Gibbs entropy: Classical uncertainty in phase space.
- Von Neumann entropy: Quantum uncertainty in Hilbert space.
- Shannon entropy: Information-theoretic uncertainty in abstract probability space.

Same mathematical structure, different physical interpretation.

- And now, in modern quantum information theory, the von Neumann
  entropy plays the starring role again. It quantifies:

    - How entangled two subsystems are (entanglement entropy).
    - How much information is lost to the environment
      (decoherence).
    - The ultimate limits of quantum communication and
      computation (via quantities like mutual information and
      channel capacities).

- In short: Gibbs gave entropy *teeth*, von Neumann gave it
  *quantum depth*, and Shannon gave it *meaning*. They all
  discovered the same equation hiding in different corners of
  nature.
