### Dialetric
    - The term _dielectrics_ originates from the Greek words
      *dia* (meaning "through") and *electric,* reflecting the
      ability of these materials to influence electric fields.
    - Faraday introduced it to describe substances that do not
      conduct electricity _directly_ but instead allow an
      electric field to pass through them. 
    - These materials can store electrical energy by becoming
      `polarized` when exposed to an electric field, playing
      a critical role in the transmission and distribution of
      electric force.
    - More info: how a dialectic material interacts with electric
      charge and the electric field:

        1. Not conducting electricity directly: 
            - A material that doesn't conduct electricity resists
              the _movement_ of `electric charges` (electrons or
              ions) through it. 
            - In other words, it is an `insulator`; charges
              cannot flow freely within the material. 
            - For example, rubber or glass does not allow current
              (flow of charge) to pass through them under normal
              conditions.

        2. Allowing an electric field to 'pass through': 
            - Even though the material resists the flow of
              charges, it can still respond to an electric field.
            - This happens because the material's internal
              structure allows its charges to slightly shift in
              response to the field, a phenomenon known as
              `polarization`. 

        - In a `dielectric` material, molecules or atoms
          rearrange or stretch so that their positive and
          negative charges shift slightly, creating tiny dipoles
          (positive and negative poles within the material). 
        - This rearrangement effectively transmits the influence
          of the electric field through the material, even though
          no free charges (electrons or ions) move across it.

        - This process is what enables dielectrics to "allow an
          electric field to pass through them," as the field's
          effects propagate by inducing `polarization`, not by
          moving charges through the material.

        - In summary, dielectrics do not conduct electricity (no
          flow of free charges) but interact with electric fields
          by `polarizing` internally, which allows them to
          influence and transmit the electric field's presence
          and effects. This is a key principle behind their use
          in capacitors and insulation.

### Difference: Mechanical Waves versus EM waves
    - Electromagnetic (EM) waves do indeed behave differently
      from mechanical waves in terms of energy transfer, and they
      are a unique type of wave. Let's see how EM waves are
      distinct and why they exhibit these unique properties.

    - Differences Between EM Waves and Mechanical Waves

    1. Medium of Propagation:
        - _Mechanical Waves:_ Require a medium (solid, liquid, or
          gas) to propagate. Examples include sound waves (air),
          water waves (water), and seismic waves (earth).
        - _EM Waves:_ Don't require a medium and can propagate
          through a vacuum. Examples include light, radio waves,
          and X-rays.

    2. _Energy Transfer Dependence:_
        - _Mechanical Waves:_ The energy transfer rate (power)
          depends on both the square of the `amplitude` and the
          square of the `frequency`. 
            - This is because mechanical waves transfer energy
              through the oscillation of particles in the medium,
              and both amplitude and frequency affect the energy
              imparted to these particles.
        - _EM Waves:_ The energy transfer rate depends only on
          the square of the `amplitude` of the electric field.
          The intensity `I` of an EM wave is given by `I ∝ E^2`,
          where `E` is the electric field amplitude. 
            - The frequency does not affect the energy transfer
              rate in the classical wave description, although it
              affects the energy of individual photons in the
              quantum description.

    3. _Nature of Waves:_
        - _Mechanical Waves:_ Involve the oscillation of
          particles in a medium, which can be longitudinal
          (particles oscillate parallel to wave direction) or
          transverse (particles oscillate perpendicular to wave
          direction).
        - _EM Waves:_ Consist of oscillating electric and
          magnetic fields that are perpendicular to each other
          and to the direction of wave propagation. These fields
          can propagate through a vacuum without the need for
          a medium.

    ### Why EM Waves are Unique

    1. _Electric and Magnetic Fields:_
        - EM waves are composed of oscillating electric and
          magnetic fields that `regenerate` each other as they
          propagate through space. This mutual generation allows
          them to travel through a vacuum, unlike mechanical
          waves that require particle interaction.

    2. _Frequency Independence in Classical Description:_
        - In the classical description, the energy transported by
          an EM wave is proportional to the square of the
          amplitude of the electric field, `E`. The frequency `f`
          does not affect the intensity (power per unit area)
          because the energy carried by the wave depends on how
          strong the electric and magnetic fields are, NOT how
          fast they oscillate.

    - For mechanical waves, energy transfer involves the motion
      and interactions of particles, which are influenced by both
      the amplitude and frequency of the oscillations.

    3. _Photon Perspective:_
        - When considering EM waves from a quantum perspective,
          each photon carries energy `E = hf`, which depends on
          the frequency. However, the classical description of
          wave intensity remains independent of frequency because
          it deals with the collective effect of many photons,
          and the energy transfer at the macroscopic level is
          determined by the field amplitudes.

    - ### Summary
        - EM waves are indeed a unique type of wave due to their
          ability to propagate without a medium and their
          distinct energy transfer characteristics. 
        - The energy transfer in EM waves is governed by the
          amplitude of the electric field in the classical
          description, making them different from mechanical
          waves, where both amplitude and frequency play
          significant roles. This distinction highlights the
          fundamental differences between how mechanical and
          electromagnetic waves operate and transfer energy.

              important. (When you use the same complex number

              the original derivative. When you use a different
              number in each point, it changes.)

              is to add a new field A. When you and multiply each
              point of the universe by a different complex number
              of modulo 1, then A changes in a simple to
              calculate but not obvious way. The change in A fix
              the problem with the derivatives of Ψ.
            - So now the equations of the universe with Ψ and
              A don't change when you make this change. (The
              technical term for this is U(1) local gauge
              symmetry.) When you write carefully how a universe
              like this look like, the new field A is
              electromagnetism. (Actually, you can get the
              electric field and magnetic field using the
              derivatives of A.)

### paramagnetic and diamagnetic 
    - 1. Paramagnetic:
        - are substances that are weakly attracted to an external
          magnetic field. When placed in a magnetic field, the
          atoms or molecules align their magnetic dipoles (tiny
          magnetic fields within them) with the external field. 
        - This alignment causes the material to become weakly
          magnetized in the same direction as the applied field.
          However, once the external magnetic field is removed,
          the material loses its magnetism.

    - 2. Diamagnetic:
        - Diamagnetic materials are substances that are weakly
          repelled by an external magnetic field. Unlike
          paramagnetic materials, in diamagnetic materials, the
          atoms or molecules generate tiny magnetic fields in the
          opposite direction to the applied field, leading to
          a weak magnetic repulsion.
        - Just like with paramagnetic materials, once the
          external magnetic field is removed, diamagnetic
          materials also lose any temporary magnetism.

        - Diamagnetic:
        - Diamagnetic materials are substances in which the atoms
          or molecules produce tiny magnetic fields that oppose
          an external magnetic field. This opposition is due to
          changes in the orbital motion of electrons when exposed
          to the field, resulting in a very weak interaction.
        - While this interaction can be described as a weak
          repulsion of the external magnetic field, it's more
          accurate to say that diamagnetic materials reduce the
          strength of the magnetic field within themselves. They
          are not truly "repelling" the external field in
          a literal sense, as ferromagnetic or paramagnetic
          materials might "attract" a field; instead, they create
          a subtle counteracting effect.
        - Like paramagnetic materials, diamagnetic materials lose
          any induced magnetism when the external magnetic field
          is removed.

        - The key is that diamagnetic materials do not strongly
          "repel" the external field, but rather counteract or
          diminish its effects locally within the substance. This
          subtle distinction avoids implying an active
          "repulsion" similar to that seen in strong magnetic
          interactions like those between two like poles of
          magnets.



    - Summary:
    - Paramagnetic: weakly _attracted_ to external magnetic fields.
    - Diamagnetic:  weakly _repelled_  by external magnetic fields.

    - Note that both paramagnetism and diamagnetism are
      relatively weak effects compared to _ferromagnetism_, which
      is the strong, permanent magnetism seen in materials like
      iron and cobalt.

### Electromagnetic Induction, Electrostatic induction
    - $MY_IMAGES/physics/EM/Maxwell-guided/electrostatic-induction-Fig-1-26.jpg
    - Yes, electromagnetic induction and electrostatic induction
      are two distinct phenomena.
    - Electromagnetic induction involves the generation of an
      electric current in a conductor due to a changing magnetic
      field. It relies on the interaction between magnetism and
      electricity.
    - On the other hand, electrostatic induction is a process
      that involves the redistribution of electric charges in
      a conductor in the presence of an electric field. It occurs
      without any direct contact between objects. This phenomenon
      occurs when a charged object is brought near an uncharged
      (or neutral) object, causing a separation of charges within
      the neutral object.
    - To understand electrostatic induction, imagine you have
      a positively charged object and bring it close to a neutral
      conductor. The positive charges in the object repel the
      electrons in the conductor, causing the electrons to move
      away from the side closest to the charged object. This
      movement of electrons results in a slight positive charge
      on one side of the conductor and a slight negative charge
      on the other side.
    - Electrostatic induction is commonly observed in situations
      such as charging by induction or the behavior of capacitors
      in electrical circuits.
    - In summary, electromagnetic induction involves the
      generation of an electric current in a conductor due to
      a changing magnetic field, while electrostatic induction
      involves the redistribution of electric charges in
      a conductor in the presence of an electric field. Both
      phenomena are significant in the field of electromagnetism
      but have different underlying principles and effects.

### Poynting Vector same as EM Wave?
    - No, the Poynting vector and electromagnetic waves are not
      the same thing, but they are related. 
    - The Poynting vector, named after the physicist John Henry
      Poynting, is a mathematical construct that describes the
      direction and magnitude of the energy flow in an
      electromagnetic field. It is defined as the cross product
      of the electric field vector and the magnetic field vector,
      scaled by the permeability of free space. The Poynting
      vector represents the power per unit area carried by an
      electromagnetic field.
    - On the other hand, an electromagnetic wave refers to the
      propagation of electromagnetic energy through space.
      Electromagnetic waves consist of varying electric and
      magnetic fields that oscillate perpendicular to each other
      and to the direction of wave propagation. These waves can
      transport energy over long distances without the need for
      a medium.
    - The relationship between the Poynting vector and
      electromagnetic waves lies in the fact that the Poynting
      vector determines the direction and magnitude of the energy
      transport in an electromagnetic wave. In regions where
      electromagnetic waves are present, the Poynting vector will
      indicate the direction in which energy is being carried by
      the wave. Thus, the Poynting vector provides a useful tool
      for analyzing the behavior of electromagnetic waves.

### Heaviside ellipsoid
    - A point charge AT REST with respect to the ether produces,
      according to both intuition and Maxwell’s equations, an
      electric field whose equipotential surfaces surrounding the
      charge are spherical.
    - But what happens when the charge distribution is in uniform
      motion relative to the ether?
        - Today, we ignore reference to the ether and simply
          exploit the Lorentz covariance of Maxwell’s
          equations,and transform the stationary solution to one
          associated with a frame in relative uniform motion. 
        - But in 1888, the covariance group of Maxwell’s
          equations was not yet discovered, let alone understood
          physically, the relativity principle not being thought
          to apply to electrodynamics, and the problem of moving
          sources required the solution of Maxwell’s equations.
    - These equations were taken to hold only relative to the
      rest frame of the ether. Oliver Heaviside found (by hunch,
      not brute force) and published the solution: the electric
      field of the moving charge distribution undergoes
      a distortion, with the longitudinal components of the field
      being affected by the motion but the transverse ones not.
    - The _new_ equipotential surfaces define what came to be
      called a Heaviside ellipsoid

### OU definition of radiation
    - In physics, radiation is formally defined as the release or
      transmission of energy associated with particles or waves
      through space or a material. (!!)
    - This means ‘radiation’ includes the energy of PARTICLES
      such as helium nuclei (or alpha particles) and electrons
      (or beta particles) that are released when an unstable
      nucleus in a substance such as uranium undergoes
      radioactive decay.
    - It also includes the various types of electromagnetic WAVE
      in the electromagnetic spectrum, such as radio waves and
      X-rays, as well as infra-red, ultraviolet and even visible
      light.



### OU Ionising Radiation
    - Radiation can be sub-classified based on the amount of
      energy it carries. Low energy radiation, such as that
      carried by radio waves and visible light is known as
      non-ionising radiation.
    - We are surrounded by non-ionising radiation in our daily
      lives and typically we do not need to take any special
      measures to protect ourselves from it. For example,
      microwave ovens are a source of non-ionising radiation that
      can be found in most homes.
    - In contrast, radiation with a relatively higher energy per
      particle is known as IONISING RADIATION.
    - When ionising radiation interacts with atoms and molecules,
      there is enough energy transferred to knock an electron
      free of (or ionise) the atoms and molecules that it comes
      into contact with.
    - As the chemical components of living tissue can be ionised,
      special protective measures and handling techniques have to
      be used when interacting with this radiation, to minimise
      the risk of potentially serious health effects.
    - Examples of ionising radiation include the particle
      radiation mentioned earlier and also high frequency
      ultraviolet, X-ray and gamma ray radiation waves.

    - After all, theres nothing deposited on what have been

        > Thus when the charged conductor is introduced into the
        > closed space [the psitively charged sphere into the
        > room, "bringing" electricity with it] there is

    - We though we were "bringing" something called

# comment:
    - Maxwells electromagnetic theory allowed the theoretical
      physicist to perform a purely _mental version_ of the
      _Michelson-Morley_ experiment:

    - One could construct a mathematical description of light
      travelling through space, and inquire of it how light would
      appear to observers moving at different speeds.

    - One of the straightforward predictions was that someone
      travelling toward a source of light should see it shifted
      to higher frequency, so that red light would become
      blueish. (This corresponds to the Doppler effect for sound,
      by which the siren of an ambulance approaching a bystander
      sounds higher in pitch and turns lower after the ambulance
      goes past)

    - But the same mathematical description of light also yielded
      a _surprising_, seemingly nonsensical result:

    > although the wavelength of the light would change for
    > different observers, its _SPEED_ would not.

    - This seems to be an evident impossibility. If we are moving
      toward a source of light, then surely that light must
      strike us with a higher speed than if we were stationary or
      moving away. How can it be otherwise?

    - Yet the mathematics of Maxwells equations insist that the
      speed of light is always the same, regardless of one's
      state of motion, and the negative result of the
      Michelson-Morley experiment backs up this assertion.
      Einstein may or may not have know about the
      Michelson-Morley experiment, but he surely knew about the
      mathematical problem.

# causality
    The speed of light _c_ plays a fundamental role
    regarding causality because of the relativity of
    simultaneity.

    For two events _A_ and _B_ with space-like separation
    (that is, such that a signal connecting A and B would
    have to propagate faster than c), the time ordering is
    ambiguous: different inertial observers will disagree
    about whether A precedes B in time or vice versa.

    According to special relativity, there is no objective
    fact about which event occurs first, and hence no
    possibility of a causal relation between them, because
    the relation between a cause and its effect is
    necessarily time-asymmetric.

    As Bell explained, "To avoid causal chains going
    backward in time in some frames of reference, we require
    them to go slower than light in any frame of reference."
