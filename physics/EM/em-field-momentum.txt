### KEY 4: ‘Momentum’ of the Magnetic Field

- Context: In the 19th century, EM theory was closely tied to
  Vector Potential _mechanical_ analogies.
    - The idea of momentum in the EM field arises from attempts
      to understand how electric currents and magnetic fields
      interact with a `medium`—then conceived as the *ether*.

### 1. Maxwell, Faraday, and the Mechanical Ether

* Faraday introduced the idea of *lines of force*, emphasizing
  fields as physical entities rather than mere mathematical
  tools.
* Maxwell extended this by giving the ether mechanical
  properties:

* It could store energy.
* It could transmit forces.
* It could exert pressure.
* The next conceptual step:
    - if the ether can transmit forces and store energy, it could
      also *possess motion and momentum*.


### 2. Self-Induction as an Analogy to Inertia

* Observation: When a current in a circuit is interrupted, it
  does not stop instantaneously—it resists change, similar to how
  a mass resists acceleration.
* Quantitative measure: This effect is captured by
  self-inductance, which depends not only on the current but also
  on the geometric configuration of the wire.

  * If inertia were only due to moving charges, changing the
    shape of the wire would not affect the resistance to current
    change.
  * But bending the wire or forming a coil changes the
    self-inductance, indicating the effect is tied to the
    *magnetic field geometry*, not just the particles.


### 3. Electromagnetic Momentum and the Vector Potential

* Maxwell interpreted the self-induction effect as a dynamic
  property of the ether.
* He introduced the concept of “electrokinetic momentum”,
  associating motion with the magnetic field around
  a current-carrying wire.
* Mathematically:

  * This momentum could be evaluated using the vector potential
    A, via the line integral around a circuit:

    * Conceptually, A plays a role analogous to mechanical
      momentum p = mv.
* Key idea: The magnetic field is not just a static entity; it
  carries momentum, stored in the field and transferable to
  matter.


### 4. Historical Interpretation

* Pre-electron era: Momentum was attributed to ether motion, not
  to discrete particles.
* Maxwell’s electromagnetic momentum can be seen as a precursor
  to the modern concept of field momentum:

  * In modern terms: the momentum density in the electromagnetic
    field is g = ε₀ E × B (Poynting vector divided by c²).
  * Maxwell’s “vector potential as momentum” is an early, more
    mechanical way of thinking about the same concept.


### 5. Summary Points

* Self-induction ↔ inertia analogy: The resistance of a current
  to change is like mass resisting acceleration.
* Ether dynamics: The effect cannot be attributed solely to the
  current carriers; it involves the surrounding medium.
* Vector potential as momentum: The line integral of A around
  a circuit quantifies the “momentum” stored in the magnetic
  field.
* Legacy: This reasoning laid the groundwork for modern field
  theory, where fields themselves carry energy and momentum, even
  without material carriers.


# Bridge from “electrokinetic momentum” to modern EM field momentum:


### 1. Maxwell’s Seed Ideas

* Maxwell imagined the ether as a mechanical medium with energy,
  pressure, and momentum.
* Self-induction and the vector potential A were interpreted as
  momentum stored in this medium.
* Although electrons weren’t known, Maxwell could mathematically
  assign a quantity (via ∮A·dl) that behaved like momentum
  associated with the field.
* This was primitive in the sense that it relied on the
  mechanical ether as a carrier; it was not yet the relativistic,
  particle-free field momentum we use today.


### 2. Transition to Field Momentum

* After the discovery of electrons and the abandonment of the
  ether, the same phenomena were reinterpreted as momentum of the
  electromagnetic field itself, not a medium.
* Poynting (1884) formulated the Poynting vector S = E × H,
  describing the flow of electromagnetic energy.

  * By dividing by c² (in SI, g = ε₀ E × B), one obtains the
    momentum density of the field.
  * This is now understood as real momentum that can exert forces
    on matter (e.g., radiation pressure, optical tweezers).


### 3. Modern Understanding

* The electromagnetic field has energy and momentum independent
  of matter.
* Key formulas:

  * Energy density: u = ½(ε₀ E² + B²/μ₀)
  * Momentum density: g = ε₀ E × B
* Momentum transfer: When an EM wave hits a surface, it can push
  it—demonstrating that the field carries momentum, just as
  Maxwell imagined, but without needing the ether.
* Vector potential A still appears in modern formulations:

  * In quantum mechanics (Aharonov–Bohm effect), A has physical
    significance even in regions where B = 0, echoing Maxwell’s
    intuition that the “momentum” resides in the field
    configuration.


### 4. Key Insight

* Maxwell’s early “electrokinetic momentum” was conceptually
  correct: magnetic fields carry momentum.
* The modern view abstracts away the ether, replacing it with
  fields themselves as physical entities.
* So, the lineage is: Ether + self-induction → vector potential
  as momentum → Poynting vector & field momentum → modern EM
  field theory.
