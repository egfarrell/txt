# On Matter and Motion
    - Thomas K. Simpson
    - Great Ideas Today, 1986

- Maxwell’s little book _Matter and Motion_ is the sort thats
  easily overlooked. It's brief and seems "elementary". It
  doesn't seem to get far with the subject of _mechanics_.

- There are few equations, illustrations or examples, and it has
  no worked problems. It's also over a century out of date. At
  best it seems the kind of introductory text one might recommend
  to a beginner whose ultimate ambitions lay in other directions.
  The error entailed in such a judgement has to do with our
  understanding of the term _elementary_.

- In ordinary language this word suggests superficiality,
  something watered down for beginners. 
    - Though Maxwell is certainly aiming his book at beginners,
      I think he has the opposite notion of what 'elementary' is,
      or what it means to place ourselves at the threshold of
      a science. 
    - The elements of a science lie not on its surface but in its
      depths. They are few and uncomplicated, and in that respect
      they are appropriate for _beginners_. Indeed beginners may
      be in a better position to appreciate them than experts,
      whose long experience with complexities often makes simple
      things invisible.

- Maxwell is evidently concerned with the foundations of this
  science of _mechanics_. He seems to enjoy putting his case for
  their reformulation to the working man, not to his fellow
  scientists!

- Maxwell taught for years in adult education classes, and this
  little treatise was published by the 'Committee of General
  Literature and Education of the Society for Promoting Christian
  Knowledge'. 
    - As I discuss elsewhere Maxwell was _greatly drawn_ to the
      figure of Michael Faraday, a brilliantly productive
      scientist without mathematical training. 
    - It seems important to Maxwell to express the outcome of
      sophisticated mathematical investigations in plain prose
      ("forms of words") which can be understood by the
      unmathematical reader.

- At the time _Matter and Motion_ was published, Maxwell was
  heavily engaged at Cambridge University. He had been appointed
  the university’s first professor of experimental physics and
  was giving lectures on mechanics, electricity, magnetism, and
  heat to students unskilled in mathematics.

- This was a sharp turn from his own background as a Cambridge
  student where he had covered much of the mathematical physics
  of the day. 
    - He had only recently published his _Treatise on Electricity
      and Magnetism_ for Cambridge students studying that subject
      within a severe discipline in analytic mathematics.

- In his Cavendish lectures, Maxwell was regularly translating
  his mathematical ideas into new non-mathematical formulations,
  using models, illustrative experiments and careful prose. 
    - Out of this came his _Elementary Treatise_ in Electricity
      and Magnetism, which unfortunately he did not complete
      before his death.

- I presume _Matter and Motion_ represents the kind of foundation
  he was laying in mechanics for his non-mathematical Cambridge
  students.

- Maxwell makes it clear that he prefers this elementary mode of
  presentation. He does not think of it as intellectually
  inferior or inappropriate. 
    - It's evident that Maxwell approaches the foundations of
      mechanics with the spirit of a reformer. 
    - Not only does he set out to purge certain important errors,
      but even in a science as old and established as mechanics,
      he sees a new kind of possibility which will come with
      a _reformation_ of the elements.

- The beginning students immediately find themselves caught up in
  a workshop in which the most fundamental of the physical
  sciences is undergoing reconstruction. 
    - The reader of 'Matter and Motion' is in a specially
      privileged position, _seeing Maxwell reshaping the first of
      the sciences_.

- Certainly _Matter and Motion_ is unlike other elementary texts,
  of Maxwell's time or ours. The reader is given no quarter but
  taken straight to the _heart_ of the difficulty, there is no
  preliminary 'terra firma'.

- We don’t begin with "simple" cases, in which a stone stands
  firmly on the ground and a lever is applied to it, or a ball is
  dropped from a given height at a given time, to fall
  a specified distance.

- Instead, from the outset we find ourselves dealing with systems
  of bodies, whose positions are defined, not with respect to
  a fixed earth, but in _relation_ to one another. Such a system,
  we are told, may be small or it may be the _whole universe_.

- The term `configuration` is introduced abstractly as "the
  assemblage of relative positions." Two themes are thus
  introduced in full earnestness from the beginning:

    1. theme 1:  the wholeness of a system of bodies, 
        - the `whole` is to be treated as an entity prior to the
          separate consideration of its parts;

    2. theme 2:  the _relativity_ of all our accounts of
       physical systems.

- Without arguing that 'Matter and Motion' itself had any
  particular influence, we can see that it stands at a historical
  turning point in physics, and is consciously transforming the
  foundations of mechanics.

- The new concept which allows us to take the 'system' as the
  object of scientific reasoning is that of `energy`, with the
  principle of its conservation.

- Maxwell probably takes directly from metaphysical convictions
  the `principle of relativity`, which in 'Matter and Motion'
  goes hand in hand with the _wholeness_ of the system,

- Maxwell makes clear this is a treatise about _abstract
  dynamics_ not about Physics. I.e., a science of axiomatic
  principles and their consequences, to be _contrasted with
  Physics_, which takes observed phenomena into consideration.

- Throughout 'Matter and Motion' Maxwell considers our most
  fundamental ideas:
    - of time,
    - of space,
    - of matter, force, and energy.

- They are the subject matter of this treatise, and one of its
  major concerns is to reshape and purify them and the science
  based on them.

- Thus Its appropriate that in Article 15 he turns to "a few
  points relating to the _metaphysics_ of _space_.

- In so doing, he joins issue with Descartes in a way that
  suggests not only his disagreement but a deeper affinity of
  purpose. 
    - Maxwell goes on to say in Article 16 that Decartes was very
      wrong about the definition of matter, not having understood
      its identification with the _principle of inertia_, and
      hence was wrong in turn about the idea of space.

- Maxwell insists 'Matter' is one thing, while 'Space' is
  another. _Descartes had in effect identified them_. 
    - With this fundamental correction, however, I think Maxwell
      comes close to pursuing the Cartesian goal of a study of
      mechanics from the point of view of thought alone: 
        - in abstract dynamics we are dealing with pure concepts.

- Thus the Cartesian title 'Matter and motion', which alludes to
  the two principles of Decartes account of the physical world,
  tells us where Maxwell stands.

- Whereas Newtonian forces must be expressed between specific
  bodies, its possible to work with the _energy_ of an entire
  system as a whole. 
    - Each configuration of a conservative system will correspond
      to a definite energy, and passing from state to state in
      such a system we will pass through definite energy changes.

- In these terms, Dynamics can mount the higher ground of the
  "configuration" and characterize systems of wholes and their
  relationships as its primary concern.

- Thus Maxwell points out that where Newton spoke of a "force" of
  one body on another, the larger view is that of _stress_ which
  sees the relativity of the two bodies in their mutuality.

- All this however, takes us to the central thesis of 'Matter and
  Motion': _the principle of relativity_. 
    - This is a powerful insight into the central problem of
      dynamics as well as the future course of physics.


- Maxwell is unyielding on this concept, which he sees as
  belonging _to our ideas themselves, not as contingent upon the
  phenomena of the world_.

- Our common words seem to speak of absolutes, and make
  a distinction between "relative" and "absolute" space for
  example.

- But for Maxwell, it's a fundamental metaphysical truth that
  "All our knowledge, both of time and place, is essentially
  relative" - article 18.  
    - (Sept 2016: Just like Barbour!)

- Maxwell must feel then, that Newton was on the wrong track in
  conceiving absolute time and space as the foundations of the
  mathematical theory of mechanics. 
    - By contrast he recognises that _phenomenal time and space_
      are relative. The foundations of dynamics must speak of
      these concepts as inherently relativistic. 
    - Einstein, not Maxwell, carries this through to completion
      for both space and time; but Maxwell certainly defines the
      task.

- One way to present the reader with such a relativistic view of
  a system is by way of a diagram, and for Maxwell the notion of
  a _diagram_ as a representation of a physical system is very
  important.

- As scientific editor of the 9th Edition of the Encyclopaedia
  Britannica, he wrote an article on "Diagrams" - a topic that
  would not have occurred to most editors. 
    - There are many instances of diagrams in 'Matter and Motion'
      because they express in a single image both our knowledge
      and its limitations.

- Maxwell gives us diagrams, not only of the positions of systems
  of bodies, but of displacements, velocities, and accelerations.
  The important thing about his diagrams is they have _no
  origins_. 
    - They do not locate their points with respect to a supposed
      coordinate system: there is no _frame of reference_.

- Points are located and move and accelerate only with respect to
  one another. This diagram of displacements (without an origin)
  therefore represents neither more nor less than all we can ever
  know about the displacement of the system (article 22).

- If Maxwell is to remain true to the principle of relativity as
  he states it, the difficulty is not that we are doomed to
  ignorance, but that position and displacement are inherently
  relative. There is in truth, _no origin_.

- In Article 29 of 'Matter and Motion', Maxwell presents the
  diagrams of position and velocity in a way that dramatizes his
  insight concerning relativity. 
    - The diagram of position is rightly labeled "Diagram of
      Configuration". 
    - In a sense position suggests absolute location, whereas
      'configuration' immediately expresses the fact that the
      only "figure" that emerges is a pattern of points relative
      to one another.

- Maxwell has presented this diagram of configuration, not only
  without an origin, but printed as bright dots on a _black
  field_, so that not even the paper appears to us as terra
  firma.

- The bodies which make up a system appear naked, like points of
  light in the night sky. Between them there are no absolute
  measures of distance any more than there are in the cosmos. 
    - The transformed dynamics that emerges from 'Matter and
      Motion' is not a mere structure of thought but a working
      instrument at the cutting edge of science.

- Maxwell labels these dynamic ideas as "scientific" i.e.
  science-making. In effect the reader is invited to stand with
  Maxwell at the borders of the unknown, and to survey with him
  the _terrain which lies beyond_.

- Ultimately, Maxwell perceives that a _completed dynamics_ would
  be coextensive with the whole of physical science, projected
  into "dynamical form".

- Maxwell refers the reader to his current efforts to accomplish
  this dynamical theory in the realm of electromagnetism, in his
  'Treatise on Electricity and Magnetism' (1873).

- The dynamical approach brings to _each physical problem_ only
  the conviction that it can be described in the abstract terms
  of dynamics. Its thus the minimal and safest hypothesis.

- Maxwell also sees, in Article 148, its promise for _molecular
  physics_.

- One problem, however, constitutes the burden of the latter part
  of the book: the problem of the _aether_.
    - Maxwell says:
    > "Energy cannot exist except in connection with matter."

    - But energy, in the form of light and heat, traverses _the
      space between_ the sun and the earth.

    - Hence there must be matter "_existing in the interplanetary
      spaces_" and in fact, "disseminated through the whole of
      the visible universe (article 108). (Dark matter? Dec 2017)

- This "matter", the "aether", _cannot be gravitational in
  proportion to its mass_. Yet Newton, by means of experiments in
  the Principia, demonstrated that all matter exerts
  a gravitational force corresponding to its mass.

- Beginning with Chapter VII, Maxwell therefore very carefully
  reviews the theory of the _pendulum_ and its application to the
  measurement of the gravitational constant.

- In Chapter VIII he concludes, notwithstanding the experimental
  evidence, that "Its extremely doubtful whether _the medium of
  light & electricity is a gravitating substance_, though its
  certainly material and has mass" (article 145).
    - (Doesn't Marc Lange say the EM field has gravitational
      effects? Sept 2016)

- The implicit question, appearing as an _enigma_, in effect sets
  the stage for much of the physics of the twentieth century.

- In reflecting on the relation between 'Matter and Motion' and
  the physics of the twentieth century, its important to recall
  the breadth of Maxwell’s claim for the inherent relativity of
  the concepts of time and space.

- Its sometimes supposed that Maxwell, in searching for that
  _matter which conveyed the energies of light_ and electricity,
  hoped to find an _absolute reference frame_ and thereby escape
  the implications of relativity.

- I believe Maxwell makes clear in this text that his sense of
  relativity cuts deeper than that:
    > To discover the existence of a medium, and to determine our
    > velocity with respect to it by observation on the motion of
    > bodies, is a legitimate scientific inquiry...

    > but supposing all this done, we should have discovered, not
    > an error in the laws of motion, but a new fact in science.
    > (article 48).

- Motion relative to an _aether_ would then be an important
  physical fact, but it _would not alter_ the foundations of
  dynamics. Those are relativistic in their very conception.

- Therefore Maxwell would _remain a relativist_ even in the face
  of the discovery of a _universal aether_. The insights of this
  little "elementary" treatise cut very deep.
