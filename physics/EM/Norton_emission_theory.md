### 1. A Puzzle
    - Einsteins famous remarks make no sense in terms of an Ether
      theory of light, because an Ether theorist would have
      easily answered them.

    - Einstein Wrote: "...I would observe such a beam of light as
      an electromagnetic field _at rest_ though spatially
      oscillating. There seems to be no such thing, however:"

        1. "...neither on the basis of experience..."
            - _Ether theorist_: ...but we don't experience frozen
              light for the simple reason that we are not moving
              at `c` through the ether. If we were moving that
              fast, we would experience frozen light.


        2. "...nor according to Maxwell's equations..."
            - _Ether theorist_: Not so. A short calculation shows
              Maxwell's equations predict light becomes frozen
              for observers moving at `c` through the ether.


        3. "... From the very beginning it appeared clear to me
           that, judged from the standpoint of such an observer,
           everything would have to happen according to the same
           laws as for an observer who, relative to the earth,
           was at rest. For how should the first observer know or
           determine, that he is in a state of fast uniform
           motion?..."
            - _Ether theorist_: An observer knows he is moving
              rapidly wrt the `ether` simply because light has
              become frozen. Analogously a surfer knows he is
              moving since he stays on the wave.


### 2. A proposed solution from John Norton
    - There's another way to understand Einstein's thought
      experiment. The key is not to relate the experiment to
      `ether` theories of EM.
        - Rather, we know that before 1905, Einstein devoted some
          effort to so-called `emission` theories of light and
          electromagnetism. Eventually he found such theories
          untenable though.

    - I propose Einstein's thought experiment provided a clear
      way of formulating those objections and supported him in
      his final decision:
        - to give up the `emission` theory in favor of retaining
          the Maxwell-Lorentz theory, but with a radically
          altered theory of space and time.


### 3. An emission theory of Light and Electromagnetism
    - Einstein recalled how prior to his discovery of SR, he had
      investigated emission theories, with a similar approach to
      Walter Ritz. 
    - At that time, in the standard electrodynamics of Maxwell
      and Lorentz, EM action always propagated at `c` with
      respect to the ETHER.
        - The simplest example was the propagation of
          a lightwave. But it also held for the action of one
          charge upon another 
            - (don't understand this. Apr 2023: oh do understand:
              its how the force field around a charge propagates
              at speed `c` and acts on another charge?).
        - This made it seem _impossible_ to conform the principle
          of relativity to electromagnetism.

    - The `ether` supplied a _preferred state of rest_ essential
      to the theory, but incompatible with the idea that all
      inertial states of motion are equivalent.

    - So Ritz in 1908, and Einstein sometime before 1905, tried
      to modify EM theory so that electromagnetic effects are
      always propagated at `c` with respect to the `source` of
      the effect. 
    - If such a theory worked, it would no longer require an
      `ether` state of rest and it could perhaps conform to the
      principle of relativity.

    - The animation below displays the difference. 
        - $MY_IMAGES/physics/EM/Ritz-animation.gif

    - On the _left_, in the Maxwell-Lorentz theory, electromagnetic
      action propagates from a fixed point in the ether. So when
      two charges moving together act on each other, the source
      of the effect felt by one is a fixed point in the ether
      left behind by the moving source. 
        - Since the effect propagates from a point left behind by
          the moving charges, an observer moving with the charges
          can use this fact to determine that the charges are
          moving. 

    - On the _right_, we see the corresponding process in
      a modified "emission" theory, such as devised by Ritz and
      Einstein. 
        - The motion of the source is added to the propagating
          effect. So now the effect propagates isotropically from
          a point that moves with the source. 
        - To see this, notice how the expanding spherical shells
          remain centered on the moving positive charge that is
          their source, just as would happen if the two charges
          were at rest. 
        - The propagation of electromagnetic effects can no
          longer be used by observers moving with the two charges
          to detect their absolute motion; the principle of
          relativity is no longer threatened.


    - The simplest electromagnetic action is the propagation of
      light. So in this theory, the velocity of the _emitter_,
      the source, is _added_ to the velocity of the light
      emitted. For this reason it is known as an "emission"
      theory.
        - Apr 2023: Ah this explains the confusion I had on the
          Nullabour with the torch experiment. I was
          unconsciously asuming an emission theory!

    - Although this initially seemed promising to Einstein, who
      was intent on restoring the relativity principle, he
      ultimately rejected the `emission` theory. 
    - His later correspondence is littered with remarks on the
      problems the theory faced. Two will return as our story
      unfolds.

        1. In a letter to Ehrenfest in 1912 (and elsewhere),
           Einstein remarked that an emission theory ran afoul of
           an elementary result of optics:
            - the physical state of a light ray is completely
              determined by its intensity and color (and
              polarization)

        2. In an interview with Shankland in the 1950s, Einstein
           remarked that an emission theory could not be
           formulated as a _local field theory_ (ie: in terms of
           differential equations).
            - In a local field theory, we reconstruct the time
              evolution of a field by taking its state at one
              instant and consulting the theory's differential
              field equations.
            - These equations take the present state of the
              fields and tell us how rapidly they are changing.
              From these rates of change we can infer the future
              states of the field. 
                - (A similar analysis tells us how the field will
                  alter at different parts of space.)


### 4. Einstein's Thought Experiment: EMISSION theory context

1. The first objection was we don't experience frozen light.
   That's a puzzle in an emission theory of light. We must
   presume there are light sources with all sorts of velocities
   around us. A light source moving rapidly away from us will
   emit a lightwave that propagates slowly with respect to us.
   The most extreme case is of light source moving away from us
   at `c`. That source will leave a frozen light wave behind in
   space, as the animation shows:
    - So, if the emission theory is correct, we'd expect to
      eventually run into frozen lightwaves, emitted by rapidly
      receding sources. But we experience no such thing.

2. The second objection was that frozen light is incompatible
   with Maxwell's equations. Why is this a problem for an
   emission theory when such a theory _does NOT employ_ Maxwells
   equations?
    - An emission theory allows the existence of frozen light
      waves. But the emission theory must agree closely with the
      treatment of static fields in Maxwell's theory which was
      experimentally well confirmed.
    - However Maxwell's theory does not admit static fields that
      correspond to frozen light waves.

3. In his 3rd objection, Einstein lamented an observer catching
   the light beam, "...how should the first observer know or
   determine, that he is in a state of fast uniform motion?"
    - Of course, in an emission theory, a "state of fast uniform
      motion" must be read as:
        - "fast uniform motion wrt the `source` of the light."
    - At first it's not clear why it should matter whether the
      observer catching the light beam can make this judgment.

    - It turns out to be important if the overall emission theory
      is to be _deterministic_; that is, if the present state of
      fields in space are able to determine how they will develop
      in the future. Einstein's worry is that determinism will
      fail.
        - To see why, imagine you are an observer given
          a waveform, but all you know about it is the state of
          it at the _present instant_.
            - Would you be able to tell whether the waveform is
              one frozen in space; or whether it's one
              propagating past you?

        - Both are possible in an emission theory. Which is the
          case depends on your velocity wrt the light's source.
            - If you are moving at `c` wrt the `source`, 
                - the wave is frozen.
            - If you are at rest with respect to the source, 
                - the wave is propagating at `c`.

    - Can you tell which case it is, merely by looking at the
      waveform at an instant? You cannot.

    - Einstein's earlier remark about light is now decisive.
        - A light wave is fully characterized by its color,
          intensity and polarization and both these cases agree
          on these 3 properties.
        - The waveform has no property at an INSTANT that would
          allow you to predict its future time development. This
          is indeterminism.
        - The present state of the wave does not determine its
          future time development.

    - While this circumstance might just be an odd incompleteness
      of our knowledge, it becomes a crisis if we imagine that we
      are not human observers but the differential equations of
      a local field theory.

    - As we saw above, a basic function of those field equations
      is to take the present state of the fields and infer the
      rates of change of the field.
        - The rates of change then determine the time development
          of the waveform; whether it propagates or not and how
          fast it propagates.
        - This essential function will not be possible in an
          emission theory, for the instantaneous state of the
          lightwave does not determine the rates of change of the
          field.

    - Hence, thanks to Einstein's thought experiment, we infer
      that an emission theory cannot be formulated as a local
      field theory.


- We can now summarize the problems with emission theories from
  Einstein's thought experiment:

    - "...I should observe such a beam of light as an
      electromagnetic field _at rest_ though spatially
      oscillating. There seems to be no such thing, however:"

    1. "...neither on the basis of experience..."
            - _Emission Theorist worries:_ An emission theory
              allows frozen waveforms for observers in all
              inertial states of motion, so we should expect to
              experience them.

    2. "...nor according to Maxwell's equations..."
            - _Emission Theorist worries:_ An emission theory
              should agree closely with static fields in
              Maxwell's theory, but Maxwell's theory prohibits
              static fields of frozen light
            - (except in the special case of observers moving at
              `c` with respect to the ether)


3. "... From the very beginning it appeared to me intuitively
   clear that, judged from the standpoint of such an observer,
   everything would have to happen according to the same laws as
   an observer who, relative to the earth, was at rest.
    - For how should the first observer know or be able to
      determine, that he is in a state of fast uniform
      motion?..."
        - _Emission Theorist worries:_ We cannot tell from the
          instantaneous state of a light wave whether it is
          a frozen wave or a propagating wave.
        - So differential field equations also cannot tell,
          implying that an emission theory of light cannot be
          formulated as a local field theory governed by
          differential field equations.

### 5. Conclusion
    - When Einstein abandoned `emission` theories of light, he
      also had to abandon the hope that electrodynamics could be
      conformed to the relativity principle by the normal sorts
      of modifications to electrodynamic theory occupying
      theorists at that time.
    - Instead Einstein knew he must resort to extraordinary
      measures. He was willing to reach his goal by re-examining
      our basic notions of space and time.

    - Einstein concluded the report on his youthful thought
      experiment:
        - "One sees in this paradox, the germ of SR is already
          present. Today everyone knows that all attempts to
          clarify this paradox were condemned to failure so long
          as the axiom of the absolute character of `time`, or of
          `simultaneity`, was rooted unrecognized in the
          unconscious.
        - To recognize clearly this axiom and its arbitrary
          character already implies the essentials of the
          solution of the problem."
