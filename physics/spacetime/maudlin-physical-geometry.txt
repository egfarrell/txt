### Discrete spacetime
    - Topology was initially developed as a tool for describing
      certain spaces — a key example being Euclidean space. 
    - In particular, the spaces most naturally suited for
      topological treatment are continua (we explain continua
      later!). 
    - But the single most important object of which we need
      a geometrical account is physical space(or spacetime), and
      there is noguarantee that physical space is a continuum.
    - Indeed, many physicists believe that ata sufficiently fine
      scale physical space is discrete rather than continuous. If
      standard topology is not an effective tool for articulating
      the geometrical structure of discretespaces, then it may
      not be well suited for the primary requirements of physics.
    - It would, in any case, be preferable to have an account of
      geometrical structure that can be applied with equal ease
      to discrete and continuous spaces. The Theory of Linear
      Structures can be so applied.

### Time as a spatial dimension
    - Relativity is often taken to imply that time is "just
      another dimension" like a spatial dimension, so the notion
      that there is anything physically special about time (as
      opposed to space) is therefore outmoded classical thinking.

    - Relativity is said to postulate a "four-dimensional block
      universe" which is "static", and in which the passage of
      time is just an illusion.

    - In short, Relativity is commonly characterised as having
      _spatialised time_. ... Of having put the temporal
      dimension on an equal physical footing with the spatial
      dimensions, and thereby robbing time of any fundamental
      difference from space.

    - But my contention is just the opposite: Relativity shows,
      for the first time in history, how to _temporalise space_.
      In relativity, but not in any preceding classical theory,
      one can regard time as the basic organising structure of
      space-time.

    - Key: In a precise sense, space-time has geometrical
      structure only because it has temporal structure, and
      insofar as there is spatial geometry at all, it is
      parasitic on temporal structure.

    - We should note that the geometrical role of time in
      structuring space-time is not (at a technical level) at all
      contentious. 
      - The standard account of space-time structure in
        Relativity permits a simple temporal characterisation of
        time-like lines while no parallel characterisation of
        space-like lines exists. The only real bone of contention
        here will be the significance of that fact.

### "Metaphorical" versus "Geometrical" Spaces
    - Almost any collection of object could be considered to form
      a "space". Eg. In Newtons theory of gravity applied to
      point particles, one can speak of the "space of solutions"
      of Newtons equations of motion.

    - Each "point" in this space, each individual element,
      describes the motions of a set of particles governed by
      Newtonian Gravity.

    - There is an intuitive sense - which can be made technically
      precise - in which different solutions can be "closer" or
      "farther" away from one another, and hence an intuitive
      sense in which the whole set of solutions can be thought of
      as having a "geometry".

    - But this sort of talk of a "space" is evidently not
      literal. This "space" is obviously a "metaphorical" space.

    - Analogously, philosophers often speak of "logical space" as
      the set of all possible worlds. But this "space" talk is
      just a picturesque means of discussing various ways and
      degrees that individual possible worlds are similar to each
      other.

    - In contrast, consider Euclidean space. Euclidean space is
      also an abstract object in the way all mathematical objects
      are abstract. But Euclidean space is not just
      metaphorically a "space". When we say one point is
      "closer"to another than to a third, we aren't suggesting
      that the 1st point is more _similar_ to the second than to
      the third. In fact, all points in Euclidean space are
      intrinsically exactly alike: they are all in themselves
      perfectly identical.

    - The points of Euclidean space really are points with no
      internal structure - unlike the "points" of the space of
      solutions to Newtons equations, 

    - The "points" of the space of solutions form
      a (metaphorical) "space" only because they are highly
      structured and different from one another. 
      - The points of Euclidean space, being all intrinsically
        identical, form a space only because of structure that is
        _not_ a function of their intrinsic features. Euclidean
        space is therefore an instance of what I mean by
        a geometrical space.

    - Other examples of geometrical spaces include non-Euclidean
      spaces studied in Riemannian geometry, Minkowski spacetime
      and also the spacetimes that are solutions to the equations
      of GR.

    - In contrast, the set of integers or set of real numbers do
      not from a geometrical space: the elements are not all
      intrinsically alike and the "geometrical" notion of
      "proximity" for example, is determined by their different
      intrinsic natures.
      - The sense that the number 2 is "closer" to the number 1,
        that it is to 100, has to do with the arithmetic nature
        of these objects and not with _any extrinsic structure
        that unites them_.

    - Very interesting point: So why is the characteristic of
      geometrical spaces that all points are intrinsically alike
      not a necessary and sufficient condition to define
      a "geometrical" space? I mean, this fits the notion of
      mathematical spaces that are normally studied under the
      rubic of "geometry"
      - Well the thing is we also the actual _physical_
        space(time) we walk around in to count as a geometrical
        space too. But the points of our physical space are not
        all intrinsically alike - some points may be occupied by
        matter or by certain fields.
      - Even so, these differences of material content do not
        analytically determine the geometry of the space we live.
        The instrinsic structure of two points in _physical_
        spacetime do not determine their geometrical relation to
        one another. (In Einsteins field equation, the
        geometrical structure of spascetime can be mathematically
        specified independently of the matter fields; the
        geometrical structure stands on one side of the equation.
        So as far as geometry is concerned as opposed to the
        physical laws that produce the geometry, the points of
        spacetime are all the "same").
      - In other words, the geometrical structure of real
        spacetime doesnt not _supervene_ on the intrinsic
        features of the points of spacetime. So physical
        spacetime in which we live counts as a geometrical space.
