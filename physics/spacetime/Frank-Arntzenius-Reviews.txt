### Reviews By

# Review: Friedel Weinert (Bradford Uni)

- The book has the enticing title 'Space, Time & Stuff'.
- At first sight it seems to be a contribution to the philosophy
  of space and time. However, the first impression soon
  evaporates. By the author's own admission, this book ended up
  as a text for philosophers with a taste for its heady mixture
  of mathematical physics and fundamental metaphysics and who
  regard this approach as a valuable exploration of epistemic
  possibilities.
- The author invites the reader to see 'this book ... as an
  attempt to reduce all of physics to geometry'. A claim, which
  will raise eyebrows amongst researchers who either work on
  statistical mechanics, thermodynamics and cosmology or
  investigate the foundational problems in these areas.
    - (Readers who are interested in wider philosophical views on
      space and time or the question of 'arrows of time' will be
      disappointed since these topics are left aside.)
- The book claims to be about the structure of space and time, or
  rather space-time, but many other loosely related topics are
  also discussed (metaphysical issues in GR, QM, topology, gauge
  theories, calculus).

- Chapter I assesses Julian Barbour's idea that _time has no
  structure_ a view, which is found to be viable but implausible.
    - In this chapter the author also discusses Newtonian and
      relativistic physics, especially in terms of the
      distinction between the topology and metric of time.
    - For the general reader, and barring some reservations
      mentioned below, this is a more accessible chapter.
    - Especially the discussion of the topology and metric of
      time is useful.

- Chapter II is a discussion of the geometric structure of
  spacetime, and questions if spacetime topology is _separable_.
    - ie what happens in spacetime is entirely determined by:
        - what is the case at EACH POINT in space-time' or
        - it only gives rise to what the author calls
          `neighbourhood separability`.
    - Arntzenius argues in favour of neighbourhood separability:
      a 'topology is separable in the sense that given any
      division of the total space into open neighbourhoods, the
      topology of each of the neighbourhoods determines the
      overall topology'.
    - This view implies that the world, including the classical
      domain, has 'irreducibly holistic properties'.
    - Rather strangely, David Lewis is introduced as 'perhaps the
      best-known proponent' of space-time substantivalism (rather
      than John Earman).
    - Perhaps this attribution reveals the author's metaphysical
      preferences, as various forms of substantivalism, is
      defended throughout the book.

- Chapter III examines QM and asks what quantities should be
  taken as 'fundamental' in a metaphysical sense.
    - Before the author enters the metaphysical jungle of
      varieties of realism and examines 'separability' and the
      fundamental space for QM, he provides an excellent
      discussion of the _basic mathematics of QM_.

- Chapter IV is devoted to the topic of 'pointless spaces':
    - the question whether there are instants of time, points in
      space and space-time or whether space-time consists of
      finitely extended regions.
        - see:
          $MY_NOTES/maths/analysis/Bell-Primer-Infinitesimal-Calculus.txt
    - Although the author regards the latter view as defensible,
      in the end 'pointy spaces' appear preferable to him.
        - Aside: Pointy spaces not possible in Smooth
          Infinitesmal Analysis SIA!
        - $MY_NOTES/maths/analysis/Bell-Primer-Infinitesimal-Calculus.txt
    - A general feature of the book is that many chapters end in
      such inconclusive outcomes, which is probably a consequence
      of investigating epistemic possibilities.

- Chapter V returns to the much discussed debate between
  substantivalism and relationism in space-time theories.
    - Though it defends a version of substantivalism, and
      produces a number of arguments against relationism (the
      hand argument, parity violations) its discussion is sloppy:
        - Newton's bucket experiment is not an argument for space
          but an argument for 'absolute space' and Leibniz's
          relationism does not amount to a denial of space, since
          Leibniz characterizes space as the order of coexisting
          things (and time as the succession of the order of
          coexisting things).
    - This chapter does not engage with Leibniz's own writings
      but repeats the usual, narrow-minded view of relationism as
      a view about spatial-temporal relations between material
      bodies occupying space-time regions.
    - This characterization has become popular in the
      English-speaking world since the work of Friedman and
      Earman and is used by these authors to criticize Leibnizian
      relationism for its claimed inability to ground a proper
      theory of motion.

- Chapter VI discusses the implication of gauge theories for the
  conception of properties ('are velocities properties?') and
  whether we should assume the existence of a fibre-bundle space
  over and above space-time.
    - The notion of `fibre bundles` is well explained.
    - The chapter defends fibre-bundle substantivalism against
      gauge relationism, which is taken to deny fibre bundles.
        - 'Gauge relationism ... is the view that there are no
          fibre bundles, that the states of gauge fields and
          connection fields do not correspond to geometric
          features of fibre bundles, but instead correspond to
          certain _properties of paths_ in space-time'.

- Chapter VII is 'Directions, hands and charges' and discusses
  the CPT theorem which states that 'any relativistic world is
  invariant under spacetime mirroring'. It claims that from this
  valid inferences about space-time can be drawn.
    - But it ends in a now familiar, ambivalent conclusion.

- Chapter VIII distinguishes various versions of nominalism
  (hard, soft) and asks whether physics could be done without the
  help of mathematics.
    - According to the author the 'incursion of mathematics into
      physics' is worrying for two reasons:
        - the relations which mathematical entities bear to
          physical objects, and
        - the very existence of mathematical entities.
    - The author admits that scientists are not interested in
      such nominalistic projects, which attempt to rewrite
      physics in terms of concrete physical entities only.
      Indeed, the chapter sits rather uncomfortably with the
      preceding chapters, which discuss mathematical issues in
      great technical detail.

- The author started with the admission that he failed to write
  an accessible book for 'anybody who is interested in the
  structure of space and time and knows a bit of calculus.'
    - For the general reader interested in the philosophy of
      physics, this books fails on various levels.
    - Despite the often excellent discussions of technical
      issues, the level of explanation is uneven. E.g various
      notions of space-time (Newtonian, Neo-Newtonian, Minkowski,
      relativistic space-times) are introduced in early chapters
      but are not sufficiently distinguished.
        - This makes it hard for the general reader to follow the
          discussion in the valuable first chapter, while
          informed readers will find no new material here.
    - Later on, both the Stern-Gerlach experiment and the
      violation of parity symmetry would have deserved fuller
      expositions.

- The book offers detailed discussions of very arcane subjects:
    - whether time is structureless?
    - whether space-time has a neighbourhood topology?
    - whether velocities are properties?
    - whether pointless spaces can be envisaged?
    - whether space-time and fibre-bundle spaces exist?
- These will interest only a small readership of devotees.
- At the same time, other relevant topics to the question of
  physical time, like thermodynamics, statistical mechanics,
  quantum cosmology, and the important mechanism of decoherence
  are completely ignored.

- Finally the book is based on the naive assumption that the
  metaphysical structure of reality is a _deductive consequence_
  of the mathematical structure of physical theories.
    - E.g., the author wishes to understand quantum theories 'as
      accounts of what there is, fundamentally speaking, and of
      the pattern of fundamental properties and relations'.
    - He repeatedly claims that 'this book is mostly about asking
      what the fundamental structure of the world is'.
        - What is meant by 'fundamental' structure is not
          physical structure.
        - Rather 'what we can and do know about the metaphysical
          and physical possibilities, derives from our knowledge
          of the fundamental objects and predicates, and what the
          fundamental laws are in which they figure'.
    - Unsurprisingly, hardly any chapters end in a clear,
      unambiguous conclusion.
    - The reader is left with a bewildering choice of
      metaphysical positions e.g.:
        - amplitude realism,
        - liberal amplitude realism,
        - density-operator realism,
        - flash realism,
        - various versions of substantivalism (including
          super-substantivalism) and
        - relationism
    - ... all demonstrating that the so-called _metaphysical
      structure of reality cannot simply be read off the
      mathematical structure_ of physical theories.
    - The author seems to concede this point at the end:
        > 'Mathematics is a stupendously useful tool. It
        > describes and systematizes a vast array of possible
        > structures, any one of which we might find ourselves
        > having reason to ascribe to part of the real world. We
        > should feel free to avail ourselves of these options,
        > without fearing that once we start doing so, we will
        > somehow end up having to believe that every one of
        > those structures is instantiated somewhere in reality'.
    - The inflation of metaphysical positions stands in stark
      contrast to the value of simplicity, which the author
      praises as one of the great virtues of science because
      'simple theories are more likely to be true'.

- One chapter is entitled 'Pointlessness'. Many scientifically
  minded readers may feel this could apply to the whole book.
    - However members of the metaphysical club will embrace the
      the blurb 'that when trying to extract the fundamental
      structure of the world from physics theories, one ignores
      philosophy at ones peril!'
    - But this reviewer walked away without lasting damage.


# Review: David John Baker (Michigan University)

- The back of this excellent monograph closes with a bold remark
  on its methodology:
    - "Pivotal: The assumption that the fundamental laws of
      physics are simple in terms of the fundamental physical
      properties and relations.
    - Without this assumption one gets nowhere trying to extract
      the fundamental structure of the world from theories of
      physics."

- I could not have put it better myself. One caveat, though:
    - I am not sure 'simplicity' is the best name for the
      cocktail of considerations appealed here as premises.
- Frank Arntzenius privileges spatiotemporally local and
  separable ontologies, for example, but these strike me as
  virtues of easy conceptual intelligibility or methodological
  conservatism rather than simplicity.
- Regardless, the reader can look forward to a marshaling of
  nonempirical theoretical virtues behind various alternative
  pictures of fundamental reality, in search of the best
  metaphysical explanation for some successes of modern physics.

- Arntzenius offers no worked-out theory of simplicity and the
  other virtues, but here he is in good company (nearly
  everyone's?). In cases where empirically equivalent hypotheses
  are on the table, we must trust that the intuitions of
  scientifically trained scholars encapsulate a rough but
  reliable sense of a theory's standing with respect to
  theoretical virtues.
- Since scientists themselves carry on without any systematic
  picture of these virtues, theoretical science itself could not
  proceed without this assumption.

- Could one ask for a better method? One could, I suppose, but
  it's not easy.
- Given the state of the field at present, the alternatives are
  to abandon the sort of questions this book asks or else use
  something like Arntzenius's method to _tentatively_ answer
  them.
- I like the way Arntzenius proceeds. The reader should just
  remember that these are tentative conclusions with only
  slightly more evidential backing than opposing views.

- The bulk of the book tackles puzzles about space and time:
    - the familiar controversy between substantivalism and
      relationism, the existence of instantaneous velocity, and
      pointless or `"gunky" spaces`.
    - Arntzenius is the leading authority on the latter two
      topics, and his treatment of these issues is very rich.
    - The discussion of substantivalism, relationism, and related
      issues is equally rich, and notably, it largely avoids
      rehearsing tired thought experiments in favor of posing
      interesting new ones.
    - E.g., Arntzenius introduces the character of the "strong
      relationist about time," who holds that there are no
      temporal relations between events at different spatial
      locations.
    - By examining this fascinating straw man, Arntzenius sheds
      considerable light on the virtues and vices of saner
      related positions, such as the view:
        - there are _no spatial relations_ between objects at
          different times.

- This is the book's greatest virtue:
    - it is bursting with great material on central topics that
      cannot be found anywhere else. It opens with a clear,
      minimally technical exposition of Barbour's antirealism
      about time, along with Barbour and Bertotti's method to
      formulate a "timeless" version of Newtonian physics.
    - This is the best introduction to Barbour's physics I know
      of, and anyone interested should begin with Arntzenius's
      first chapter.

- Chapter 7 is a foundational survey of the CPT theorem
  (charge-parity-time reversal) and related issues, another
  essential resource that was previously missing from the
  literature.
    - This chapter is extremely up-to-date, and in particular
      discusses the breakthrough work of Hilary Greaves and
      Teruji Thomas, which has not yet been published.

- Some of the best chapters examine the nature of matter more
  than space or time.
    - Chapter 3, "The World According to QM," is interesting.
      Rather than rehashing which QM interpretation is the best,
      Arntzenius picks one ('many-worlds') and asks which picture
      of microscopic reality is most successful, assuming that
      interpretation's answer to the measurement problem.
    - While the other interpretations are already well understood
      in this regard, in many-worlds this is ongoing work.
    - Unlike other proponents of many-worlds, Arntzenius has the
      sensibilities of an ordinary scientific realist rather than
      a structural realist. So for those of us who and wondering
      what "structure" actually means, his approach is
      refreshing.

- After the competing ontologies are compared, Arntzenius favours
  the "Heisenberg operator realism" of Deutsch and Hayden, with
  Wallace and Timpson's "spacetime state realism" a close second.
    - Both pictures proceed by assigning fundamental quantities
      to regions of space-time:
        - represented by field operators for Deutsch/Hayden
        - and local density operators for Wallace et al in such
          a way that the expectation values of observables can be
          reconstructed as functions of these fundamental
          quantities.
- Arntzenius is judicious in comparing these two ontologies, but
  I'd like to see the story of their empirical adequacy fleshed
  out further.
- I gather his two preferred versions of 'many-worlds' are
  supposed to be empirically equivalent to the ordinary theory
  because one can derive the expectation values of observables.
- But what is an observable quantity like energy or momentum, on
  these pictures, when the fundamental quantities are just given
  by operators assigned to regions?
- And what meaning does a statistical expectation value have in
  contemporary 'many-worlds', which is entirely deterministic and
  involves only a thin notion of probability, as the
  decision-theoretic weight rational agents will assign their
  successors in future branches of the universe's state??
- These are not rhetorical questions, though I think they are
  tough ones. I'd just like to hear more.

- The book closes with what may be its most significant and
  lasting contribution:
    - an attempt (with Cian Dorr) to reduce all the mathematics
      needed for physics to geometry alone.
    - Although the title is "Calculus as Geometry," they do not
      mean to reduce calculus to the geometry of abstract
      mathematical spaces but rather to the geometry of the
      concrete space that physical objects exist in.
    - This approach is inspired in many ways by Hartry Field's
      `Science without Numbers`, but it is not unambiguously
      nominalist in the same sense since it supplements ordinary
      physical space with a fiber bundle over that space, which
      gives the possible values of gauge fields.
    - Moreover, they freely employ mereology in their
      constructions.
    - But as they rightly point out, the game need not be to
      satisfy some ideal of "true" nominalism. Rather, their
      project is to make do with less ontology than the
      traditional mathematical Platonist.

- That said, substantivalism about the fiber bundle is
  a controversial assumption. Although it's probably fair to say
  that such substantivalism is "independently-motivated", it has
  also been the target of powerful objections, e.g. by Richard
  Healey. So we have an interesting contest between the parsimony
  of Arntzenius picture and the theoretical considerations Healey
  raises against fiber bundle substantivalism.

- Equally interesting is Arntzenius and Dorr's suggestion of
  a new, nominalist-friendly theory of quantity according to
  which the values of a physical quantity like `mass` correspond
  to points in "mass space" occupied by physical objects.
    - This is perhaps best understood as a new alternative to
      both _absolutist theories_ of quantity (in which values of
      mass are fundamental monadic properties) and _comparativist
      theories_, in which relations like "twice as massive as"
      are fundamental?.
    - But I think close analogues to the older theories will
      persist. E.g., like the space-time substantivalist, the
      realist about `mass space` must determine whether the
      points in the mass space we live in bear primitive
      relations of identity to points in other possible worlds.
    - If so, the resulting theory will be similar to absolutism,
      in that the possibility in which every object has twice its
      actual mass is a distinct possibility rather than
      a redescription of the actual world. Otherwise, mass space
      theory will be more analogous to comparativism in this
      regard.

- This book is ideal for the advanced reader. I wouldn't really
  recommend it to a beginning student or someone totally
  unfamiliar with related literature. It can be a little fast at
  some points, a little careless at others. But I didn't find any
  places where skimmed details made a difference to the arguments
  and I had a blast reading it. Metaphysicians and philosophers
  of science interested in the metaphysical edge of their field
  will find a lot here that is genuinely new, creative, and
  exciting: not just about space and time. The title promises
  stuff, and the book delivers a lot of great stuff.



# Review: Stephen Weinstein (Perimeter Institute)
- Space, Time, and Stuff FRANK ARNTZENIUS

- A polygraph more than a monograph, this book is an 'examination
  of some loosely related topics' at the place where physics
  meets metaphysics. Among the loosely related topics are
  questions regarding the proper mathematical representations of
  space, time and spacetime and a bit on other 'stuff', mainly
  the interpretation of QM and gauge field theories.
    - Its thematic unity comes from a few methodological
      assumptions, acknowledged in the introduction but neither
      explicated nor defended. Hence, the book will be of
      interest largely to those sympathetic to these precepts.

- Arntzenius is interested in extracting information about the
  nature of space, time and matter from _physical theory_. His
  fundamental assumption is that 'the laws of the world are
  simple in terms of the fundamental objects and predicates'.

- The truth of the world is to be found in discovering the
  simplest formulation of the theory. He thinks that the history
  of science shows us that our best estimate as to the relative
  merits of theories should be based on judgments concerning the
  simplicity or naturalness of theories which are compatible with
  phenomena, not on judgments regarding the genuineness of the
  possibilities that are associated with each theory.

- Though many might agree with the author that judgements
  regarding which possibilities are genuine are not reliable, it
  is not obvious that the history of science shows that
  simplicity (or naturalness etc.) is a guide to truth,
  especially since, as Arntzenius himself acknowledges, the
  history of science is full of empirically successful theories
  that are now known to be false. 
    - Even if it were true that simplicity (or naturalness, or
      something like that) were a useful criterion for theory
      selection, we have no particular reason to think that
      _simple theories_ are true theories.

- Suppose we concede that 'simplicity' is indeed a guide to
  truth, history notwithstanding. Unfortunately we aren't offered
  any account of what simplicity (or naturalness) actually
  amounts to. There is a reference to ongoing, unpublished work
  by Cian Dorr on this important topic, but nothing further. Yet
  in many chapters, _simplicity is appealed to as a criterion_ in
  order to find a winner amongst either conflicting theories or
  conflicting interpretations of a given theory or theoretical
  framework. Thus the plausibility of the author's conclusions
  will depend largely on whether one shares his intuitive
  conception of what counts as simple. Or natural.

- Much of the book is given over to technical exposition, with
  the informal style beloved of metaphysicians who model
  themselves after David Lewis. But the informal tone veers too
  often into glibness, and the presentation is also marred by
  poor mathematical typesetting and sloppy notation (e.g.
  integrals without limits of integration), all of which renders
  the technical content incomprehensible to anyone not already
  familiar with it.

- All of the above is a critique of the general approach to the
  topics and the exposition thereof. Let us now take a brief look
  at the subjects covered, and Arntzenius's take on them.

- Chapter 1 is an examination of the ontological status of
  _time_, whether time can be dispensed with as a fundamental
  entity in classical physics. The author's answer is a qualified
  yes: Arntzenius is impressed with, but not entirely swayed by,
  Julian Barbour's Leibnizian arguments.

- Chapter 2, 'There Goes the Neighbourhood', is a critique of
  Lewis's claim that 'the world is a vast mosaic of local matters
  of fact' consisting of properties instantiated at `points` in
  spacetime. 
    - Arntzenius investigates and concludes that Lewis is wrong,
      really the properties are intrinsic to `neighbourhoods` of
      those points, _infinitesimal_ regions surrounding the
      points. 
    - Arntzenius's straightforward point is that many of the
      properties Lewis is concerned with, such as velocities of
      particles or rates of change of fields, are in fact not
      intrinsic, in the sense of 'intrinsic' given by Lewis, to
      the points themselves. 
    - Rather, they are intrinsic to the infinitesimal
      neighbourhoods around the points. 
    - This point could be made, it seems to me, quite clearly in
      five pages or so, but the author takes the reader on
      a nearly 40 page excursion, including a rather unnecessary
      side-trip into the axioms of topology.
    - Concision is not one of the book's strong suits. Chapter
      2 is atypical of the rest of the book in one respect: 
        - the discussion is not about choice between conflicting
          interpretations of physical theory, but rather
          a critique of a view on offer. Elsewhere, Arntzenius
          attempts to adjudicate between competing
          interpretations.

- Chapter 3 examines the conflicting interpretations of QM, while
  chapter 4 inquires if spacetime really consists of points.

- Chapter 5 takes on the old question about the implications of
  Newtonian and later Einsteinian physics for the existence of
  space (and time): 
    - does space exist, or 
    - is space merely a relation between objects?

- Chapter 6 deals with the interpretation of `gauge theories`,
  a type of field theory used to describe what are currently
  understood to be the fundamental particles and forces (except
  for gravity).

- Chapter 7 examines if space, time, or spacetime have
  a 'handedness' structure, a distinction between right and left,
  and forward and backward in time. 
    - In most of these, considerations of _simplicity_ play
      a central role in distinguishing the preferred
      interpretation or formulation.

- Finally, in chapter 8, 'Calculus as Geometry', we find
  a project that seems fundamentally at odds with previous
  chapters. 
    - Co-authored with Cian Dorr, it explores what the authors
      call a 'nominalization' of a chunk of physics. 
    - The project is driven by a worry that physics should
      fundamentally be about '_concrete physical entities_', and
      these entities should have 'intrinsic structure that has
      nothing to do with relations to the mathematical realm'. 
    - What is odd is that the fundamental objects and predicates
      of the various theories and interpretations till now have
      all had a mathematical character. 
    - Clearly there are other motivations for re-expressing the
      content of physical theories in terms of various
      `'concrete' non-mathematical entities`. 
    - But there is no a-priori reason to think that such
      a re-expression would yield a more simple theory in terms
      of its fundamental objects and properties. 
    - And if it is not simple, then under the methodological
      precepts adopted, it is not a legitimate guide to the truth
      about nature.

- In any case, Arntzenius and Dorr distinguish the 'easy
  nominalistic project', in which the intrinsic properties of the
  `entities` are non-mathematical, from the 'hard nominalistic
  project', in which the `laws` governing these properties are
  also stated in non-mathematical terms. 
    - What they mean by 'non-mathematical' is actually
      geometrical in the sense of Euclid's geometry (rather than
      Cartesian analytic geometry), since Euclid's axioms can be
      stated entirely in terms of 'concrete' objects and their
      relations; 
        - these relations do not involve talk of real numbers,
          groups, or other paraphernalia used in modern analysis. 
    - Since the usual formulation of calculus does rely heavily
      on this sort of apparatus, they take on the project of
      re-expressing calculus in purely geometrical terms. This
      takes up the bulk of chapter 8.

- The conclusion of chapter 8 (and the book as a whole) is so
  modest that one wonders why the book was written at all.
    - The authors acknowledge it might be thought that 'the
      theories we're developing are much more complex than
      familiar platonistic ones' but suggest that the
      mathematical theories they are intended to displace
      actually have the 'complexity of platonistic theories',
      albeit a complexity 'hidden behind a hierarchy of
      definitions, which practitioners rarely have a need to
      consult'. 
    - And so it may be, but one might think then that some sort
      of sense needs to be made of measuring the complexity or
      simplicity (or naturalness) of the theories at issue.
    - Indeed, this might be a case in which _simplicity_ pulls in
      one direction (towards theories expressed in mathematical
      language) while _naturalness_ pulls in another.

- But rather than venture into these waters, Arntzenius and Dorr
  simply opine: 
    - 'We think it is a mistake to think of `mathematical
      ontology` as an all-or-nothing deal', and we should feel
      free to avail ourselves of mathematical structure 'without
      fearing that once we start doing so, we'll somehow end up
      having to believe that every one of those structures is
      instantiated somewhere in reality'.

- As the authors avoid adjudicating the ontological questions
  stemming from a successful nominalization / geometrization of
  physics, this reviewer shrugs his shoulders and wonders what
  the point of the book, might be in the first place.
