- important:
- In the course of it, he introduced the so-called cosmological
  constant (Einstein 1917b) hoping it would ensure his theory
  satisfied what he named "Mach's principle" (Einstein 1918e):
    - i.e. the requirement that the metric field (and thereby the
      space-time structure) be fully determined by its material
      SOURCES.
    - In that case, all reference to motion with respect to
      space-time can be read as shorthand for motion with respect
      to the matter generating the metric field.
    - This, in turn, would render all motion relative.

- With the 1915 version of the field equations, as Dutch
  astronomer Willem De Sitter pointed out in 1916, _ABSOLUTE
  MOTION_ does actually survive because the metric field is
  determined not only by material sources but also by `boundary
  conditions` (values of the metric field at spatial infinity)

- Einstein's ingenious response was to propose the universe is
  spatially closed so it would have no boundary (Einstein 1917b).
  He gave a simple example of such a universe, which is both
  closed and static.
    - However the metric field describing the space-time geometry
      of this universe, _was not a solution_ of the field
      equations of his 1915 theory.
    - To convert it into a solution, he had to add a term with
      the cosmological constant to these field equations.
        - This term describes a gravitational _push_ compensating
          the usual gravitational pull to ensure that his static
          universe would not collapse.

- Einstein believed the amended field equations wouldn't allow
  any solutions without matter and would therefore satisfy Mach's
  principle.
    - However De Sitter quickly produced a VACUUM SOLUTION of
      these new field equations. Einstein tried to find fault
      with this solution but in 1918 had to concede it was
      unobjectionable.

- This meant yet another attempt _to relativize all motion_ had
  failed.
    - However, as Christopher Smeenk shows, Einstein's and De
      Sitter's models for a global space-time geometry of the
      universe nonetheless ended up launching the field of
      'relativistic cosmology'.

- More recently, with the discovery of the accelerated expansion
  of the universe, even the cosmological constant has made
  a comeback, despite Einstein calling it his biggest blunder.
  (Gamow 1970, 14950).
    - These developments illustrate how Einstein's failed crusade
      against _ABSOLUTE MOTION_ still resulted in major
      contributions to physics.

- It may have helped Einstein to accept the failure of Mach's
  principle when in 1918, he realized it was based on a view of
  matter and space-time relations that had become anachronistic
  by then.
    - Both matter and space-time were represented by fields in
      Einstein's view, the EM field and the metric field.
    - Mach's principle thus amounted to the requirement that one
      field be reduced to another. (??)
    - Another option was to search for a theory to unify both
      fields,
        - _just as SR had unified the electric and magnetic field
          into an EM field_
        - and GR had united gravity and inertia into a single
          inertio-gravitational field, represented by the metric
          field.

- Whether or not this consideration actually played a role, in
  the early 1920s Einstein embarked on the search for a unified
  field theory modeled on GR.
    - He never found a theory that satisfied him, but his
      attempts continued during the last three decades of his
      life.

- Einstein's strategy while searching for a unified field theory
  is markedly different from his earlier “fixed points” strategy.

- Now he was searching for a “constructive theory.” He had
  preferred such theories all along.
    - When he introduced the distinction between principle
      / constructive theories, he wrote:
        > “When we say we understand a group of natural
        > processes, we invariably mean a constructive theory has
        > been found which covers the processes in question”

    - A physical theory is satisfactory only if its structures

    - The theory of relativity is ultimately as little

    - (Einstein to Arnold Sommerfeld , January 14, 1908 [CPAE 5,

  started looking for a “principle theory” because he was unable
  to find “the true laws by means of constructive efforts”

  he appears to have been confident the theory he was looking for

### --- Page 24 ---


- Physical considerations, energy-momentum conservation,
  agreement with Newton's theory in the appropriate limit, the
  _analogy_ between the metric and electromagnetic field had all
  guided Einstein in the original formulation of the Entwurf
  field equations, and also in their further analysis.

- However, as documented in the Zurich Notebook, Einstein had
  only settled on the “physical strategy” leading him to these
  equations after a different “mathematical strategy” had failed
  (Renn 2007a, Vol. 1, 10).

- The 'mathematical strategy' meant extracting mathematically
  attractive candidate field equations from the Riemann curvature
  tensor and checking if these candidates were acceptable from
  a physics point of view.

- By 1912/13 Einstein concluded that none of them were. 
    - However in November 1915 he resurrected one of these
      rejected equations to replace the discredited Entwurf
      equations (Einstein 1915f).
    - It thus looked as if he had suddenly returned to the
      mathematical strategy; perhaps because he worried that
      Hilbert might otherwise beat him to the punch.
    - Many of Einstein's statements, made at the time and later
      with years of hindsight, confirm such a switch in strategy
      in late 1915 and the episode has been presented accordingly
      in the literature (see, especially, Norton 2000).
    - This would seem to be the full story behind Einstein's
      claim that GR had made him “into a believing rationalist.”

    - However, Janssen and Renn (2007) have argued that it was
      the physical strategy, not the mathematical one, that led
      Einstein back to the rejected Zurich notebook field
      equations.
        - in this interpretation, Einstein retained the formalism
          of the Entwurf theory and changed only one element:
            - the definition of the gravitational field in terms
              of `gradients` of the gravitational potentials, the
              components of the metric tensor field.
                - (which are the components: the 'gradients' or
                  the 'potentials'?)
            - There is strong textual evidence for this
              reconstruction.
            - In particular, Einstein identified his old
              definition of the gravitational field as a “fatal
              prejudice” and his new one as “the key to the
              solution.”
        - However in his presentation of the new field equations,
          Einstein (1915f) strongly suggested that the new theory
          amounted to a wholesale replacement of the old one and
          put great emphasis on the mathematical elegance of his
          rediscovered field equations.
        - Janssen and Renn (2007, 841) argue this makes it
          understandable that Einstein would come to misremember
          the completion of GR as a triumph for his mathematical
          strategy.
        - This case of selective amnesia would serve him well
          later when he had to convince himself and others that
          his strategy for finding a unified field theory could
          boast of at least one spectacular success.

- Einstein clearly recognized that such principles cannot be
  found through simple induction from experimental data.
- “The scientist,” Einstein wrote, “has to WORM these general
  principles out of nature by perceiving certain general features
  (capable of precise formulation) out of comprehensive complexes

    - be grounded in “comprehensive complexes of empirical facts”
    - and must “permit precise formulation.”

### --- Page 33 ---


- Principle theories are based on generalizations of _empirical
  laws_ that are presumed to be universally valid, such as the
  1st and 2nd laws of thermodynamics.
- Such fundamental principles are not hypothetical, postulating
  an underlying mechanism, and are therefore empirically better
  confirmed.
- Furthermore, they allow for logically complete deductions of
  more specific physical laws, such as the deduction of Wien's
  displacement law from the laws of thermodynamics.
- HOWEVER, they do not lead to a sense of _understanding_ natural
  processes like constructive theories do.

- He criticized the “inductive method” central to Mach's
  Scientific philosophy which assumes that fundamental principles
  result from step-by-step generalization of individual empirical
  data.

- Echoing a passage from the lecture quoted previously, he
  stressed there is no general method for the formulation of
  fundamental principles, but that such principles result from
  the “intuitive grasp of the essentials of a large complex of
  facts” (Einstein 1919g).

- This implies that fundamental principles cannot be proven from
  the facts; they can only be falsified if an empirical fact is
  in conflict with a consequence of the theory.

- Einstein's stress on the _holism of theories_, as opposed to
  the testability of individual statements, comes close to
  Duhem's view of theories (cf. note 66), as Howard discusses in
  Chapter 11.

- By the time of the Herbert Spencer lecture fifteen years later,
  Einstein had lost confidence that the “intuitive grasp of the
  essentials of a large complex of facts” is a reliable guide to
  the fundamental principles at the heart of a theory.

- Einstein gives the example of gravitational theory, where the
  “sympathetic understanding of experience” produced two
  completely different theoretical systems, the
  action-at-a-distance forces of Newtonian theory and the
  space-time curvature of GR (Einstein 1933b, 2734).


### --- Page 34 ---


- Einstein's belief that theories are free creations of the human
  mind that are _constrained but not determined_ by empirical
  data is close to Poincaré's conventionalism, which also had
  a major influence on Schlick.

- Einstein followed this interpretation, up to a certain point,
  in “Geometry and Experience” (Einstein 1921c) and took over
  Schlick's distinction between
    - a _PURE GEOMETRY_, which is axiomatic in form and
      analytically true, and
    - a _PHYSICAL GEOMETRY_, which is defined by “coordinating
      definitions” that connect geometrical concepts to physical
      entities.
    - Physical geometry thus becomes an empirical science which
      can be falsified by experiment.

- Poincaré had postulated that these coordinating definitions are
  purely conventional and concluded that geometry is immune to
  empirical refutation:
    - one could always modify the coordinating definitions to
      preserve the simplicity of Euclidean geometry.
    - Schlick agreed with Poincaré that the choice of geometry is
      conventional, but justified non-Euclidean geometry in GR
      because what matters is the maximal simplicity of BOTH
      geometry and physics, NOT the maximal simplicity of
      geometry by itself.
    - However, as Friedman's contribution shows, Einstein
      distanced himself from the Poincaré-Schlick position
      stating that, although correct in principle, this position
      would never have allowed him to find GR. (Einstein 1949b,
      678)
    - Einstein maintains we are not free to redefine our concepts
      of space and time at will, at least not at this time in our
      development.
    - He argues that his thought experiment of the rotating disk
      depended on a physical concept of rigid rods and clocks,
      that is, the assumption about what we call space and time
      MEASUREMENTS is not purely conventional, but physically
      meaningful.
    - This caveat illuminates the thinking behind Einstein's
      critique of Weyl's proposal for a unified field theory.
        - Einstein judged it as formally beautiful, but
          unsatisfactory since it did not agree with a physical
          definition of oscillating atoms as clocks.

    - "_epistemo-logical realism_" likely refers to
      a philosophical position that emphasizes the importance of
      knowledge and understanding based on `objective reality`. 
        - It might imply a belief that knowledge should be
          grounded in the real world, and that our understanding
          of reality should be based on _empirical evidence_ and
          _rational inquiry_.
    - Einstein apparently repudiated or rejected this philosophy
      for the rest of his life, meaning he did not subscribe to
      or support the idea that knowledge should be solely based
      on an objective, independent reality. Instead, his approach
      to heuristics was based on mathematics but differed from
      the stance of epistemological realism.

- Einstein's critique of QM was not based on epistemological
  realism, but rather on methodological lessons he had learned
  from his earlier work in relativity and statistical mechanics.

- Guided by his understanding of relativity theory as a theory of
  invariants, Einstein postulated that the physics within QM
  needs to distinguish between objective facts and their
  non-objective descriptions.

- Lehner calls Einstein's position "methodological realism" and
  identifies this as the root of Einstein's conviction that QM
  was only a _phenomenological statistical description_ and not
  a complete theory.

- In this context, his critique of positivism received new
  urgency, as defenders of the emerging Copenhagen orthodoxy
  explicitly invoked positivism to avoid the questions that
  Einstein wanted to pose.

- A summary of Einstein's positions on both QM and positivism can
  be found in his “Reply to Criticism” in the Schilpp volume.
  (Einstein 1949b).

- Here, Einstein explicitly expresses his rapprochement to Kant's
  _transcendental idealism_ (ibid., 674, 678, 680), which Thomas
  Ryckman discusses in Chapter 12.

- As Ryckman reports, this reappraisal of Kant has a long
  prehistory which reaches back into Einstein's work on GR and
  his dissatisfaction with a positivistic theory of science.
    - In the end, Einstein could see Kant as an inspiration for
      finding a _third position_ beyond the antagonistic poles of
      realism and positivism.

- The “narrow whirlpool of personal experience” from which
  Einstein periodically felt the urge to escape had greatly
  expanded in the wake of the British solar eclipse expeditions
  of 1919, led by Arthur S. Eddington, which confirmed the
  prediction of light bending of general relativity.4
    - Some authors, notably Earman and Glymour (1980a), have
      argued that the observations were inconclusive.
    - For an opposing view, see Kennefick (2009a, 2009b); see
      also CPAE 9, Introduction, Sec. III.

- For the reception of GR by the astronomy community, especially
  in the USA, see Crelinsten (2006).

- Light bending constitutes one of the three “classic tests” of
  GR, the other two being the:
    - gravitational redshift
        - (Earman and Glymour 1980b ; CPAE 9, Intro, Sec. IV)
    - anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion
        - (Earman and Janssen 1993, Janssen 2003a).

- Einstein did not see these “classic tests” as the best evidence
  for his theory. In a way reminiscent of his reaction to
  Kaufmann's alleged refutation of SR, he told a correspondents
  decades later:
    - “Even if the deflection of light, the perihelial movement
      or line shift were unknown, the gravitation equations would
      still be convincing because _they avoid the inertial
      system_ (the phantom which affects everything but is not
      itself affected).
    - It is really rather strange that humans are normally deaf
      to the strongest arguments, while always inclined to
      overestimate measuring accuracies” (Einstein to Born, May
      12, 1952; Born 1971, 192).


### --- Page 45 ---

- A _Copernicus process_ can be thought of as the
  _reorganization_ of a system of knowledge in which previously
  marginal elements take on a key role and serve as a starting
  point for a REINTERPRETATION of the body of knowledge.
- Typically _much of the technical apparatus is kept_, inference
  structures are reversed, and the previous conceptual foundation
  is discarded.
- Einstein's achievements during his miracle year of 1905 can be
  described in terms of such a Copernicus process.


### --- Page 46 ---

- In his Autobiographical Notes, Einstein (1949a, 1453) describes
  how he probed the foundations of physics from 1895-1905,
  beginning with critiques of mechanics and electrodynamics,
  found them inadequate, and then tried to construct an
  alternative foundation based on known experimental facts.

- Initially, the endeavor only led to frustration and despair.
  Yet out of this emerged the annus mirabilis.
    - The three papers completed in the spring of 1905
      collectively represent a consolidation of lessons learned
      and insights gained in this failed enterprise.

- The interpretation of probabilities as _time averages_ led him
  to consider fluctuations, which became the central tool in his
  attempts to understand microphysics.

- Rynasiewicz and Renn (2006) have speculated that prior to 1905
  Einstein was trying to lay a new atomistic foundation for all

- In the end, Einstein could see Kant as an inspiration for
  finding a third position beyond the antagonistic positions of

- There's an even weightier argument that has not been considered
  in this context.
    - QM confirms the _locality_ of all observable physical
      processes, as demonstrated by the various no-signaling
      theorems, which show that entanglement cannot be used for
      the transfer of information.
    - However, this means that the nonlocality of the
      interpretations entirely _resides in the theoretical
      structure_ and not in the phenomena.

- Einstein was remembered as the developer of relativity,
  a theory which challenged the scientific and philosophical
  orthodoxy and required a _new kind of empiricism_ (Schlick
  1921), whereby one could defend the empirical integrity of
  relativity theory against challenges mainly coming from
  Kantians.

- Second, relativity theory was wrongly cited as validating
  central tenets of logical-empiricism, such as
  _verificationism_, and Einstein was wrongly represented as
  having endorsed those same philosophical principles.
