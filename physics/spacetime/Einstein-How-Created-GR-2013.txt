### --- Page 6 ---

- Thus we arrive at the _non-causal_ concept of inertia, and
  there is no need to posit some infinite internal power

- Today’s definition of inertia in any textbook is a direct
  descendent of Newton’s. Inertia IS STILL a non-causal concept
  of motion.
    - From this point of view there is no answer to the question,
      “What is inertia?” – beyond its role as a description of
      how bodies behave when moving or at rest.

- This brings us back to the idea of relativity:
    - for the concept of inertia is the keystone of the principle
      of relativity of motion, along with Galileo’s descriptive
      experience of things (butterflies, dripping water, and the
      like) in the cabin of a moving ship.


### --- Page 7 ---

- Furthermore this experience applies to any frame of reference
  under the same conditions as the cabin of a ship, and hereafter
  the cabin or any such dwelling will be called a “system.”

- Surely most readers can recall similar experiences of the
  relativity of motion in a car, train, or airplane.

- Einstein thus bestowed the expression “_inertial system_” to
  any such defined space moving at a constant speed (or at rest),
  and this abstract term will be used throughout this book.

- This idea of an inertial system nicely encompasses the idea of
  the relativity of motion, since there is no way to detect or
  know absolutely whether the inertial system within which one is
  dwelling is MOVING or not.
    - Or alternatively, there is no method of knowing what is
      really at rest: you or the outside world.


### --- Page 92 ---

- Thus there are two concepts of mass,
    - gravitational and
    - inertial

- As we saw in Galileo’s deduction on falling bodies, his insight
  into how falling bodies accelerate implied (in our new
  terminology) a proportionality between gravitational and
  inertial mass

- (Fig. 11.2 was a visual way of saying the same thing).
    - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/fall-same-rate.jpg

- So, the two masses seem to be identical, but are they?

- Their identification was part of the theoretical structure of
  Newton’s physics, but he wanted to test it empirically.
    - He did so by using pendulums (the bobs being the masses).
    - In an oscillating pendulum both concepts of the masses come
      into play:
        - the _gravitational_ mass,
            - since the bob is falling under gravity,
        - and _inertial_ mass
            - since the bob of mass `m` is moving back and forth
              in an arc

- Newton made two identical pendulums etc etc (never knew he
  actually tested that gravitational = inertial mass)

### --- Page 225 ---

- All of nature, matter and light, seemed to be _dualistic_,
  presenting both wave-like and particle-like behavior.
  Physicists were now confronted with deeper questions:
    - How can these opposites be reconciled?

- What does this _dualism_, along with the need for a statistical
  understanding of phenomena, tell us about the world?

- Einstein said in a 1927 lecture:
    > “What nature demands from us is not a quantum [particle]
    > theory or a wave theory; rather, nature demands a SYNTHESIS
    > of these two views which thus far has exceeded the mental
    > powers of physicists.”

- This limitation he saw as epistemological; for him, this
  dualism was about our limited knowledge of reality, as was the
  role of statistics.
    - Nature itself was neither particle-like nor wave-like.
      There was a deeper reality presently beyond our
      comprehension.

- A real or complete theory would produce "a _synthesis_ of the
  two views", Einstein believed.



### --- Page 231 ---

- key: "antipostivist creed"... and new foundations needed!

- At Oxford University in 1933 Einstein delivered the Spencer
  lecture, from which was quoted the antipostivist creed:
    > “I hold it true that pure thought can grasp reality, as the
    > ancients dreamed.”

- He also pointed out a potential historical parallel:
    - From the 19th to the 20th century classical mechanics gave
      way to relativistic mechanics; importantly, the latter did
      not replace the former but rather extended it further into
      the realms of objects traveling near the speed of light,
      where the old mechanics did not apply.

    - Therefore in retrospect the old mechanics was incomplete
      and required a _modification_ by relativity.
        - For example, relativistic effects reduce to classical
          physics when the speed of an object is much slower than
          light-speed.

    - QM was also incomplete (Einstein thought) and required
      a _modification_, which a new theory would impart.

    - But the crucial point is: even though relativity in the end
      produced a modified version of Newtonian physics, it did
      not _emerge_ from classical physics;
        - That is, Einstein did not begin with the old physics
          and modify it to produce relativity, although initially
          he did try such tactics.
        - Instead he went _back to basics_, setting an entirely
          new foundation, with a new set of postulates.

    - Only through this new path was it possible to derive the
      relativistic modifications of classical physics.
    - In view of that – and assuming an historical parallel,
      Einstein believed that a similar process of setting an
      _entirely new foundation_ was necessary for the eventual
      modifications of quantum physics.
