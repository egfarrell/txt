### Importance of Reflection
    - Question: How do we identify aspects of our physical
      knowledge, as knowledge of space and time?
        - How do we understand features of our experience as
          indicating spatio-temporal relations?
        - How do the laws of physics reveal something to us about
          the nature of space and time?
    - I see TWO compelling reasons to focus on these questions.

    - ON THE ONE HAND, I believe it'll give us a more
      illuminating picture of the connection between
        - the metaphysics of space and time
        - and the development of philosophy in general.
        - Historically, there have been two main attempts to
          integrate the physics and philosophy of space and time
          with a general theory of knowledge:
            - Kant’s critical philosophy: attempting to
              comprehend Euclid and Newton within a theory of the
              'synthetic a priori'
            - logical positivism: attempting to comprehend
              Einstein within a 'conventionalist' view.
        - These attempts are widely recognized as failures, and
          I don’t intend to rehabilitate them.
        - But I believe insight can be gained from a better
          understanding of why they failed.
        - I hope to show the task they failed in, to explain
          the peculiar character of theories of space and time,
          and the _peculiar_ role they have played as
          presuppositions for the empirical theories of physics,
          is as important for us as it was for them. Moreover, is
          more nearly within our grasp.

    - ON THE OTHER HAND, I believe that focusing on these
      questions will illuminate the history of physics.
        - I hope to show that the moments when such questions
          became most urgent are exactly the most _revolutionary_
          moments in the history of space-time physics.
        - The great conceptual transformations brought about by
          Newton, Einstein, and their fellows simply couldn't
          have happened as they did,k without profound reflection
          on these very questions.


### Kindle annotations
    - Added on Wednesday, September 19, 2012, 12:46 AM

    - Indeed, the empirical success of physics was itself made
      possible, in some part, by the achievements of that
      philosophical effort. 
        - This effort aimed at defining spatio-temporal concepts
          in `empirically` meaningful ways, often in defiance of
          the prevailing philosophical understanding of those
          concepts.

    - Newton's theory of absolute space and time was not a mere
      metaphysical appendage to his physics. 
        - It had some genuine foundation in the laws of motion. 
        - _GR did not "relativize" all motion._ 
        - Both theories characterize space-time geometry as an
          objective physical structure.

    - The debate between relationalism and absolutism (or
      substantivalism) effectively reduces to the question: 
        - which of these positions is best supported by current
          physics?

    - How is _objective_ knowledge of spatial and temporal
      relations, let alone of "space itself" or "time itself,"
      possible? 
        - It cannot be the object of such an analysis itself. 
        - We cannot perform tests to see whether forces conform
          to the principle, for it is a criterion by which we
          identify force in the first place.

    - Rather than being mere metaphysical baggage carried by an
      otherwise empirically successful theory, it was inseparable
      from Newton's effort to define the empirically measurable
      quantities of classical mechanics.

    - The essential feature of GR, on this view, was not that it
      ELIMINATES the idea of a _privileged coordinate system_ but
      that it represents the geometry of space-time as a function
      of the mass-energy distribution.

    - Within the philosophy of science, this view, and the
      weaknesses of the positivists' view, first came into
      prominence with the publication of: 
        - Stein's "Newtonian space-time" (1967).
