- The conclusion that projectiles do NOT need a mover is
  inevitable.
    - Once it becomes clear that projectiles move on their own,
      not by a medium, then Aristotle’s view, that everything in
      motion must be moved by something, is essentially finished.
    - The motion of an object which moves on its own is now
      called motion by inertia.
    - It is controversial whether Galileo clearly realized the
      idea of inertial motion.
    - However, what ultimately matters is the essence of his
      arguments: that a body left on its own moves on its own and
      does not need a constant mover. And this is the very core
      of the fundamental idea of inertia.
    - Galileo had tried to answer the question of why free bodies
      would continue to move on its own forever, provided nothing
      prevents them from doing so, by assuming that the continued
      motion of a projectile is impressed upon it by its thrower.
    - We have not done better than him – inertia still continues
      to be an _outstanding puzzle_ in physics.
    - The inertial motion of a body involves two questions:
        - why does a free body move uniformly forever?
        - why does a body resist the change in its uniform motion
          when it encounters an obstacle?

- Therefore the motion of a body cannot be discovered by
  performing mechanical experiments on the moving body itself
  (the type of experiments Galileo considered).
    - Now this conclusion, derived from experimental facts, is
      Galileo’s principle of relativity:
        - by performing mechanical experiments, the uniform
          motion of a body cannot be detected.

- We will apply Galileo’s approach to studying the physical
  meaning of his principle of relativity.
    - As we will see, the exploration of the internal logic of
      Galileo’s principle of relativity will reveal its profound
      depth and help us arrive at the unexpected result that _SR
      is logically contained in Galileo’s relativity principle_
      (more specifically its 4D formulation given by Hermann
      Minkowski).
    - In other words, the basic knowledge about the 4D
      representation of SR had been hidden as one of the
      logically possible interpretations of the physical meaning
      of Galileo’s principle of relativity.
    - Such a result implies that SR could have been discovered
      earlier.

- Galileo’s principle of relativity simply reports the NULL
  results of experiments intended to detect the uniform motion of
  an object, but does not answer the fundamental question of WHY
  we cannot discover that motion.

- The way the principle is stated, by performing mechanical
  experiments we cannot detect our uniform motion in space, seems
  to imply that there is such a motion but we cannot discover it.
    - At the very moment we start to ask this type of question,
      which marks the beginning of what we called exploring the
      internal logic of Galileo’s relativity principle, we are
      overwhelmed by an avalanche of other questions and apparent
      paradoxes.

- Is space an entity?
    - Since the time of Newton and Leibnitz there has been
      a continued debate over the nature of space.
    - There exist two opposing views
        - absolutism: space is a substance (substantivalism) and
        - relationism: space is a collection of relations between
          physical objects.
    - We will not enter that debate here but, in order to avoid
      semantic misunderstandings, we make it clear that by space
      we will understand the _entity_ in which the Earth moves.
    - One cannot deny the existence of that entity by claiming
      that it is merely nothingness or just a set of relations
      between the Earth and the other celestial bodies.
    - Such a denial must obviously answer the old argument:
        - E.g., if there were nothing between the Earth and the
          Moon, why would they not be touching each other?
        - The relationists must also explain how the Earth can
          move in a collection of relations between objects.

- One may be tempted to postulate that space is a different kind
  of medium which does not resist the motion of objects, and for
  this reason no mover will be necessary.
    - The obvious question from such an ad-hoc hypothesis is how
      a medium (one of whose basic features is to impede the
      motion of any object) could offer no resistance to moving
      objects.

- A natural beginning would be to try to understand the physical
  meaning of his relativity principle by asking why it is
  impossible to discover the uniform motion of the Earth in
  space, if it is moving, or the uniform motion of a ship
  (everyone sees that the ship is moving in space).
- After some discussion they would arrive at the only two
  logically possible interpretations:
    - There IS absolute uniform motion but we cannot detect it.
    - There ISN'T absolute uniform motion and that's why we
      cannot detect it.

- It does seem logical to interpret Galileo’s principle of
  relativity to mean that we cannot discover absolute uniform
  motion because it does not exist.
    - This interpretation is more logical, since if uniform
      motion in space doesn't exist it immediately becomes clear
      why it cannot be detected.
    - But if there is no ABSOLUTE uniform motion, it follows that
      there is no UNIFORM MOTION at all because absolute uniform
      motion is motion in space and if absolute uniform motion
      does not exist it means that uniform motion in space does
      not exist either. (Hmmm ?)
    - In such a case the only motion possible would be
      accelerated motion since such a motion can be detected due
      to the existence of inertial forces.
        - (We all know this from our everyday experience; the
          question of the reality of inertial forces will be
          discussed specifically in Chap. 9.)
    - In terms of being detectable, accelerated motion is
      absolute, which seems to imply it is motion with respect to
      an absolute space.
    - Such a conclusion appears to support the traditional
      research team since, if absolute accelerated motion exists,
      absolute uniform motion should also exist – we all see that
      both accelerated and uniformly moving bodies move in space.
        - Wait: I thought he just said above uniform motion in
          space DOESNT exist?

- We shall examine the question of whether absolute accelerated
  motion requires an absolute space in Chap. 4 and Chap. 9,

- The first interpretation of Galileo’s relativity principle,
  that absolute uniform motion exists but cannot be detected,
  seems to lead to the following paradoxical situation:
    - we can say that an object moves in space (since we cannot
      deny the obvious), but it would be incorrect to say that
      the object moves WITH RESPECT TO space
        - (since ‘with respect to space’ implies that the
          object’s motion can be measured as in the case of an
          object moving with respect to another object).

- This situation appears to be paradoxical because our everyday
  experience tells us that ‘motion in something’ is equivalent to
  ‘motion with respect to something’.
    - That is why, as space is something, an entity, it does
      appear to follow that motion _in it_ should be equivalent
      to motion _with respect to it_.
    - As we will see shortly, this is one of the many instances
      where our everyday experience has provided us with reliable
      knowledge.

- After they have formulated all logical possibilities, they can
  turn to their common sense on again.
    - Such a strategy, which requires a lot of dedication to
      achieve, may help them realize that a single implicit
      assumption, _THAT THERE IS JUST ONE SPACE_, is the cause of
      the paradox they face: that there is no uniform motion at
      all if there is no absolute uniform motion.

- It would not require much analysis to realize that the
  possibility ‘there is no absolute space’ also has two possible
  meanings:
    - there is no space, and
    - space is not absolute.
- As it is clear that objects are separated by something, it
  looks unrealistic, even to the radical research team, to
  declare that space does not exist.
- Then the only remaining meaning is that space is not absolute
  in the sense that it is NOT A COMMON SPACE to everyone.
- Therefore ‘space is NOT absolute’ means that the idea there is
  only one space does not exist.

- The radical research team realize that they have managed to
  identify the implicit assumption, that only one space exists,
  caused the paradox they faced.
- So they decide to replace it with the explicit alternative: the
  assumption that there exist many spaces.
- Then it becomes immediately clear that the paradox disappears:
  uniform motion can still exist but it is not an absolute
  uniform motion, which is by definition uniform motion with
  respect to the absolute space, meaning with respect to the
  single space.
- An object can be at rest in one space, but moving uniformly at
  different speeds in other spaces.

- As they expected, their traditional colleagues did take it for
  granted that only one space existed, which appeared to have led
  to the paradox.
- If space were one thing (one entity), the expressions ‘motion
  in it’ and ‘motion with respect to it’ should be equivalent.
- But they are not, which indicates that the implicit assumption,
  there is one space, might have caused the paradox.

- However, to have a logical resolution of a paradox is one
  thing, but to be able to translate that resolution in terms of
  our everyday experience is quite another.
- It is easy to say that there are more spaces, but we all
  perceive the physical objects as existing in a SINGLE 3D space.
- That is why it is really difficult to consider seriously the
  existence of more 3D spaces.
- The traditional researchers may even try to ridicule their
  radical colleagues by pointing out that it is quite obvious
  that one space exists, since we only perceive one space.

- The radical research team has already gained sufficient
  experience dealing with such apparently obvious contradictions
  in what we perceive.
- So, they tell their traditional opponents:
    - “Let us see what we really perceive.”
- We see objects at different distances from us and believe that
  all of them exist in the same 3D space.
- However, in 1676 Romer measured that light was propagating at
  a finite speed, which demonstrated that we see only past images
  of objects, since it takes time for the light from those
  objects to reach our eyes.
- As a physical 3D space is defined as:
    - ALL SPACE POINTS EXISTING SIMULTANEOUSLY at a given moment
      of time,  (hmm.. think about this)
    - it follows that objects we see at different distances from
      us have, in fact, existed in different 3D spaces belonging
      to different moments of time (see Fig. 3.1).

- Therefore what we ‘perceive’ through the objects we see is not
  a space at all
    - the same 3D ‘space’ we ‘perceive’ (the inclined lines in
      Fig. 3.2) is in fact a set of fragments from _different_ 3D
      spaces corresponding to different moments of time.
    - As Fig. 3.2 clearly shows, the belief that we perceive
      objects occupying the same 3D space is WRONG.

- But the different 3D spaces corresponding to different moments
  of time are not the 3D spaces that led to the resolution of the
  two paradoxes, since that resolution required that at any
  moment more than one space should exist.
- At this stage of the analysis it cannot be immediately
  concluded how the assumption of different spaces should be
  understood.
- There exist TWO logical possibilities:
    - Possibility 1:
        - the different spaces are NON-COINCIDING, which implies
          that these spaces are 3D cross-sections of a space of
          at least four dimensions
            - (in the same way that different lines, which are 1D
              spaces, are 1D cross-sections of at least a 2D
              space),
    - Possibility 2:
        - ... the different spaces coincide (in the sense that
          there is no need for a space of extra dimensions) but
          are in translational motion.

- What is important, however, is that we do not ‘perceive’ the
  same 3D space. And this is a crucial achievement since it can
  provide us with a hint on how different 3D spaces may exist.
- If the ‘space’ we ‘perceive’ through the objects we see
  consists of fragments from 3D spaces corresponding to DIFFERENT
  MOMENTS of time, then it is a logical possibility that
  different 3D spaces can be constructed in the same way: as
  consisting of fragments from 3D spaces belonging to different
  moments of time (the inclined lines in Fig. 3.3).

- A possible objection to this logical possibility is that the
  horizontal lines in Fig. 3.2 do not represent different 3D
  spaces; it is simply the same three-dimensional space at
  different moments of time.
- The radical research team is unlikely to be impressed by such
  an objection since it is dictated by our common sense, which is
  not a reliable authority; as they advance in their analysis of
  the second interpretation of Galileo’s principle of relativity,
  their reliance on common sense gradually diminishes.



- Because the radical research team do not know at this moment
  which interpretation of the 'many spaces assumption' is
  consistent with the non-existence of absolute uniform motion,
  they obviously cannot accept the common view that the _same_ 3D
  space belongs to different moments of time.

- In order to gain some additional insight into these questions,
  lets join the traditional team who are trying to answer why we
  cannot detect absolute uniform motion.
- As traditional thinkers, they are not impressed by the
  resolution of their paradox with the help of the radical idea
  of MANY SPACES.
- And worse: the assumption of 3D spaces in relative motion
  defeats the idea of absolute uniform motion (as motion in the
  space) and therefore threatens their basic belief in the
  existence of absolute uniform motion.
- That is why the traditional team have been thinking of using
  different kinds of experiments to try to detect absolute
  uniform motion.
- They have been considering a reformulation of the first
  interpretation of Galileo’s principle of relativity:
    - from “absolute uniform motion exists but cannot be
      discovered” 
    - to
    - “absolute uniform motion exists but cannot be detected with
      the type of experiments which Galileo performed”.
    - Since Galileo studied only mechanical experiments, the
      traditional team decides to see whether experiments
      involving light can help them discover the absolute motion
      of the Earth.
- Such a thought (Gedanken) experiment was not unthinkable in the
  17th century, since Romer’s experiment in 1676 had already
  determined light speed was finite.
- In fact, Galileo himself suspected light propagated at a finite
  speed and even devised experiments using lanterns on hills.
