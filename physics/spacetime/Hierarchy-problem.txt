ChatGPT said:

The hierarchy problem is a puzzle about why gravity is so much weaker than the other fundamental forces, or more precisely, why the Higgs boson’s mass is so small compared to the Planck scale.

Here’s the setup:
In the Standard Model, the Higgs field gives mass to particles. The Higgs boson itself has a measured mass of about 125 GeV. But quantum corrections—loops of virtual particles in quantum field theory—tend to push the Higgs mass toward the highest energy scale in the theory. If we believe the Standard Model remains valid up to the Planck scale (≈10¹⁹ GeV, where gravity becomes strong), then the “natural” Higgs mass should also be enormous, unless an almost miraculous cancellation occurs between bare parameters and loop corrections.

That cancellation would need to be fine-tuned to something like 1 part in 10³⁴. Physicists find that grotesquely unnatural, so they call it a hierarchy problem: why such a vast gap between the electroweak scale (≈10² GeV) and the Planck scale (≈10¹⁹ GeV)?

Various ideas have been proposed to explain this:

    Supersymmetry (SUSY) once promised to cancel those quantum corrections neatly, but so far no superpartners have shown up.

    Technicolor and composite Higgs models suggest the Higgs isn’t fundamental, avoiding the fine-tuning.

    Extra dimensions could lower the “true” fundamental scale of gravity.

    Anthropic reasoning or the multiverse approach says perhaps it’s just an accident of where our universe’s parameters landed.

At its core, the hierarchy problem is a question of naturalness: should we expect a huge disparity in scales to exist without an underlying reason? It remains one of the deepest open questions linking particle physics and cosmology.

It’s a bit like tuning a radio to a station where interference cancels perfectly—possible, but suspiciously precise.

1. Start from the force ratio picture

Between two protons separated by distance r:

    Gravitational force: F_g = G m_p² / r²

    Electric force: F_e = (1 / 4πϵ₀) e² / r²

The ratio is F_g / F_e = (G m_p² 4πϵ₀) / e² ≈ 10⁻³⁶

That means gravity is 10³⁶ times weaker than electromagnetism at the scale of ordinary matter. This gives the intuitive version: gravity feels absurdly weak.

2. Now step into the “energy scale” language

Physicists often switch to energy or mass units (GeV) because quantum field theory measures strengths and interactions that way.

In quantum field theory, a mass corresponds to an energy scale via E = m c², and we set c = 1, so mass and energy are interchangeable.
For instance, the proton mass m_p ≈ 1 GeV.

The gravitational coupling constant G can also be written in energy units. When you rewrite Newton’s constant G in natural units (ħ = c = 1):

G ≈ 1 / M_Pl²

where M_Pl ≈ 1.22 × 10¹⁹ GeV is the Planck mass, the energy scale at which gravity becomes as strong as the other forces.

- Now compare the two key energy scales in our universe:
    - Electroweak scale (set by the Higgs field): v ≈ 246 GeV
    - Planck scale (gravity becomes strong): M_Pl ≈ 10¹⁹ GeV
- Their ratio is v / M_Pl ≈ 10⁻¹⁷.

- That’s the same “tiny number” you saw in the force ratio!

4. The conceptual link

So:

    The force ratio picture tells us: gravity’s coupling between protons is tiny.

    The energy scale picture tells us: the scale where gravity becomes strong (the Planck scale) is astronomically higher than the energy scale governing the Higgs field (the electroweak scale).

Both describe the same hierarchy: the enormous gulf between everyday particle physics and the scale where gravity matters quantum mechanically.

In short:
tiny gravitational force ratio (≈10⁻³⁶) ↔ huge energy scale ratio (≈10¹⁷).

The hierarchy problem is asking: why does nature separate these scales so dramatically, yet have them coexist in one consistent quantum theory?
