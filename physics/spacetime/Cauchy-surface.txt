### Imagine a movie of the entire universe

- Picture spacetime as a giant 4D film reel: 
    - every event, past to future, is one frame. 
    - A Cauchy surface is like pausing the film at one moment;
      a `snapshot` that captures everything that exists
      simultaneously in that moment.

- But unlike Newtonian mechanics, “simultaneous” isn’t absolute.
  In relativity, different observers slice spacetime differently. 
    - Still, a Cauchy surface is one particular way to make such
      a global slice — one that truly cuts through all
      worldlines.

### Now about “inextensible timelike worldlines”

- A timelike worldline is just the path through spacetime that
  a physical object (something with mass, moving slower than
  light) traces out.

- “Inextensible” just means you follow that path for the entire
  lifetime of the object — from its birth to its death, as far as
  it can go without hitting an edge or singularity.

- So an “inextensible timelike worldline” is the complete story
  of an object’s life.

- The “crosses exactly once” part

- Now we can restate the definition more vividly:
    - A Cauchy surface is a spacelike surface that every possible
      object’s entire worldline intersects once and only once.

### That means:

- Once: 
    - nothing is left out — the surface includes a moment from
      every physical history.

- Only once: 
    - it doesn’t double-count anyone’s history — no object
      appears twice in that slice.

- If you know the complete physical state on such a surface — the
  field values, particles, everything — then the laws of physics
  (if deterministic) can tell you everything about the entire
  spacetime, both future and past. It’s a clean, global “initial
  condition” surface.

### Why this matters for Maudlin’s argument

- In an ordinary, globally hyperbolic spacetime (say, flat
  Minkowski space), every inextensible timelike worldline passes
  through each “moment of time” exactly once. That’s why you can
  evolve the universe deterministically forward or backward from
  a state on one Cauchy surface to another.

- But in a spacetime with a black hole that evaporates, the
  late-time slice after evaporation fails this property: some
  worldlines — those of matter that fell into the singularity
  — end before they reach the slice. So that slice is not a true
  “moment of the universe”; it misses parts of the story.

- Therefore, when you ask “where did the information go?” you’re
  really just looking at an incomplete frame of the film and
  expecting it to tell the whole story. That’s the root of the
  supposed paradox.

### In short:
- A Cauchy surface is the kind of slice of spacetime on which the
  whole universe fits, exactly once per worldline. If your slice
  misses any part of the universe — such as inside a singularity
  — it isn’t Cauchy, and you’ve lost your right to demand
  determinism or information conservation for it.
