### 01 Spacetime and Worldlines 
    - x-coord: Time (blue) 
    - y-coord: Spatial (red)

    - note: no object is STATIONARY in GR. Even if it doesn't
      move in space, it moves forward in time, prgressing towards
      the future.
    - All objects have a 'speed' (`c`?) in spacetime. The only
      thing that can change is how the speed is distributed
      BETWEEN the coordinates.


### 02 Spacetime velocity       
    - all objects move thru spacetime at the same 'speed': 
        - `c` the speed of light.

    - First equation: the velocity norm is `c`
        - `||ṽ|| = c`

    - Interesting: when using natural units, `c=1` ... then time
      starts to measure distance... 
        - and the proper time `τ` of an object measures the
          distance travelled through spacetime.

    - For each second of `τ`, an object travels a distance of
      1 light second through spacetime...

### 03 Geodesics                
    - describe the EVOLUTION of the velocity vector as proper
      time `τ` passes...
    - When no force is applied, objects move in 'straight'
      wordlines
        - Reason for straight lines? Come from 'symmetry':
        - An object has no reason to turn one way or the other
        - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-12.jpg
    - Method for predicting the trajectory of an object:
        - if we know the velocity vector at one instant, we can
          'transport' the arrow (velocity vector) along itself to
          gradually predict the movement of an object.
        - This type of trajectory, formed by transporting the
          velocity vector _along itself_ is called a `geodesic`
    - On a geodesic, a vector doesn't turn, so its derivative wrt
      proper time `τ` is zero
        - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-13.jpg
        - it just means the natural movement of an object is
          non-accelerating, and when they don't experience any
          force they tend to move ahead...
    - But we saw the velocity vector can be written as the SUM of
      its (components * basis vectors)
        - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-14.jpg

    - From here just some random screenshots
        - the change in the velocity vector wrt the coordinate
          system(for one component)
        - So this is the acceleration wrt the coordinate system
          (for this component)
        - In fact, these white 'change' vectors are actually the
          Christofels
            - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-30.jpg
            - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-31.jpg
    - There are 8 Christofels: two components for four different
      vectors
        - Why 4 vectors?? 
            - Why not one velocity vector? Or why not two change

### 05 Curvature                
    - Interesting: if you transport a vector along a flat
      surface, the image of the  vector is in the _same
      orientation_ in the new location:
        - But on a curved surface, the vector has a different
          orientation!
        - The orientation of the vector is _path dependent_ on
          a curved surface!
        - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-70.jpg
        - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-71.jpg
        - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-72.jpg
    - Transport a basis vector along two different paths.
        - The result is oriented differently, if a curved
          surface.
        - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-73.jpg
        - The difference between the two images is `R`. The more
          curved the surface, the longer `R` becomes.
            - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-72.jpg
    - `R` is the difference between two derivatives of the basis
      vectors in opposite orders.
        - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-73.jpg
        - The derivatives of basis vectors give Christofel
          symbols
            - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-74.jpg
        - Riemann tensor too unweildy. Use Ricci tensor instead.
    - skipped bits...
    - The Ricci tensor 
        - measures how `volume` changes as you transport it along
          a curved surface.
        - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-80.jpg
    - The Ricci scalar
        - for certain symmetrical surfaces, we can characterise
          the entire curvature by a single number, the Ricci
          scalar

### 07 The Einstein equation    
    - Most powerful idea: equate the two notions of spacetime
      geometry and energy flow
        - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-100.jpg
    - We'll write an equality between curvature and content
        - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-101.jpg
        - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-102.jpg
    - Eqn is inferred
        - In our mathematical model, this is the only equation
          that can't be proven. It is _inferred_ from
          observations, postulated because it very well describes
          our world.
    - Touch the real world
        - This equation contains all the predictive quality of
          GR. It allows us to relate the abstract mathematical
          model to concrete predictions.
    - Usual form of the equation
        - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-103.jpg
        - note: the constant beside `T` the energy-momentum
          tensor defines the intensity of gravity in the
          universe.
        - This constant is chosen for compatability with older
          theories and recovers Newtonian Gravity when the pull
          is weak.
    - Hard to solve
        - The Einstein eqn is very hard to solve. The lhs
          contains the Ricci tensor and Ricci scalar: very
          complicated!
            - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-104.jpg
        - Also: both sides of the eqation are closely related.
            - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-105.jpg
            - So we often make approximations and use computer
              simulations to solve such problems
                - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-106.jpg
    - Technical Note:
        - note 1: The Ricci scalar is a `contraction` of the
          Ricci tensor
            - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-107.jpg
        - Whaaaat....??
        - The Einstein equation remains valid when we swap the
          Energy-Momentum tensor `T` with the Ricci tensor `R`!!
            - (apart from changing the gravitational constant)
            - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-108.jpg
        - The new eqn is completely equivalent and in some cases
          can be easier to use!
            - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-109.jpg
    - Example
        - Assume a spherical mass
            - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-110.jpg
        - This spherical mass could be the sun or the Earth.
        - Our goal is to obtain the `metric tensor` `g` at
          a point around this body (the Earth) and hence the
          spacetime geometry around the body.
            - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-111.jpg
        - Now choose a coordinate system, and observe the test
          point from a very far distance...
            - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-112.jpg
        - Our point sits in a vacuum, therefore the
          energy-momentum `T` is zero
            - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-113.jpg
        - Use the 2nd version of Einsteins Equation
            - we deduce the Ricci tensor = 0
            - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-114.jpg
            - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-115.jpg
            - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-116.jpg
        - Solving loads of differential eqns and using the
          symmetries we postulated for this problem, it's
          possible to solve for `g`
            - We get the Schwarzschild Metric
            - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-117.jpg
        - Interesting: if the mass of the object is zero, we
          recover the Minkowski metric from the Schwarzschild
          metric
            - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-118.jpg
        - For a sufficiently compact object, where it's surface
          is close to it's centre, we can reach a point where the
          temoral coordinate of the Schwarzschild metric goes to
          zero.
            - We've reached the point of no return.
            - Time is infinitely stretched compared to the
              outside. (so is proper time `τ` stretched?)
            - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-119.jpg
        - This is the Schwarzschild radius, the horizon of a BH
            - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-120.jpg


### 08 Summary and Applications 
    - Catalogue to different possible solutions and calculate:
        - Time dilation for astronaut in space station
        - Eqns of motion for an object falling towards the sun
        - Explain behaviour of light around a BH
            - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-130.jpg
    - Steps for problem-solving in GR
        - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-132.jpg
        - 1. Determine spacetime geometry: 
            - Choose the metric `g` from several options
            - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-134.jpg
        - 2. Choose the coordinates
            - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-135.jpg
            - Minkowsi often uses Cartesian coords to reflect its
              linear structure
                - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-136.jpg
            - Sometimes no existing solutions (Minkowski,
              Schwarzschild etc) are suitable. In that case, we
              have to: 
                - determine the most appropriate coords, 
                - start directly from the Einstein Equation, 
                - and integrate the different curvature tensors
                  to find an expression for the metric tensor.
                - This is often very complex and needs computer
                  simulations.
                - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-138.jpg
        - 3. Reduce the number of dimensions.
                - Analyse the symmetries of our problem to reduce
                  the number of dimensions.
                - e.g. for an orbiting body, it will be confined
                  to a plane, so we can align our coords to this
                  plane and reduce the problem to two dimensions
                  of space.
                - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-139.jpg
                - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-140.jpg
        - 4. Seek the solution. 
            - The problem DESCRIPTION is now complete. 
            - We have two possibilities: we can 
                - use the `|norm|` of the velocity to find the
                  velocity components of an object 
                    - (find temporal component of an object, the
                      rate our time passes compared to its own
                      proper time)
                    - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-141.jpg
                    - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-142.jpg
                - or find the acceleration components
                    - how the velocity components of an object
                      changes over time
                    - This uses the Christofels and geodesic eqn
                    - $MY_IMAGES/physics/GR/ScienceClic/GR-maths-143.jpg
