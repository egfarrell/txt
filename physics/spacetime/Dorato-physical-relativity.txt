# Relativity theory: STRUCTURAL and DYNAMICAL explanations
    - Mauro Dorato

### Links

- Phenomena like rod contractions and clocks retardations have
  attracted the attention of philosophers since Einstein’s
  original paper on SR.
    - Very often, the key question about such relativistic
      effects is: _are they real?_

- The answer depends on what is meant by the metaphysically
  appealing, but philosophically unsuitable adjective: 
    - "REAL"!
    - If "real" means "measurable", then the answer ought to be
      an uncontroversial "YES", as every experimental physicist
      working at CERN knows.
    - If "real" means "dynamical", then I guess the majority of
      physicists & philosophers would answer with a resounding
      "NO".
    - Can we explain the relativistic effects as a _projection_
      of 4D conceived rods and clocks onto different, arbitrarily
      chosen inertial frames of Minkowski spacetime?

- Harvey Brown and co-workers (especially Oliver Pooley,
  a brilliant, former student of his) have been arguing that
  length contractions and clock dilations, regarded so far as
  purely kinematical effects, need a dynamical, presumably
  quantum explanation in terms of Lorentz covariant laws: 
    - an explanation that does not require the postulation of
      a _privileged_ inertial frame.

- To my knowledge, Brown’s "Physical Relativity. Space-time
  structure from a dynamical perspective" is the first book-
  length defence of this interesting thesis, resurrected by John
  Bell (1976), but running through the last century like an
  underground river that every now and then emerges to the
  surface.
    - Bell J. (1976): “How to teach special relativity” p. 67
    - $MY_BOOKS/Physics/QM/Speakable-and-Unspeakable-in-QM-2e.pdf

- The Leitmotiv running through the book, is Einstein’s famous
  distinction between two types of physical theories: 
    - "Principle" theories 
    - "Constructive" theories

- The "Principle" ones, like thermodynamics, are empirically
  based on general characteristics of empirical processes;

- The "Constructive" ones, like statistical mechanics, build up,
  in Einstein’s words "a picture of more complex phenomena from
  the materials of a relatively simple formal scheme" (p.71).

- This distinction runs deep into questions about the nature of
  scientific explanations, but Brown stays away from...

- Historical considerations are important from Brown’s
  perspective, as they explain why most physicists, partially
  influenced by an incorrect picture of Einstein’s contribution
  to SR vis à vis Hendrik Lorentz’s, are still convinced that
  contractions and dilations are _kinematical effects_.
    - As chapter 5 convincingly shows, Einstein’s decision to
      favour the formulation of SR as a 'Principle theory' was
      a consequence of the shaky ground on which the newly born
      quantum theory was resting in 1905 (for a similar claim,
      see Rynasiewicz 2005).
    - Because the light quantum hypothesis had already convinced
      Einstein that Maxwell’s equation "could not claim to exact
      validity", the light Postulate of SR had to be formulated
      independently of any theory about the constitution of light
      (p.77).
    - For similar reasons, Brown argues, the other pillar of SR,
      the Relativity Principle, was originally presented by
      Einstein as a postulate to bypass Lorentz’s (and
      Poincaré’s) problem: 
        - explaining the Relativity Principle by deriving it from
          Lorentz covariant dynamical laws governing the _inner
          structure_ of rods and clocks.

- However, in various chapters Brown shows that Einstein did not
  like to view rods and clocks as "primitives" of the theory, and
  thought of them as "moving atomic configurations".
    - According to Einstein, SR should ideally be formulated as
      a constructive theory, in the same sense that statistical
      mechanics "builds up" the thermal phenomena.

- Parallel to his attempt of showing the importance of
  a dynamical underpinning of SR, is Brown’s more disputable
  thesis that no other explanation of contractions and dilations
  is available:
    - "a moving rod contracts and a moving clock dilates because
      of what it is made of, not because of the nature of their
      spatiotemporal environment" (p.8).
    - If this claim amounted to the reasonable thesis, already
      advanced by Di Salle (1995), that the Minkowski metric is
      only a "_codification_" of the behaviour of rods and
      clocks, and cannot be regarded as a _cause_ of such
      a behaviour, I would wholeheartedly agree, for many of the
      reasons provided by Brown in chapter 8.

- However, as Brown knows, the correctness of this claim by
  itself does not entail that such a behaviour is caused by
  something else.
    - Unclear: 
        - We don’t need to consider spacetime as the cause of
          a Lorentz deformation to appreciate that the view which
          regards entities as four-dimensional worldtubes
          exemplifying the structure of Minkowski spacetime might
          yield a satisfactory explanation of the reason why the
          shape of a rod is different in different inertial
          frames, WITHOUT having to introduce any dynamical

- Considering that Brown himself elsewhere seems to have
  partially endorsed this view, it is surprising that its
  consequences for his main problem – why do physical entities
  conform to Minkowski geometry? – have not received sufficient
  attention.
    - This drawback may be a consequence of Brown never
      discussing the nature of physical explanations in general,
      and does not defend a distinctive view about it.
    - Sometimes he tacitly endorses a deductive-nomological model
      of explanation, as when he claims that for the first time
      in history GR explains inertia (p.141), while throughout
      the book he seems to be defending a causal/dynamical
      account of relativistic effects, against a structural view
      of explanation ala RIG Hughes (1989 and 1993).

- In chapter 1, the overview, we start our historical journey,
  beginning with the FitzGerald who, in a note published in
  Science in 1889, tried to explain the null result of Michelson
  and Morley’s 1887 experiment.
    - By following an idea of Heaviside’s, FitzGerald "feigned
      the hypothesis" that motion could have an effect on the
      electrostatic forces acting within the body by distorting
      its shape (not necessarily by _contracting_ it).

- In chapter 2 we find a condensed criticism of the thesis that
  contractions and dilations can be understood only via the usual
  Lorentz transformation of spacetime coordinates, and therefore
  in a pure kinematic fashion.
    - There are two remarkable arguments here:
        - The first concerns the nature of _inertia_,
        - the other on the role of the _relativity of
          simultaneity_ in explaining the relativistic effects.

- The former argument is important, because it shows Brown’s
  tendency to look for explanations which might simply be
  unnecessary.
    - E.g., he wonders why force-free particles conspire to
      follow rectilinear paths without being able to influence
      each other and adds that "anyone who is not amazed by this
      conspiracy has not understood it" (p.15).
    - I must be one who has not understood inertia: 
        - isn’t going in straight paths (or following geodesics
          in Lorentzian manifolds) simply in the `definition` of
          inertial motion, namely a correspondence rule or
          meaning postulate linking a geometric notion (being
          straight or minimizing distance) with a physical
          concept (being force free)?
    - Brown sees this objection, of course, but adds that the
      empirical content is:
        - "the claim that inertial coordinate systems exist,
          applicable to all free bodies in the universe" (p.15)
    - But even if the 'law of inertia' is an empirical existence
      claim, it would still not follow that the "conspiracy"
      above is something that needs to be explained, and is
      explained by GR.
    - In chapter 9 we read that "GR is the first of many
      dynamical theories that explains inertial motion, based on
      the profound Aristotelian distinction between natural and
      forced motions of bodies. (p. 141).
        - The explanation is obtained via the vanishing of the
          covariant divergence of the stress-energy tensor
          (ibid.).

- It seems to me that Brown’s view of explanation neglects the
  fact that scientific revolutions are often characterized by
  phenomena previously thought to need a causal explanation being
  declared "natural", and the transition from Aristotelian to
  Newtonian physics shows this transformation of _a problem into
  a postulate_ for the question "why do bodies continue to
  move?".
    - If inertial motion is the new _natural_ state of bodies in
      Aristotle's sense, it does not need to be explained by
      impetus theories or by other causal paraphernalia of the
      Aristotelian tradition.
    - In the new Newtonian framework, what needs to be explained
      is rather the _deviation_ from inertial motion, via the
      introduction of forces (the causes of such a deviation).

- Important!
- With GR, however, free fall becomes the new natural state of
  bodies, for the simple reason that it is _LOCALLY identifiable
  with inertial motion_:
    - it is for this very reason that gravitational forces (qua
      causes) in GR are _dispensable_.

- Brown is correct in remarking that spinless test particles
  following geodesics "can be derived from the vanishing of the
  covariant divergence of the stress energy tensor associated
  with the body" (p.161).
    - But I would not regard this as an "explanation" of inertial
      motion for two reasons: 
        - (aside: is Brown relying on the nomological-deductive
          model of explanation here?) 
        - First, inertial motion and free-fall may be regarded as
          standards against which we explain other motions (they
          are "natural motions" in the sense briefly illustrated
          above).
        - Second, since the association between the geometrical
          concept of "_geodesic_" and the physical notion of
          "_being force free_" is a precondition for applying the
          model described by Einstein's field equations to the
          physical world, the explanation in question seems
          circular to me.

- For the 2nd argument, Brown claims that relativistic effects do
  NOT presuppose the _relativity of simultaneity_ (p. 29, note
  43), because rods and clock protocols are defined wrt a single
  frame.
    - That is true of course, but if measuring the length of any
      rod in any frame presupposes the simultaneity of its ends,
      why is the relativity of simultaneity insufficient to
      explain Lorentz’s deformations?
    - The standard argument, where if simultaneity were not
      relative we would not have rods contractions, should not
      have been confined to a footnote, since it tends to push
      the explanation of the relativistic effects toward
      kinematics.
    - Regarding this problem, Brown claims that unless spacetime
      has a metric or scale factor, the cross sections of the rod
      relative to two different frames could not be compared, so
      that the relativity of simultaneity is neither necessary
      nor sufficient for the contraction.
    - But if we agree that "contraction" is a `purely relative
      phenomenon` (according to Brown there is no privileged
      frame) and if we endorse the innocuous claim that the laws
      governing the behaviour of the constituents of rods and
      clocks are Lorentz covariant, it is not clear to me what
      else we have to explain.

- Salmon (Salmon 1990).
    - The forward movement of the balloon during take off can be
      explained BOTH by:
        - the Principle of Equivalence and 
        - by causal mechanisms due to the pressure gradient of
          the air as a consequence of the acceleration of the
          aircraft.
    - Exactly as the two explanations in this case complete and
      don’t exclude each other, I submit that in Brown’s case the
      _dynamical and the geometrical explanations might well
      coexist_, a possibility Brown does not consider.

- Of course, one could ask: why does such a stipulation hold?
    - And here, if I had to take a stand, I would go along
      Brown’s line and claim that in SR the geometric structure
      depends on the physics and laws of the system.
    - But note that in GR, given the new, _dual nature of the
      metric tensor_, this separation of geometry from physics
      cannot be done, so that the question about the order of
      explanation only seems to arise for SR.

- The final section of chapter 9 is particularly important, as it
  indicates that once we endorse a _dynamical underpinning of
  SR_, we must also solve the conflict between gravitational
  phenomena and QM.
    - And one may wonder with Brown whether a constructive
      account exists of the relativistic effects even just in
      QFT, given its complexity:
        > "I am unaware of a systematic derivation of even the
        > stable structure of the hydrogen atom within
        > relativistic QFT" (p.148, n. 55).
    - One question especially seems missing here: given the lack
      of a quantum theory of measurement, how can we hope to give
      a dynamical, quantum explanation of the contractions of
      classically conceived rods?
        - Don’t we need to solve the _measurement problem_ first?
        - Brown is silent on this, which suggests however an
          interesting analogy between a dynamical approach to SR
          and QM, which is not considered by Brown, despite his
          admiration for John Bell.
        - One could argue that there must be a theory of
          measurement in terms of QM, in the same sense there
          must be a quantum theory of the solid structure of rods
          undergoing changes in shape, and much for the same
          reason.
        - Phenomenologically, both QM and SR work quite well by
          positing classical objects as primitive and given
          apparatuses in QM, rods in SR. 
            - But we might want to know why Born’s rule is so
              successful in the same sense in which we want to
              know _why rigid bodies undergo measurable
              contractions_ in moving frames.

- Once again, a discussion of the Erlangen-Program’s view of the
  _objective and the invariant_ being one-and-the-same could have
  strengthened the book:
    - if there's no such thing as "length at a time", why do we
      need a dynamically-based explanation for the _relative
      change_ in the length of rigid bodies?
    - Examples like those illustrated by Bell, in which a rope
      linking two bodies accelerating away from an inertial frame
      eventually breaks, might have helped Brown’s cause.
