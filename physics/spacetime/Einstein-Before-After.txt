### Great physical intuition: EM field
- `KEY 1`: LAGRANGIAN IMPLIES 'MECHANICAL' EM FIELD?
    - Lagrange’s method in mechanics was widely used in the 19th
      century. Maxwell described this method and then showed it
      could be applied to EM.
    - He didn't explicitly present the Lagrangian function
      describing the EM field. However, since he accepted that
      the EM field has both potential energy `W` and kinetic
      energy `T` , it's possible to write down a Lagrangian
      function `L = T − W` describing this field.
    - The possibility of describing EM according to the
      Lagrangian formalism was interpreted by Maxwell and his
      contemporaries as strong evidence that it was possible to
      provide a _mechanical theory_ for EM.
    - [Maxwell] chose to avoid any specific model. Instead, he
      adopted a general approach, stating that all EM phenomena
      could be understood as effects transmitted through the
      underlying _ether_. 
        - He calculated the general mechanical properties
          ascribed to that medium, without proposing detailed
          mechanical models for the ether.

- `KEY 2`: ETHER STRESSES (magnetism)
    - According to Maxwell, magnetic interactions are also
      associated with ether stresses.
    - The description of those stresses is highly complex in the
      general case (in the presence of matter), but in a vacuum
      they reduce to:
        - a longitudinal attraction and
        - a transverse pressure,
        - both with the same absolute value `p_m`, equal to the
          density of magnetic energy `ρ_m`:
        - $MY_IMAGES/physics/EM/formula-magnetic-energy.jpg

- `KEY 3`: EM WAVE: PRESSURE
    - (radiation pressure?)
    - By also following Faraday’s suggestion, Maxwell developed
      the EM theory of light.
    - Here Maxwell made use of the theory of ether stresses to
      compute the _pressure_ produced by an EM wave.
    - Suppose a polarized plane EM wave travels in the `z`
      direction.
        - The electric and magnetic fields are perpendicular to
          each another and transverse to the wave propagation.
        - Let us suppose that the electric field is in the `x`
          direction and the magnetic field in the `y` direction.
        - If the total energy density of the wave is `ρ`, on
          average half of this energy will be electric and half
          is magnetic. Therefore, the mean electric stresses are:
            - $MY_IMAGES/physics/EM/EM-wave-pressure-formula.jpg
    - Maxwell’s Treatise in 1873 contained the theoretical
      prediction that light should produce `pressure`.
    - In 1876, Adolfo Bartoli (1851–1896) independently deduced
      a formula for radiation pressure, using only
      a thermodynamic argument [14].
        - He showed that, if radiation didn't produce pressure,
          it'd be possible to violate the second law of
          thermodynamics.
    - Bartoli’s argument was generalized by Boltzmann (1844–1906)
      and Prince B. Galitzine (c. 1840–1916), some years later.
    - The _coincidence_ between the equations obtained by the
      thermodynamic argument and Maxwell’s theory of ether
      stresses led to strong confidence in those results.
    - Actually this result is not a specific property of EM
      waves. _Any wave_ carrying energy (such as sound or water
      waves) will also produce pressure.

- `KEY 4`: 'MOMENTUM' OF THE MAGNETIC FIELD
    - key:
        - self-induction 'similar' to inertia?
        - vector potential = ether 'momentum'?
    - In Maxwell’s theory the ether had several mechanical
      properties:
        - It amassed energy,
        - it produced forces,
        - it could produce pressure.
    - Ascribing motion and momentum to the ether was the next
      step.
    - Inertia
        - Following Faraday once more, Maxwell remarked that the
          phenomenon of _self-induction_ exhibited properties
          similar to _inertia_.
        - When an electric current flows through a circuit, and
          the electromotive force that produces the current is
          interrupted, the current does not immediately stop; it
          has a tendency to _continue for some time_.
        - The resistance of the current to stopping depends on
          both the value of the current and the geometric
          configuration of the wires through which it is flowing,
          which can be described by a quantitative property: the
          self-inductance of the circuit.
            - If this effect only depended on the value of the
              current and wire length, it would be possible to
              ascribe it to the inertia of the _particles_ that
              carry the electric charge (note: electrons were
              then unknown).
            - However, when the same wire, carrying the same
              electric current, is bent or transformed into
              a coil, its self-inductance changes.
            - Therefore, the self-inductance cannot be
              interpreted as a property of the `electricity
              carriers`.
    - How can it be interpreted then?
        - Maxwell provided several interpretations, all of them
          involving the _dynamics_ of ether.
        - On one hand, it was possible to account for the effect
          by taking into account the energy of the magnetic field
          around the wires:
            - the energy depends on the geometric configuration
              of the circuit.
        - On the other hand, Maxwell associated the magnetic
          field with _ether motion_; and therefore, the
          self-inductance could be associated with some dynamic
          effect around the wires, which Maxwell called
          “`electrokinetic momentum`”.
        - Maxwell proved that it was possible to evaluate this
          momentum by integrating the `vector potential` around
          the circuit.
        - The vector potential could be interpreted as a MOMENTUM
          associated with the ether. (!)

- `KEY 5`: PATH TO VECTOR POTENTIAL
    - key: various names:
        - 'electro-tonic function'
        - 'electro-tonic state'
        - 'electromagnetic momentum'
        - 'vector potential'
    - The path that led Maxwell to the concept of the vector
      potential is very interesting (see [11]).
    - Faraday had interpreted the EM induction as due to changes
      of “the electrotonic state” – an obscure concept he
      introduced in the 1830s.
    - Inspired by a paper by William Thomson (1847), in 1856
      Maxwell was able to represent the induced electromotive
      force at each point of a circuit in very simple form, which
      can be represented in modern notation as:
        - `E = dA/dt`
        - Eqn. 14
        - (put 'hat' on E and A)
    - In this paper, Maxwell interpreted the vector `A` as the
      “electro-tonic function”, or “electro-tonic intensity”. In
      the same paper, he clearly established the relation between
      this function and the magnetic induction `B`. 
    - In modern notation:
        - `B =∇ × A.`
        - (put 'hat' on B and A)
    - Several years later, Maxwell gave the same vector another
      name: “electromagnetic momentum”.
    - Why 'momentum'?
        - The new name was probably inspired by Eqn. 14 because,
          when we multiply both sides by an electric charge, the
          LHS of the equation becomes a `force`, and according to
          Newton’s second law the RHS should be the time
          derivative of the `momentum`.
    - Afterwards, in his Treatise, Maxwell introduced a third
      name for the same quantity:
        - “vector potential” – the name we use nowadays.
    - Note: here Maxwell is ascribing a new dynamical property to
      the `ether`: MOMENTUM.
    - In the presence of a magnetic field, or more exactly, in
      the presence of the vector potential, a charge will have
      a dynamical momentum, even if it is at rest.

- Faraday usually described the electric and magnetic effects
  using the concept of “lines of force”. According to Faraday,
  electromagnetic forces are carried by 'lines of force'
  stretched between electric charges.
    - In the current teaching of physics (especially undergrad)
      we still make use of lines of force.
        - However, the contemporary use is not equivalent to
          Faraday’s concept, because we don't conceive of those
          lines as _substantial physical realities_.
    - Michael Faraday accepted that the EM field has physical
      reality and quantitative properties, even in the _absence_
      of matter. This same idea was later used by Maxwell, in his
      EM theory.

- Why did Faraday introduce this conception of the EM field as
  physical reality?
    - The main reason seems to be the possibility of INTERVENING
      in the electric and magnetic interaction.
        - Gravitational effects behave as direct actions at
          a distance, in a straight line, without suffering any
          change when matter is interposed.
        - (? that can't be fully true?)
    - On the other hand, Electric and magnetic forces seem to
      propagate along _curved lines_, and ARE influenced by
      INTERPOSED (intervening) matter.
        - Therefore, they behave as influences that gradually
          propagate in space; a space filled with some substance
          from one point to the next.
        - Since 1832 Faraday believed that actions took some time
          to pass from one point to another, and he thought that
          it would be possible to measure their corresponding
          speed

- To account for the bending, Faraday supposed that the lines of
  force _repel_ each other, and for that reason they separate
  from one another and bend towards regions where there are no
  other lines.
    - Therefore, Faraday’s inductive lines of force had two main
      properties:
        - they produced induction and electrostatic attraction
          along the direction of the line;
        - and they produced a MUTUAL lateral _repulsion_

- The problem to be solved is to find the system of equations
  (i.e. “transformation”) connecting these two sets of
  quantities.
    - He points out at the start, “It is clear that these
      equations must be linear because of the properties of
      homogeneity that we attribute to space and time.”

- One result from this is that the electromotive force arising
  upon motion in a magnetic field, i.e. the so-called “Lorentz
  force”, is nothing but the electric force appearing in the
  moving system.

- In Section 8, he considers the transformation from rest to
  a moving system, of the energy in a ray of light enclosed
  within a closed surface, and comments:
    - It is noteworthy that the energy and the frequency of

    - Thus, he finally arrives at an expression for the pressure
