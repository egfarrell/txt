### Basics

- Einstein–Cartan (E-C) theory is basically GR with `torsion`
  included. Standard GR assumes the connection (the thing telling
  you how vectors move around) is torsion-free. E-C relaxes that
  assumption.

- Key idea: matter with spin acts as a source not only for
  curvature (as in GR) but also for torsion.

### In symbols:
    - GR:  connection `Γ` is symmetric ∴ torsion T = 0
    - E-C: connection allowed to have an antisymmetric part → torsion T ≠ 0

- The torsion couples to the spin density of matter. So curvature
  corresponds to mass-energy, while torsion corresponds to
  intrinsic angular momentum (spin).

- This theory naturally emerges if you write gravity as a gauge
  theory of the local Lorentz group SO(3,1). In that formulation,
  the metric is built out of a tetrad (vierbein) field eᵃₘ, and
  the connection ω couples to spinors. When you vary the action
  with respect to ω, torsion pops out algebraically in the
  presence of spin.

- So:
    - curvature ← energy-momentum
    - torsion ← spin

- At everyday scales the spin density of matter is tiny, so
  torsion effects are negligible, which is why standard GR works
  fine. But when spin densities become extremely large — near
  Planckian densities, inside black hole cores, or early-universe
  conditions — torsion may prevent singularities. E-C gravity
  often replaces the big bang singularity with a bounce.

# Why quantum gravity folks mention it:

- If you try to couple `spinor fields` to GR in a geometrically
  natural way, you automatically end up with E-C, not vanilla GR.
  It's the minimal classical limit of many attempts to unify
  gravity with quantum field theory because QFT fields carry
  spin. GR, as usually taught, quietly throws away the torsion
  term, which is arguably an unjustified simplification.

- In other words:
- If your theory includes fermions and treats geometry seriously,
  you usually get E-C in the classical limit. So people working
  toward quantum gravity frequently say: if you're serious, you
  should know E-C, because it’s the “corrected” version of GR
  that plays nicely with spin.

- This does not solve QG. It is just the right classical baseline
  to start from.

- It points to interesting deeper ideas: gauge theory
  formulations of gravity, tetrads, spin connections, loop
  variables, and so forth. That path eventually leads toward
  formulations like loop quantum gravity, spin foams, Poincaré
  gauge theory, and other things where geometry is “quantized” in
  terms of algebraic objects rather than just fields on
  a manifold.

# Pick it up on the Side?

- Yes — that is basically correct. Einstein–Cartan theory is
  rarely treated in standard GR textbooks, especially the ones
  used in typical undergrad or first-year grad courses.
- The “canonical” GR curriculum focuses on metric geometry,
  Levi-Civita connection, and curvature, and simply sets torsion
  to zero from the start. The idea that you might allow torsion
  (and that fermions actually demand it) usually appears only in
  more advanced or specialized contexts.

- So in most physics programs, a student can get all the way to
  a PhD having never heard of Einstein–Cartan in a formal course.

- Why it gets left out:
    - GR is already a big subject. The standard approach assumes:
    - the connection is metric-compatible, and
    - torsion = 0
    - These assumptions make life simpler and most astrophysical
      phenomena don’t require torsion anyway. So textbooks
      streamline the story to keep the learning curve manageable.

- What “pick it up on the side” really means:
- It doesn’t require some kind of secret apprenticeship. It just
  means:
- You usually learn E-C by reading papers or monographs rather
  than standard GR textbooks.

### Common autodidact paths:
    - Hehl, von der Heyde, Kerlick & Nester (1976 review) — the
      classic E-C reference
    - Nakahara’s Geometry, Topology and Physics touches the
      tetrad/spin-connection formalism (but not always torsion in
      detail)
    - Rovelli, Thiemann, and others in loop quantum gravity
      contexts — they assume tetrads and spin connections, which
      implicitly leads you to E-C
    - “Gauge theories of gravity” lecture notes are where most
      people first see it cleanly presented

- In practice, the first time someone seriously needs to couple
  spinor fields to curved spacetime in a mathematically
  consistent way (via tetrads and spin connection), they discover
  the moral:

- Once you allow spinors, torsion is no longer optional.
- GR-without-torsion is the special case, not the general one.

- If you’re already comfortable with differential geometry and
  spinors in curved spacetime, you can absolutely teach yourself
  E-C. It’s conceptually not harder than GR; it’s simply absent
  from the standard pedagogical funnel.

- From here, the natural continuation is to explore the tetrad
  + spin-connection formulation of GR. Once that becomes
    intuitive, E-C follows almost automatically.
