### Review: G. A. Schoenagel

- This will be a difficult book to review. 
- First note: this text is hardly an "introduction." 
    - Also, it's more a monograph than a text. Had it been
      marketed as such by the publisher, it would be better
      known. 
    - The Back Cover claims it serves as "a textbook for advanced
      undergraduates and graduates". 
    - I would not recommend this text to an undergraduate
      student. With those reservations, there is much of value,
      especially, for researchers!

### Let us take a tour:

- (1) Note: the metric is `(+,-,-,-)`, opposite to many
  introductions; a student will need to re-adjust accordingly.

- (2) SR will not be reviewed in the text. 
    - A student needs keep in mind the relevant aspects as
      summarized in MTW (1973, 2017). Now, for that purpose,
      Plebanski references Trautmann and Kopczynski, Spacetime
      and Gravitation (1992). Trautmann and Kopczynski is
      a rather terse, advanced, exposition.

- (3) Mathematics is presented in a "top-down" approach. 
    - That is, the discussion begins with manifolds and proceeds
      to 'add structure' as we go forward. 
    - I recommend (as a minimum) knowing parts of track one of
      MTW. Robert Wald's initial chapters can be recommended for
      that material, also (chapter two and chapter three).

- (4) Initial Chapters (one through seven) are the 'introductory'
  aspects of this monograph. 
    - If prerequisites assumed, this is a seventy page survey of
      mathematical material: 
        - Wedge product for forms utilized (page 41, although
          forms have not yet been introduced). 
        - The discussion of signature (+,-,-,-) is useful (page
          50). 
        - Discussion of intervals in 4D spacetime is interesting
          (page 61). 
        - Gauss-Codazzi equations presented (page 68; 
            - compare this to Wald, page 258), then, an aside on
              Petrov classification (unfortunately, too brief).

- (5) If the intrepid student has succeeded in assimilating the
  seventy-page 'introduction,' the remainder of the textbook
  offers nuggets of advanced wisdom. 
    - I highlight chapter eight: 
        - Symmetries and Killing Vector Fields. 
    - Read: "the number of linearly independent Killing vector
      fields can be larger than the dimension of the manifold."
      (page 79). 
    - Spherical symmetric form given a rapid-fire derivation
      (page 84 ). 
    - A total of five pages comprises chapter nine: methods to
      calculate curvature quickly, that is brief! 
    - One turns to MTW for more.

- (6) The remainder of the book consists of advanced material. 
    - A reconsideration of Petrov classification utilizing
      spinors occupies chapter 11 (nine pages). Consult Robert
      Wald, chapter 13 or John Stewart for additional material at
      a more leisurely pace.

- (7) Finally, Einstein equations (chapter twelve).
    - First note: Equation #12.21, index positions do not match
      on both sides of the equation ! (page 131).

- (a) We read: 
    - "The Einstein equations had long been rumored to be very
      difficult, almost too difficult to find any exact
      solutions. 
    - This opinion lingers until today and is even repeated in
      some publications, although it ceased to have any basis
      decades ago," and "finding a solution for a simple
      situation does not really help in looking for more general
      solutions." (page 145).

- (b) Read: "For brevity, we have omitted several instructive
  analogies of the `linearised Einstein equations` to Maxwell's
  equations..." (page 156). 
    - That omission is unfortunate. 
    - However, Stephani's (excellent) 1990 text is cited.

- (c) Read: "there is no general criterion that would allow one
  to distinguish between a `true singularity`...from
  a singularity introduced artificially together with the
  coordinate system. (page 191).

- (d) Review: 'Relativistic Cosmology', by Ellis.                        
    - "Other observationally meaningful quantities are specific
      flux, intensity of radiation, specific intensity...while
      being useful for observations, these quantities do not
      really involve relativity in their definitions and
      properties, and are handled by routine mathematics."
      (chapter sixteen). 
    - The text references the 1971 review article 'Relativistic
      Cosmology', by Ellis.

- (8) Relativistic Cosmology
    - Chapters 16, 17, 18, and 19 are headlined Relativistic
      Cosmology.
    - Advanced , but very fascinating. 
    - We read: "Thus, if we wish to stick to the tradition that
      physics is an empirical science, inflation cannot be called
      a physical theory." (page 286, Section 17.9, by Krasinski). 
    - Interesting reading !

- (9) The Kerr solution will conclude the book. 
    - We read: "The Kerr metric was discovered rather
      accidentally..." (page 438).
    - For the story, told by Roy Kerr, I refer the reader to: The
      New Zealand Listener (25 September 2004, online).
    - Otherwise, the final chapter 20 (sixty pages) is a useful
      exposition. However, the so-called Penrose process is
      merely touched upon (page 486) and the section of Newtonian
      analogues is too brief (page 493).

- (10) There are exercises which conclude each chapter. 
    - Some are simple: 
        - "show that the geodesics on a 2-dimensional sphere are
          great circles." (problem #2, page 72, hint provided). 
    - Others are quite involved: 
        - "verify that the tensors E and H, and the vector-field
          u, determine the Weyl tensor." (problem #9, page 72).
    - Helpful hints are usually provided.
    - Topics omitted as spelled out by the authors:
      gravitational waves and experimental tests among them !

- (11) Conclusion: Not a first, nor second, course in GR.
  A potpourri of advanced topics, hardly an introduction in any
  sense of the word. With sufficient background, much is of
  interest.

- As a reference, useful for continuing enrichment.
