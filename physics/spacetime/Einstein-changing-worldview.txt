### Thoughts About a Conceptual Framework for Relativistic Gravity
    - Bernard F. Schutz

    - I will illustrate the conceptual changes in relativity
      between the "dark ages" and the modern era by considering
      two key issues listed by Ted Newman as key unsolved
      problems during the dark years of relativity.

    - The ﬁrst is the meaning of the Schwarzschild solution. 
        - In the 1930s people talked about the "Schwarzschild
          singularity" (by which they meant the horizon, not the
          crunch at the center).
    - Today we use the term "black hole."
    - There is a world of difference between the ideas behind
      these different terminologies.
        - If you think you have a singularity, then you can’t use
          it in a physical model.
            - You don’t know how to include such an object in
              a physical system, either as the outcome of
              gravitational collapse or as an object that might
              affect other objects with its gravitational ﬁeld.
                - (So my CAMK simulations would never have
                  worked? Unphysical?)
        - On the other hand, the term "black hole" is a shorthand
          description of a real object, one which you can
          conﬁdently include in models for some physical systems:
            - e.g. as the constituent of a model for an X-ray
              binary system, 
            - or as a gravitating center in galaxies
    - In the ﬁrst case you are paralyzed by incomprehension.
    - In the second you can hide away all the nonlinear general
      relativity, if you wish, and treat the object as just
      another member of the vast zoo of objects that make up our
      fascinating universe.

    - I believe the gulf between relativity and mainstream
      physics opened, not directly because of these problems, but
      because the relativity community’s response to these two
      problems was to ask the wrong questions. 
        - E.g., one of the serious mathematical challenges they
          faced was coordinate freedom. 
        - Ted Newman has cataloged the way the community clearly
          missed opportunities to understand that the so-called
          Schwarzschild 'singularity' is just a `coordinate
          effect`.
        - Today this episode seems baffling.
        - Relativists do not seem to have understood the
          importance of controlling the effects of coordinates on
          their results despite Einstein’s emphasis that the
          physics should be coordinate invariant. 
        - They even had coordinate systems available at that time
          (from Eddington and Lemaitre) that went across the
          horizon in a nonsingular way.

    - Ted Newman also gave another example: 
        - the deep confusion over the concept of energy in
          space-times containing gravitational waves. 
        - The resolution of this issue only began when Hermann
          Bondi and his successors, including Roger Penrose and
          Ted himself, discovered how to treat radiated energy
          far from its source. 
        - It is easy to understand why relativists felt they
          needed to clarify the concept of energy: 
            - energy is one of the key heuristics of mainstream
              physics. 
        - However, I don’t understand why relativists allowed the
          genuine difﬁculties of defining gravitational wave
          energy to stop them developing a physical understanding
          of gravitational waves themselves. 
        - It appears that, because it was difﬁcult to deﬁne the
          energy of a radiating system or to localize the energy
          carried by waves, relativists were unable to develop
          any useful physical models for gravitational waves
          during this period.

    - But today we know that it's perfectly possible to describe
      the generation of gravitational waves and their action on
      a simple detector WITHOUT once referring to energy. 
        - The _quadruple formula_ for the generation of the waves
          and the geodesic equation for their action on a simple
          detector are all one needs, and these tools were
          available in 1918. 
        - It's also possible to show that gravitational waves
          certainly deposit energy on some kinds of detectors,
          without having a full global energy conservation law.
        - Indeed, at the earlier meeting in Chapel Hill 1957
          (DeWitt & Rickles 2011) Feynman presented a simple
          argument showing how a gravitational wave would heat
          a detector that has internal friction. 
        - The argument is so direct that I used a version of it
          in my undergrad relativity textbook (Schutz 2009), and
          extended it to derive the standard expression for the
          local average energy ﬂux in gravitational waves 
            - (first put on a ﬁrm foundation by Isaacson 1968). 
        - I think Feynman was right to be disappointed that his
          argument at Chapel Hill seemed to impress no one and
          was not taken up by relativists at the time.

    - I think this example goes to the heart of the question.
      Feynman was asking a physicist’s question, about how
      gravitational waves act. 
        - All he wanted was a convincing intuitive argument that
          the waves were real and that he could treat them as
          part of the rest of physics, for example, by extracting
          thermal energy from them. 
        - However, the relativists of his day were not interested
          in this kind of physicist’s answer, apparently not even
          as a ﬁrst step toward a more complete understanding of
          gravitational waves. 
        - Instead, they wanted to transplant as much of the
          apparatus of energy conservation as they could from the
          rest of classical physics. 
        - Energy conservation is of course a key concept in
          theoretical physics. 
        - But the work of Emmy Noether had long before shown that
          one _should not expect exact energy conservation in the
          absence of time invariance_, e.g., in a space-time
          containing gravitational waves. 
        - In relativity, energy will always be a subsidiary
          concept, valid in some circumstances and useless in
          others.
        - I believe Feynman found it very frustrating that
          relativists seemed more interested in the
          pure-radiation energy concept, in other words,
          relativity for its own sake, rather than exploring the
          interaction of gravitational waves with material
          systems, gravitational waves as part of physics.

    - ... I remember, as a grad student, my supervisor Kip Thorne
      emphasizing that if coordinate confusion threatened, then
      one should construct a thought experiment and only worry
      about what the experimenter _could in principle measure_; 
        - and made it clear that his own supervisor, John
          Wheeler, had emphasized this to him.
        - Wheeler, of course, had worked extensively on quantum
          physics before taking up relativity in the mid-1950s. 
        - The idea of focusing on observables was natural to him
          and to other physicists of his generation. 
        - It had unfortunately not developed sufﬁciently in
          relativity, and seems clear to me that introducing the
          strict discipline of observability was essential to
          ending relativity’s isolation.

    - For people like Bondi, Pascual Jordan, Wheeler, and
      Zel’dovich, among others, it was natural to test any
      question about GR with the demand that it be phrased in
      terms of observables. 
        - Is there something you can measure? At least an
          experiment in principle? 
        - This way of thinking forces you, for example, to look
          for physical features of the black hole horizon rather
          than just the bad behavior of some metric components. 
        - Do the local tidal stretching forces near the
          Schwarzschild "singularity" remain ﬁnite? 
        - Can a real body reach and cross this surface in a ﬁnite
          amount of its _own_ (proper?) time?

    - ... than just these time-independent exact solutions of
      Einstein’s vacuum ﬁeld equations. 
        - Wheeler took a major step toward our present picture by
          showing, with Tullio Regge, that the Schwarzschild
          horizon and exterior are stable against small
          perturbations. 
        - Immediately this meant that the idealized Schwarzschild
          solution was robust enough to be included in models of
          more complicated physical systems: 
            - it would retain its essential properties even when
              disturbed. This robust object is what we now call
              a BLACK HOLE.
