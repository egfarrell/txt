### Key Points 1
    - Compression Gap: 
        - LLMs struggle with research-level math because
          professional literature uses highly compressed
          shorthand (idioms like "by standard arguments") that
          assumes the reader can fill in the gaps—something
          models can't yet do.
    - Prose vs. Logic: LLMs mimic the *texture* and "vibe" of
      expert discourse through pattern matching, but they lack
      the underlying logical grounding to actually decompress
      those patterns into valid proofs.
    - The Oral Tradition: Much of mathematical "truth" exists in
      informal academic culture (e.g., knowing which published
      papers are secretly flawed), making it invisible to models
      trained only on written text.
    - AI as "Formalization Minions": While unreliable for novel
      research, LLMs are excellent at the "grunt work" of
      translating human-decomposed tasks into formal,
      machine-verified code (like Lean).
    - Future of Publishing: The author proposes a shift where
      verified formal proofs become the primary academic
      artifact, and prose serves only as a human-readable
      summary.


### Key Points 2

- Training data mismatch:
    - LLMs struggle with research-level math not because of weak
      reasoning, but because research papers use heavily
      compressed, implicit language ("by standard arguments")
      that models pattern-match rather than truly decompress.

- Undergraduate vs. research writing:
    - Undergraduate math is explicit and pedagogical, so LLMs
      handle it well. Research math is shorthand-heavy prose
      written for experts who can mentally "fill in the gaps"
      — something LLMs can't genuinely do.

- The bullshitting problem:
    - Models produce fluent, convincing mathematical prose
      without the underlying logical grounding, meaning small
      errors compound invisibly through a derivation.

- Not just an AI problem:
    - Research math literature itself has an "oral tradition" of
      known errors that are quietly acknowledged but never
      formally corrected — meaning some of this flawed ground
      truth is baked into LLM training data.

- Potential way forward:
    - Rather than training models to be "better at math," the
      author envisions using LLMs as tireless assistants to
      *formalize* existing prose proofs (e.g. in Lean),
      transforming mathematical publishing so that verified
      formal proofs become the primary artifact, not the prose
      summary.



### Why LLMs Fail at Mathematics and Physics Research (And What To Do About It)

- tobiasosborne

- I recently had an enlightening conversation with Rob May and
  Yash Sharma of NeuroMetric.ai about adversarial verification
  protocols. Their insightful questions forced me to crystallise
  something I’d been circling for a while: why frontier models
  perform so much better at undergraduate mathematics than
  research-level work. (I assure you, the only “PhD smarts” they
  have is the ability to bullshit you on a doctoral level…)

- My current conjecture is that this isn’t primarily about
  reasoning capability. It’s about the training data itself.

### The Phase Transition in Mathematical Communication

- Consider what undergraduate mathematics looks like in text.
  Every step is explicit. Deductions are clarified. The prose
  exists to serve the logical structure. This is pedagogical
  writing. Its entire purpose is to leave nothing implicit.

- Now consider research mathematics. As we move past the graduate
  level, the character of written communication undergoes a sharp
  phase transition. Researchers make increasingly free use of
  domain-specific idioms. Multiple steps of deduction collapse
  into offhand remarks. A single sentence, “by standard arguments
  in stable homotopy theory,” might expand to an undergraduate
  thesis worth of Lean-level deductions. (Don’t believe me? Just
  search for “standard arguments” in https://mathoverflow.net/.
  There are some laugh-out-loud moments to be found…)

- Why? Efficiency of communication. When you know your readers
  can fill in the algebraic steps, there’s no need to spell them
  out. Mathematical communication becomes prose.

- And LLMs are really good at prose.

### The Compression Problem

- It’s very hard to train for truth when truth is compressed this
  way. From the model’s perspective, it’s simply more efficient
  to allocate weights toward reproducing the texture of expert
  discourse than to somehow encode the vast implicit logical
  structure that experts understand such phrases to represent.

- An LLM will generate text that reads like genuine scientific
  communication. The prose flows. The citations feel appropriate.
  The handwaving gestures in the right directions.

- But “by standard arguments” is doing no actual work. It’s
  pattern-matched prose, not compressed understanding.

### What Human Expertise Actually Looks Like

- So isn’t this what human understanding looks like too? We also
  communicate in compressed shorthand.

- Yeah, nah. When an expert writes “a standard patching argument”
  they understand that this phrase stands for something specific.
  They can imagine what the unrolled proof looks like. They rely
  on shared commons to ensure the reader imagines the same thing.
  They can control semantic drift across a chain of such
  compressions because each abbreviated step maintains its
  connection to ground truth.

- The LLM has no such grounding. Each plausible-sounding phrase
  is generated from local context, and small errors compound
  through a derivation. The model is doing prose generation, not
  decompression of understood structure.

### An Uncomfortable Truth About Mathematical Literature

- This problem isn’t actually unique to LLMs. It’s a dirty secret
  of research mathematics that prose proofs can be brittle. There
  are well-known papers that experts quietly acknowledge are
  incorrect, but this is ok because everyone also knows how to
  fix them. Nobody wants to write the correction because academic
  culture runs on politeness and nobody wants to upset the
  original author.

- You learn about these things during conference coffee breaks,
  in supervisor-student discussions. It’s an oral tradition. Good
  luck finding that training data.

- Some researchers have been concerned enough to initiate
  spectacular efforts to decompress the prose literature into
  formal proofs. The Liquid Tensor Experiment formalised
  Scholze’s work in Lean. Hales’ Flyspeck project verified the
  Kepler conjecture. These efforts have been nothing short of
  heroic. They’ve also been, umm, somewhat expensive in human
  time.

- Until now.

### The Opportunity

- We suddenly have access to tirelessly optimistic minions who
  will cheerfully – “This is excellent material, Tobias” – grind
  away formalising the most tedious lemmas until the cows come
  home. They’re not reliable for generating novel research-level
  arguments, for exactly the reasons I’ve described.
    - But, once you, the true domain expert, have done the task
      decomposition, they’re remarkably good at the mechanical
      work, the boilerplate, of expanding compressed steps into
      explicit formal proofs, given appropriate verification
      infrastructure.

- This suggests a different path forward than trying to train
  models to be “better at maths.” Maybe we can’t train for
  fidelity when the ground truth is prose. Maybe we need to
  reimagine mathematical communication altogether.

### A Different Future for Mathematical Publishing

- I have started imagining lemma-as-a-service providers supplying
  verified building blocks that researchers can cite with actual
  confidence. Or maybe proof miners with a couple of GPUs using
  open models who spend tokens to formalise a paper as
  a condition of publication`*.`

- Imagine a world where publication fees – here I am sending out
  a big f--k you to all for-profit journals – are replaced by
  a GitHub repository and a renewable energy electricity bill.

- The formal proof becomes the artifact. The prose becomes what
  it always was: a human-readable summary for experts who want to
  quickly assess relevance before examining the actual verified
  object. Extrapolating a little more, I wonder at the social
  norms that could emerge: “What, your paper doesn’t have
  a formal proof? Hmm… What are you trying to hide?”

- This isn’t a small change. It’s a transformation of how
  mathematical knowledge is produced, communicated, and trusted.
  But for the first time, just maybe, the economics might
  actually work out.
