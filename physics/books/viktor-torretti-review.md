### Viktor Blasjo:
    - review of Torretti's "Philosophy of Geometry from Riemann to Poincaré"
    - Tagline: "Extremely prejudiced"

- This is an very prejudiced history of the foundations of
  geometry. Torretti is a modernist incapable of empathy for
  anyone except Riemann. He examines the contributors to the
  foundations of geometry, summarises their work haphazardly,
  translates it into pretentious modern terminology, and
  proclaims they were stupid whenever their ideas differ from his
  own, often imagining himself slaying their argument by some
  trivial observation.

- An example is Helmholtz's discussion of the _role of rigid
  motions_ in geometry. Torretti dismisses this in two lines,
  using the analogy of two-dimensional creatures living on
  a surface.

    > "If they happen to live, say, on an egg, they will be
    > unable to build transportable rigid figures, and
    > consequently, according to Helmholtz, they will be
    > incapable of defining a geometry. Though Helmholtz
    > introduces the example of an egg, he does not draw the
    > latter consequence; had he drawn it, it would probably
    > have shocked him out of his operationist bias"
    (p. 392).

It's the same with Poincaré:

    > "Though Poincaré was well acquainted with Minkowski's
    > work - indeed he even anticipated some of its
    > technical aspects - he apparently failed to appreciate
    > its great significance for the philosophy of geometry"
    (p. 333), explicitly stating the opposite (p. 414).

- These are two instances where Torretti thinks he has refuted
  great thinkers by counterexamples that were actually well known
  and even discussed by the thinkers themselves. If Torretti was
  not so quick to dismiss everyone's understanding, this should
  have suggested to him that he had not fully grasped their
  arguments.

- Torretti maintains this condescending attitude throughout and
  only Riemann is worthy of praise.
- Unfortunately, "_Riemann's broadminded conception of geometry_"
  was replaced by "_Helmholtz's dogma of complete free
  mobility_".
- Klein too "_loses sight of the full scope of Riemann's
  conception_",  by foolishly discussing spaces of
  constant curvature even though more general spaces can be
  defined...

- Unlike Helmholtz, Torretti is not of course dogmatic in the
  least. For example, he holds the balanced opinion that "_an
  axiomatic characterization of projective space would provide
  the best approach to such a thoroughly unintuitive geometry_".

- Alas, the greatest geometers were too stupid to understand that
  this is the "_best_" approach; "_neither Klein nor his
  predecessors judged [an axiomatic approach] necessary or even
  useful_", because they were not "_aware that their true concern
  was with abstract structures, not with particular things_"

- Some readers may have been uncomfortable with Torretti's
  frequent confessions that he "_fail[s] to see_"  the value of
  so many of the arguments under discussion - indeed, some may
  wonder why he chose write a book about something he admittedly
  understands so little about - but now it is all clear.
- Poor Torretti has to deal with authors who are "not aware" what
  their "true concern" is.

- Lets examine Torretti's treatment of a specific issue: to what
  extent Euclidean geometry should be considered an _empirical_
  theory:
    > "Paradoxically, the propositions of [geometry do] not
    > seem to be liable to empirical corroboration. Since
    > the times of the Greeks, no geometer had ever thought
    > of subjecting his conclusions to the verdict of
    > experiment."

- Now this is just ridiculous, even for Torretti. Geometry is
  plainly the most empirically well-corroborated theory ever
  created. Torretti, however, with his view that the _"true
  concern" of geometry is axiomatics_, believes that "Greek
  mathematicians did not care to determine whether their basic
  premises were true or not" (p. 9).

- Torretti complains that proposed proofs of the parallel
  postulate "depended always explicitly or implicitly on new
  assumptions, no less questionable than the postulate itself",
  and is disappointed that "surprisingly ... no attempt at
  bringing out every presupposition of Euclid and filling all the
  gaps in his proofs was carried out in earnest until the end of
  the 19th century".

- In other words: __Torretti has decided that geometry is about
  axiomatics and has nothing to do with empiricism__, which
  forces him to denigrate every mathematician who worked on the
  parallel postulate and indeed everyone else for not doing
  axiomatics the way he wants.

- Let's assume for the sake of argument that the essence of
  geometry is not _axiomatics_ but _empiricism_. First of all,
  how would Euclid's axioms appear from this point of view?
- Postulates `1` to `4` are very convincing empirically, even in
  a finite universe bounded by the sphere of the fixed stars,
  contrary to Torretti's assertions; for example, postulate `3`
  does not require that "every point can be the centre of
  a circle of any arbitrary radius," but rather that one can
  construct a circle with a given centre and a given radius
  (given as in 'from here to here').

- The parallel postulate, however, is not empirically convincing.
  It makes perfect sense then, that mathematicians should
  concentrate on it. It is not "surprising" that they did not
  bother spelling out all of Euclid's assumptions since these are
  all empirically convincing (e.g., triangle congruencies, etc.).
  Indeed, from the empirical point of view, work on the parallel
  postulate was by no means a naive failure leading only to "new
  assumptions, no less questionable than the postulate itself."

- Instead, such work was very successful in reducing the
  empirically intractable parallel postulate to more tangible
  assumptions. Wallis, for example, derived the parallel
  postulate from the existence of similar figures, i.e., in
  effect, from the existence of squares, since "these figure can
  obviously be multiplied through mere juxtaposition" (p. 45).

### Marvin J. Greenberg says:
    - First of all, strictly speaking, nothing in pure
      mathematics is empirically testable because pure
      mathematics is not about physical reality.
    - That being said, one can ask whether a pure mathematical
      theory provides a good, idealized model of certain aspects
      of physical reality.

    - Even in one's mind one can imagine that the two idealized
      lines in Euclid's fifth postulate (Euclid V for short)
      might approach each other asymptotically instead of
      meeting, as Proclus said. (That can happen in hyperbolic
      geometry.)

    - Or one can imagine those two lines approaching each other,
      then eventually turning away and diverging from each other
      (as can happen in hyperbolic geometry) instead of meeting.

    - So Euclid V is a statement that is not "obviously true" and
      therefore should not be accepted as a postulate, according
      to the classical notion of what is allowed as a postulate.
      So some excellent mathematicians like Legendre labored hard
      unsuccessfully trying to prove Euclid V.

    - (The modern notion of a postulate is that it is an
      assumption that is part of the definition of some
      theoretical structure one wants to study. There is no claim
      that the postulate is "true.")

    - A classical geometer would not have asserted that
      a rectangle "exists." That is a modern form of expression.
      Euclid demonstrated in his Proposition I.46 that a square
      can be constructed on an arbitrary base; his demonstration
      used his parallel postulate. In hyperbolic geometry,
      squares and rectangles do not exist. They do not exist in
      spherical geometry either.

    - You wrote that "_the angle sum theorem for a triangle can
      readily be verified for an actual, physical triangle._" You
      mean Euclid's Proposition I.32 which asserts that the angle
      sum of any triangle equals exactly two right angles.
    - No, that assertion could only be verified for a physical
      triangle within the tolerance of experimental error.

    - In fact, if measurement of an actual, physical triangle
      showed that the angle sum differed from two right angles by
      more than the tolerance of the experimental error, the
      measurement would instead verify that Euclidean geometry is
      _not_ correct for modeling physical reality.
