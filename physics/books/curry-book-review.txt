### Amazon customer (2nd edition)
    - Really like the topics covered here. The biggest problem,
      is the terrible math fonts. In a theoretical physics book
      its vital that the math be written in clear fonts. These
      look like they were taken from a bad dot-matrix printer! 
    - There are also some conceptual errors. The statement that
      vectors are not `n-tuples` is just plain wrong. 
    - Every vector can be represented as an `n-tuple`, but not
      every n-tuple is a vector. 
    - His statement that vectors are "pointy things" is wrong,
      vectors are elements of vector spaces. It is true that
      every spatial vector is an arrow, but not every vector is
      a spatial vector.
    - He goes on to say that column vectors are tangent vectors,
      which is good; then defines the row vectors as the
      transpose of column vectors (also good). Then defines the
      inner product using the covector (row vector) index
      notation when one should be a tangent vector and other
      a convector in terms of the Einstein summation convention.
      This is very sloppy! 
    - There are also lots of formatting problems beyond the bad
      fonts in my copy; in one example he refers to a set of
      equation numbers in a exercise, but no such numbers exist.
      Some of the writing is good, but is full of mistakes,
      typos, and bad formatting.

### Jayne Overgard
    - Volume 1 is devoted to mathematics, and the first six
      chapters forms a mini course in Linear Algebra. It's not
      just fast and short, but it shows the geometric content
      much more clearly than the usual linear algebra textbooks.
      Readers will easily be able to understand linear algebra.
    - Professor Curry emphasizes the difference between a `Linear
      Transform` and its `matrix representation`. And his
      treatment of Eigenvalues diagonalization is very clear.
    - It also has 3 chapters devoted to Lie Groups and Lie
      Algebras. Most elementary treatments describe Lie Groups as
      "Groups of Matrices." Yes, that is how they are often
      realized, but that misses the point.
    - A Lie group is a group which is also a manifold. And a Lie
      algebra is the set of all left invariant vector fields on
      the Lie group. They are connected by the exponential map,
      which sends a vector in the `Lie algebra` to a point along
      a path tangent to that vector down in the group. 
        - What is good about the way Professor Curry approaches
          this subject is that is mature, yet very accessible to
          beginning students.
    - In particular, I loved the Conic Sections. Orbits of
      planets are conic sections, so we need to know more about
      them than is covered in Calculus courses. The last chapter
      does a very neat job of that. The author works in
      parametrizing conic sections, which we need for the physics
      applications.
    - All in all this is an excellent volume and I highly
      recommend it!

### Preptorial review of Curry
    - These amazing books represents 700 pages of state of the
      art math with applications in physics, engineering and
      dynamical systems as well as numerous advanced dynamics
      topics.

    - The book is mis-titled as "Hamiltonians and Lagrangians",
      because even though it has some of the best coverage of
      those areas, it contains FAR MORE "math for dynamics"
      (including physics and engineering) in "leading up" to the
      very thorough, accurate Hamiltonian and Lagrangian topics.
      Eg the 1st volume starts at basic linear algebra and calc,
      and leads all the way up to manifolds, projective geometry
      and kinematics.

    - The other "mis-title" aspect is the quality of the
      instruction. This is subtle. When you read the current
      title, you might assume its a densely packed set of tomes
      on formula after formula with proofs and derivations only
      for graduate physicists or mathematicians. Instead, the two
      texts contain some of the best pure TEACHING of ANY math
      book I've reviewed or used for my online dynamics classes.
      Curry takes a "geometric" approach, taking the time and
      space to explain in words, formulas AND pictures, the point
      of higher math applications.

    - Even better, the author has a deep intuitive grasp of
      what's "really going on" with vectors. He uses this
      understanding as a "theme" for both texts, which gives
      readers a one of a kind feel for ALL advanced math through
      that frame. Even the toughest problems and examples hearken
      back to "Why isn't this like the vectors we've studied?"
      and "How does looking at this from the point of view of
      a vector or matrix allow us to manipulate the coordinate
      axes more easily?" 
    - By then augmenting those insights and intuitive benefits
      geometrically, the books are PACKED with "aha" moments even
      for advanced engineering, math and physics students and
      teachers.

    - The material is intended for undergrad up, but bright AP
      High School students can gain a lot from the orientation
      chapters. I NEVER recommend advanced work if it is too
      tough (and Hamiltonians are tough, as the Swiss Army Knife
      of nearly all of Physics today), but the author clearly had
      _autodidacts_ and engineering/physics types like me in mind
      in including as much intuitive and geometric material as he
      does.

    - Dynamics teachers use 4 "channels" for different problems:
        - ANALYTIC:
            - formulas
        - QUALITATIVE:
            - geometric and graphic
        - NUMERIC:
            - algorithmic starting with "seed" guesses
        - STOCHASTIC:
            - mostly research applications where combinatorics
              get so hairy they are handled as random or chaotic.
            - (Even some oscillators like pendulums are nonlinear
              because they have a sin function, or when they
              "loop over" the top of the pendulum when enough
              force is applied. How much? That's where they
              become nonlinear!).

    - We didn't learn a lot of this in EE school, but dynamics
      (especially ordinary differential equations) abound in
      circuits, and EEs are gradually warming to Hamiltonians.
      This is a GREAT place for catch up it that's you!

    - By the way, the four channels aren't just for learning,
      they also are "required" by the problem design itself,
      because some problems, especially things like higher
      n-body, nonlinear, etc. just CANNOT be solved with
      formulas.

    - Where the PDE or ODE is "understood" however, Hamiltonians,
      Lagrangians, Fouriers, etc. can give a "pseudo" linear look
      that can then be intuitively solved, or at least, as we
      engineers would say, get "close enough!"

    - Highly recommended book. One of the few I'd recommend
      buying on your own even if not required for a class, and
      certainly for self-study. It will fill in a LOT of gaps in
      really "getting" what's hidden in those cryptic proofs and
      far too brief (1.3.27) type formula statements!

    - Frankly, geometry rocks, and this is one of the few authors
      that really gets it, and takes the time to show us with
      concern for his readers. Handholding abounds-- not
      something you can say with many texts today! Takes
      a special writer to avoid "showing off" -- this is a true
      teacher who cares about his readers' grasp of the material
      from many perspectives.

    - From a comparative view, the other author who uses a ton of
      geometry and intuitive examples is:
        - Arnold: Ordinary Differential Equations (Springer)
