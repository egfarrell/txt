### Introduction to functional analysis for scientists and technologists (Vulikh)
    - It is probably true that Kreyszig’s exposition of
      functional analysis is the easiest (perhaps even the best)
      introductory account of the topic. Angus Taylor wrote an
      introduction, it is more challenging than this one. I am
      pleased to say that Vulikh is every bit as useful as the
      previously mentioned expositions: filling the niche between
      those two expositions. Vulikh can serve as preliminary to
      the classic Riesz and Nagy (as their initial ninety pages
      of material is already within the entire compass of
      Vulikh). There are thirteen chapters, 400 pages.
      “Generalization,” that is the pedagogic approach here
      followed. This book takes what you already know and
      generalizes that background. The
      definition-theorem-proof-format is utilized, although that
      format hides the fact that this book is well-written.
      However, no Lebesgue integrals.
    - In contrast to Angus Taylor, there is no discussion of “set
      theory” nor topology (for that, Taylor: chapter zero and
      chapter two). Vulikh is more introductory than Taylor,
      although they complement each other nicely. While the text
      centers around “real” number systems, “complex” concludes
      some chapters (that is: chapter one, two, six, nine and ten
      concludes with complexification).
    - (1) Initial two chapters should be studied as a set.
      Finite, then infinite, Euclidean spaces.
    - (2) Chapter three and four, I also view as a set: distance,
      convergence, continuity. Next, synthesize the previous four
      chapters in preparation for the fifth chapter: Hilbert
      spaces where…we generalize the scalar product.
    - (3) Chapter six and seven go together, study them together.
      That is: “a complete normed space in which the norm is
      generated by scalar-product (Hilbert) followed by
      “functional realization of the separable
      infinite-dimensional Hilbert space.”
    - (4) Chapter seven is exceptional. Beginning with what you
      already know (mean value of an ordinary function over
      a closed interval), utilizing inequalities (p.183), then,
      piecewise-constant functions (p.189), it concludes with
      a brief mention of trigonometric integrals and successive
      approximation (that will be encountered later). Now, read
      the interesting book: Art of Mathematical Inequalities, by
      Steele (2004).
    - (5) Chapter eight and nine comprise another set: from
      linear operator to linear functional. I highlight a pretty
      section discussing “generalized” sums for series (Fejer,
      pages 275-279). If Kreyszig is handy, exercises appear
      (pages 274-275). Now, review chapter one before reading on
      to…
    - (6) Chapter ten: adjoint operators. Revisit integral
      operators (previously: chapter four, p.121, and chapter
      seven, p.211). Integral equations will be encountered again
      in chapter eleven (p.325). Chapter twelve will concern
      itself with approximations methods. The final chapter,
      interesting, is partial ordering: “comparing some pairs of
      elements.”
    - (7) In conclusion: This is a straightforward text.
      Applications are not a central feature. Generalization is
      the consistent theme. It is wonderful companion to Angus
      Taylor (who includes convexity, Kreyszig also has that
      topic in exercises, pages 334-335) and a prerequisite for
      Riesz and Nagy (they cover Lebesgue integral, chapter two,
      and applications to Stone’s theorem, pages 388-390). The
      text is different from the fine elementary text by Shilov:
      Elementary Functional Analysis. Also, read the beautiful
      essay by Gel'fand, Functional Analysis (volume three:
      Mathematics, Its Content, Methods and Meaning, pages
      227-261). If you desire exercises, I stress that Kreyszig
      is almost in a class by himself.
    - So, one pathway: first study Vulikh (alongside Kreyszig),
      then study Angus Taylor, followed by Riesz and Nagy.

### Electrodynamics: an introduction including quantum effects (Muller-Kirsten)
    - Muller-Kirsten: "a teach-yourself course"
    - Recently, I wrote that textbooks on EM were poorly written
      and EM courses poorly taught (a comment made when reviewing
      Vanderlinde’s textbook). I've not (yet) altered my
      position, but, I might add that this intermediate-level
      text almost changes that view. This text is similar to
      Vanderlinde (being detailed) yet different by having no
      tensor methods. The level of difficulty lies between
      Griffiths (elementary) and Jackson (advanced).
      Muller-Kirsten makes the claim: “the text might also serve
      as a teach-yourself course in electrodynamics.”. My
      opinion: That claim is merited.
    - (1) MKS are the units utilized (not my preference). An
      appendix lists vector identities (however, derive those
      previous to study). There is an interesting bibliography
      and footnotes. Do not ignore the footnotes! For instance,
      a footnote (p.425) refers to an unpublished report by
      Fleming, “Noether’s Theorems.” Happily, that report can be
      readily discovered (downloaded) from the web. An appendix
      summarizes Dirac-delta function. However, understand that
      material before study of the text.
    - (2) There are 21 chapters (first edition). As is
      traditional, the text begins with electrostatics. The plan:
      “we adopt the historical and phenomenological approach.”
      Section #2.5 contains Dirac-delta applications (through
      solved-problems). I emphasize the solved-problems
      throughout as they are invaluable. You could almost say
      that the solved-problems are the book (taking up half of
      it). Often, solved-problems here are discovered to be
      student exercises in Jackson. Sometimes the solved-problems
      are relevant for research (p.21, #2.6 is utilized in Edward
      Witten’s 1998 paper “Anti-deSitter Space and Holography,”
      equation #2.18, p. 12). Excellent pedagogic strategy!
    - (3) Green (function) mentioned early (p.19), then
      systematically (chapter three). Minimize energy early (p.
      38). An infinite-product representation for hyperbolic-sine
      invoked (p.50). I highlight an interesting section:
      theoretical aspects of image charges (pages 53-65). Things
      are not always entirely theoretical: the solved-problem
      “measurement of electric susceptibility” is a nice example
      (p.105). It is worth remarking that Muller-Kirsten weaves
      mathematics and physics together in an interesting fashion.
    - (4) One-hundred pages of electrostatics leads to
      magnetostatics (chapter five, p.109). I highlight: Ampere’s
      experiments and the law of Biot-Savart (pages 113-123).
      First instances of magnetic monopoles and gauge invariance
      here (p.123-125). You get a summary of formulas for
      electrostatics (p.106) and a summary of formulas for
      magnetostatics (p.150). Fast-paced, indeed! A third of the
      text is behind us. Onward:
    - (5) There is a nice selection of solved-problems regarding
      electromagnets (p.145). An interesting utilization of
      Gradshteyn’s tables (expansion of natural log of tangent,
      example 6.5, p.147). Read: “Today we are accustomed to the
      concept of fields already intuitively, so that the puzzles
      of long-ago lose their significance.” (p.159). I do not
      wholly agree with that statement. Students may read and use
      the word “field,” but that does not, however, imply
      a physical intuitiveness about it.
    - (6) Another treat: Vector potential of long solenoid. An
      instance of gauge-transformation (p.182). Muller-Kirsten
      details a solved-problem (p.188, finite-length solenoid)
      which Jackson has given as a student exercise (p., 205,
      #5.2). It is plain to see that this book will assist with
      Jackson’s textbook!
    - (7) Read: “In the literature, the Lenz rule is
      frequently--at least on first reading--difficult to
      follow.” (p.156). I wholly agree with this! Electromagnetic
      waves, briefly (chapter nine). The Green function, again
      (p.213), a solved problem, utilizing contour integration).
      The detail presented in chapter ten (moving charges in
      vacuum) is useful for reading Oppenheimer’s lecture notes
      on electrodynamics and serves as supplement to Feynman
      (volume two--or, chapter nine of Garg).
    - (8) My review, already too long. I touch on highlights:
      Born-Infeld Lagrangian invoked for a solved-problem (p.
      442, see Zwiebach, chapter twenty, for the relevance to
      string theory). A continuation of Green’s functions is here
      (relations to causality and dispersion relations, chapter
      sixteen). A footnote (p.377) referring to “an elementary
      method to calculate certain principle-value integrals”
      (1973, Caprasse), can be found online. An excellent chapter
      (seventeen) on covariant formulation (which does not
      utilize ict for 'time-component'). An excellent section
      discusses “masslessness” for electromagnetic field (pages
      434-446). A footnote refers to Freeman Dyson’s paper of
      1990: Feynman’s proof of Maxwell’s Equations. All the more
      interesting because James L. Anderson wrote: “we conclude
      that the reconstruction given by Dyson is not a derivation
      of Maxwell’s equations.” (1990, American Journal of
      Physics). 
    - A nice discussion of Casimir effect concludes the book.
    - (9) Concluding: An excellent addition literature. A mix of
      physics and mathematics, enlivened with detailed, solved,
      examples for the student. The footnotes are informative. If
      J. David Jackson is opaque at any juncture, turn to this
      helpful book for assistance. An excellent resource, highly
      recommended.


### Analysis - Volume 1 (Hille)
    - Hille: stressing historical and theoretical framework
    - I confess to confusion! I recall the days when calculus was
      in the process of a so-called “reform movement.” David
      Mumford asked: “Are we teaching calculus in the hope that
      a small percentage of our students will catch our love of
      rigor, or so that most of our students will emerge with the
      ability to use calculus in their specialties ? " 
    - I ask: Are those aspirations mutually exclusive ? The
      courses presented for my calculus sequence jumped on that
      bandwagon. The primary text utilized at the time was Zorn
      and Ostebee. Now, my confusion stems from the fact that
      I believe that great calculus textbooks have been available
      for many years prior.
    - Here is a list of fine texts: Tom Apostol, Courant, Peter
      Lax, Edwin Moise, Kuratowski, Ostrowski, Spivak, Zeldovich
      and Yaglom, Hille. That list begins with my favorite: Tom
      Apostol. I have reviewed a number of these texts, in their
      own way they are all worthy of perusal. You can acquire
      a fine calculus education from any one of them: better yet,
      all of them! I give reasons to peruse Einar Hille, Analysis
      (volume one), itself a fine textbook!
    - (1) Convexity. I remain fascinated with convexity, Hille
      gets there early (exercises, p.27) and revisits the theme
      on a number of fronts (for instance, pages 164 and 194).
    - (2) Conics. I suppose (these days), conics are slighted
      (they certainly were ignored in my day). I lean towards the
      old-fashioned, so I believe it cannot hurt to be informed
      about conics, Hille does a fine job in that regard, you
      will get much of conics in chapter one.
    - (3) Clusters, or rather, cluster points, begins chapter two
      (p.112). A thorough discussion, including a nice detour on
      differences between conditions that are: necessary,
      sufficient, necessary and sufficient (p.116). This chapter
      (two) is an exceptional discussion of the function concept.
      You will be introduced to oscillation of a function (p.152)
      and, for derivatives: “big O/little O” notation (p.173).
      Differential introduced (p.212).
    - (4) Integration appears much later than is my preference.
      I am convinced that integration should begin every calculus
      course. Hille arrives there on p.223. You get experience
      with inequalities. The exposition is replete with
      interesting examples. The discussion is prelude to infinite
      series (p.276).
    - (5) An appendix introduces complex numbers axiomatically
      (p.322). It will be used later for proof of fundamental
      theorem of algebra (p.362) and utilized for partial
      fractions (section 6.6). Helpful, indeed!
    - (6) The usual trigonometric, exponential and logarithm
      functions are developed (chapter seven). Again, the
      exposition replete with gems: arc length involves the
      entire panoply of facility with inequalities (good
      pedagogy). 
    - Lipschitz condition recounted (p.394). You got that earlier
      (p.243). Of course, infinite series are put to sensible
      use. Hyperbolic functions introduced (p. 410). This chapter
      is an exceptional discussion of all that, and more : pi,
      hyper- geometric functions, differential equations.
    - (7) Curves, that is, tracing (or, plotting) them arrives
      late (chapter nine). A thorough discussion, including
      introductory exposition of projective equivalence (p. 502)
      and algebraic curves (pages 501-510). If you desire more on
      curves and geometric applications of derivative, Goursat is
      a classic, wonderful, reference (volume one).
    - (8) The text concludes with the answers to odd-numbered
      exercises. There are a lot of exercises. The exercises are
      a treat unto themselves. Hille weaves historical
      perspective throughout. The indebtedness to Vallee
      Poussin’s Course of Analysis (1909) is mentioned (preface)
      and is evident throughout (that great text free to download
      from archive.org).
    - (9) So, back to calculus reform. I ask, what has it
      accomplished ? Rigor and applications are not mutually
      exclusive enterprises. There never was a time when great
      calculus textbooks were not available for perusal. I began
      a study of calculus in high-school. I enjoyed the topic
      then, I love the topic now more than ever. Hille is one of
      many avenues available for a fine education in calculus.


### Mathematics: Its Content Methods & Meaning by  Kolmogorov etc
    - a do-it-yourself course" (New York Times Book Review)
    - I first learned of this multi-volume exposition in
      high-school (that was the late 1970s). Certainly, I read
      volume one: algebra, calculus, ordinary differential
      equations.
    - I never did get around to the other two volumes until much
      later. That delay was a mistake: getting around to volume
      two (and three) later in life deprived me of many
      mathematical gems
    - Volume 3 is my favorite of the set. The foreword will
      explain why: “Whether a physicist wishes to know what a Lie
      algebra is or how it is related to Lie groups...they will
      find here a connected, lucid account.”
    - Had I read chapter twenty, groups and other algebraic
      systems (seventy-five pages), I do believe my interest in
      group theory would have waxed, and not waned.
    - My primary source for the topic had been Georgi (his 1982
      book, Lie Algebras in Particle Physics, first edition). His
      book made so little sense to me that I turned away from
      group theory! Of course, the problem was not with his book,
      the problem was with my background: I had precious little
      to build upon.
    - This is where this book is unsurpassed. You build
      background. Volume three builds background in real variable
      theory, linear algebra, functional analysis, non-Euclidean
      geometry, topology and groups (of course, the previous two
      volumes are sufficient background for the third). A closer
      view:
    - (1) Beginning with sets (30 page exposition). Learn of
      Dedekind, Cantor, Weierstrauss (p.16), a prelude to
      a qualitative discussion of Lebesgue integral. Read: “There
      is a very close link between closed and open sets, they are
      complements of each other. Because of this it is sufficient
      to study either closed or open sets only (p.23). For
      Lebesgue integral, I am reminded of the appendix in
      Sokolnikoff and Redheffer: being both brief and
      qualitative.
    - (2) Linear algebra begins with linear transformation, moves
      to determinants, matrices and eigenvalues (a path taken by
      others--Smirnov’s Course, volume 3, part I that exposition
      has been termed old-fashioned, MAA reviews-- but that does
      not imply that it is not useful). Faddeev writes with
      clarity of prose. A nice proof is presented (p.90),
      emphasizing the role of inequalities.
    - (3) Non-Euclidean geometry will consume ninety-pages of
      text. This is a lovely exposition. Application to rotating
      bodies (qualitative), is presented (pages 175-176). Struik
      and Synge are there in the “suggested readings“ which
      conclude each chapter. The esteemed Aleksandrov is as lucid
      as ever. Next, Topology (twenty-five pages). Read: “it was
      in the framework of projective geometry that Lobachevsky's
      non-Euclidean geometry was for the first time completely
      understood.” (p.200). Projective geometry should be exposed
      to more undergraduates (about which, more in Hilton and
      Griffiths).
    - (4) Functional Analysis: written by the great Gel’fand.
      A wonderful exposition: begin with scalar product,
      introducing Hilbert space, then orthogonal systems,
      finally, an introduction to integral equations. I save the
      best for last, as the concluding chapter of volume 3 is
      algebraic systems and groups. I am reminded of Birkhoff and
      MacLane. Learn this at the end of volume 3: “A considerable
      number of the theorems in the theory of groups and in the
      theory of rings, are statements on the arrangement of
      subgroups, normal subgroups and ideals; consequently, these
      theorems can be reformulated as theorems on the lattice of
      subgroups or ideals.” (p. 349). Beautiful!
    - (5) Concluding: Study all 3 volumes. You are certain to
      learn much: both qualitative and quantitative. Probably,
      these volumes should be required reading for all maths and
      physics undergraduates. Include these volumes alongside
      books by Saunders MacLane and Hilton and Griffiths. Highly
      recommended!

### Comprehensive Textbook of Classical Mathematics (Hilton, Griffiths)
    - mathematical excursions: both elementary and advanced
    - Here is one of those classics which is (apparently)
      destined to fall into obscurity. This is a sprawling book
      (600 pages), reminiscent of Saunders MaClane's excellent
      book, "Mathematics, Form and Foundation." However, there is
      very little overlap between these two publications. Here we
      have a prime example of a text written with the "spiral"
      approach in mind: "we believe that topics should reappear
      in any well-planned syllabus. Typically a topic, treated in
      the first place for its own sake, will be reconsidered
      later as a part of broader generalization, or as
      exemplifying a feature somewhat too subtle to be understood
      and appreciated at the initial exposure." (introduction).
    - If you do not care for such an approach, this book is not
      then for you. Otherwise, you are sure to enjoy the
      exposition ! What to discover ?
    - (1) Sets (fifteen pages): This introduction is perfect
      companion to Halmos, Naive Set Theory. From sets to
      functions, this discussion is one that I like quite a bit.
      Examples aplenty which cement terminology: injective,
      surjective, bijective. You are introduced to "commutative
      diagram" (p.32) and composition of functions. Third chapter
      revisits the theme (functions) through beautiful exposition
      of cartesian-product. Learn of relations, conclude with
      induction.
    - (2) Part Two continues a topic of chapter one, Sets
      (again). The authors write: "not essential reading for what
      follows." (p.71). The interesting discussion here is that
      permutations and combinations are introduced through
      a set-theory approach (pages 79-85). Permutations
      introduced as an example of injective function. How nice!
      Sets will occupy the following pages (up to p.120) with
      notions such as 'axiom of choice'(p.96) and Boolean algebra
      (p.110).
    - (3) Sets and functions, they comprised parts one and two.
      Now, part 3: Arithmetic. Read this: "generations of
      schoolchildren have been bored and resentful at being
      turned into slow, inefficient computers, who think they
      loathe 'mathematics,' when in fact they have never met
      a truly mathematical idea in all of the many hours spent in
      arithmetic classes." (p.121). Following which, basically
      put, introductory number theory. A highlight (at least,
      a favorite topic of mine) is the discussion of continued
      fractions (itself beginning with partial fractions).
    - (4) Let us move to part four: Geometry. Among the topics,
      meet vectors, linear independence (p.184), convexity
      (p.188), scalar and cross products. After this elementary
      approach to vector algebra we meet: linear Algebra (chapter
      fifteen). The linear algebra is presented prior to an
      elaborate discussion of length, area, volume. An
      interesting discussion. Now we come to another highlight
      (at least, a favorite of mine): Projective Geometry. Read:
      "a beautiful example of the growth of a theory is the
      subject called projective geometry, at least as far as the
      theory of conics" (p.258) and, "it is not often presented
      in traditional textbooks." Learn of this fascinating topic,
      it continues for 25 pages.
    - (5) Parts 1 - 4: Sets, functions, arithmetic, geometry.
      Part five: algebra. You will get an introduction to groups.
      Then, another stab at vector spaces and linear equations
      (we met them already). Additional abstraction with
      inner-products. From there, another look at Boolean algebra
      and
    - inequalities: "It is a common experience that students who
      have studied algebra in a traditional school-course are
      well able to handle equations, but are quite unable to
      manipulate (and, perhaps understand) inequalities." (page
      338).
    - I have to agree with this statement. The conclusion to part
      5 entails the fundamental theorem of algebra.
    - (6) Part Six: number systems and topology. "We do not
      answer the question 'What is a number ?' since
      mathematicians do not need to ask this question--and it
      would never occur to anybody else." (p.372). The number
      systems (rational, real, complex) then to a sketch of
      topology. A nice discussion of quotient space (pages 430).
      From there (quotient space) to homology and manifolds
      (qualitative introductions).
    - (7) We began the text with sets, moved on to functions,
      progressed through arithmetic, then algebra and geometry.
    - The penultimate chapters cover limiting processes and
      calculus (pages 451-567). Read:
    - "it was often used as a criterion for allowing
      undergraduates to proceed further in mathematics, and it
      was taught badly, so that many students were put-off
      mathematics, simply by failing to learn analysis." (page
      451). I have to agree with this (as it still applies).
    - Without spoiling the fun, here is the goal: "we attempt to
      expound elementary calculus in a manner which we hope will
      serve as a halfway house for students, between the early
      'intuitive' and the later 'rigorous' approaches."
      A highlight: vector-valued functions (pages 548 through
      558). The material here carries one to the brink of
      multivariable calculus. Advertisement is given for the
      excellent text, Mathematical Analysis (by Tom Apostol),
      suggested as one avenue of continuation.
    - (7) The conclusion returns to foundations: A superb
      introduction to categories and functors.
    - If you desire more of categories and functors I refer to
      Saunders MacLane: Mathematics, Form and Foundation.
    - (8) Conclusion: This really is a textbook, as there are
      exercises scattered throughout. The exercises are both
      manipulative and conceptual; both elementary and advanced.
      Helpful hints are provided. You will get aquainted with
      proof. You develop mathematical maturity. You are
      challenged. As prime example of "the spiral" methodology of
      pedagogy, Griffiths and Hilton are to be congratulated.
    - Highly recommended to anyone with an interest in
      mathematics. 



### Quantum Gravity (Rovelli)
    - "how to rethink physics from scratch" (?) (dust-jacket)
    - I have followed trends in QG since the 1970s and 3 Oxford
      conferences were devoted to the topic:
        - Quantum Gravity, An Oxford Symposium (1975), 
        - Quantum Gravity 2 (1981) 
        - Quantum Concepts in Space and Time (1986). 
    - There is much of interest in each of those volumes (the
      second is my favorite).
    - Regarding my own evolution of thought, although Rovelli's
      book is interesting, textbooks on string theory are at
      least as interesting (Zweibach, Becker and West are the
      three I have studied from).
    - In the end, I am neither a proponent of string theory nor
      loop quantum gravity. I do, however, advocate the study of
      both approaches simultaneously. Neither should be ignored.
    - Progressing through Rovelli's monograph, his words: 
    - (1) "The world is made up of fields. Physically, these do
      not live on spacetime. They live, so to say, on one
      another. No more fields on spacetime, just fields on
      fields." (p. 75).
    - (2) "There is a one-to-one correspondence between
      instantaneous states and relativistic states." (p.116). 
    - (3) "In a fully relativistic context, mechanics is the
      theory of correlations between partial observables." (p.
      120). 
    - (4) "The time coordinate in Maxwell's theory is a partial
      observable. The time-coordinate in GR is an unphysical
      parameter." (p.127). 
    - (5) " On the other hand, the conventional structure of QM
      is certainly incomplete in the light of general
      relativity." (p.179). 
    - (6) "In QM, a complete experiment consists of two events,
      a preparation and a quantum measurement." (p.198). 
    - (7) "A key result of the theory developed in the 2nd part
      of his book (LQG) is that the eigenstates of the
      gravitational field on a 3D surface are not smooth fields."
      (p.202). 
    - (8) "The notion of vacuum is strictly connected to the
      notion of energy." (p.203). 
    - The lines above are extracts from part one. Those pages are
      a preliminary to part two where you get loop quantum
      gravity in a span of 150 pages. 
    - From there, three appendices (A,B,C): Groups, History and
      Reflections of Methodology: "the best we can say about the
      physical world, and about what is physically real out
      there, is what good physics says about it." (p.423). 
    - Let us learn more: 
        - (1) Read: "the most surprising and intriguing aspect of
          LQG is the fact that it predicts an inflationary phase
          in the expansion of the early universe." (p.301). 
        - (2) I highlight a discussion of Black Hole
          thermodynamics (pages 301-315). Read of "fluctuations
          of the shape of the horizon" (p.304), Wheeler's "it
          from bit" (p.310) and Mukhanov's "discrete emission
          spectrum" (p.313). More from Wheeler and Mukhanov in
          the book "Complexity, Entropy and the Physics of
          Information." 
        - (3) I highlight Tullio Regge: “The surprising discovery
          of Ponzano and Regge was that the 6j symbol
          approximates the action of GR.” (p.336). 
        - (4) Rovelli updated in 2008 Living Reviews article
          (available free, online): "I give here a general
          overview of ideas, techniques, results and open
          problems of this candidate theory of quantum gravity,
          and a guide to the relevant literature." 
        - (5) Turn to the APS, Carlo Rovelli: "Loop quantum
          gravity — a theory that extends GR by quantizing
          spacetime — predicts that black holes evolve into white
          holes." (December 10, 2018• Physics 11, 127). 
        - (6) Ultimately, my review is not much of a book review.
          Partly, this is due to the issue I have with all of the
          QG proposals: no experimental evidence to substantiate
          anything (yet). 
        - The bibliographic notes are quite useful, the 25 page
          reference list is invaluable (for instance:
          Mathematical Apparatus of the Theory of Angular
          Momentum, 1962, by Yutsin et.al., is now online)
    - (7) Back to the beginning of my review: can we really
      expect to "rethink physics from scratch" ? 
    - The reader will have to decide if this book actually gets
      close to accomplishing that aim (I am dubious). Parting
      words from Steven Weinberg: " I still expect there to be
      a final theory, but I’m less confident that humans will
      discover it in this century." (2015, Scientific American
      interview).


### Quantum Field Theory (Mark Srednicki)
    - "step by step presentation illustrated by simple examples"
    - I am not in favor of single-volume QFT textbooks, as
      I consider Weinbergs 3-volume opus to be the finest
      available. 
    - That said, there are many single-volume textbooks on the
      market, so its impossible to recommend any one over the
      other! However as a single-volume introduction Srednicki is
      rather useful. 
    - Note: the preface (for students) exhibits eight equations.
      Those equations are the bare-minimum necessary to
      contemplate perusal of the book (they are necessary, but
      not sufficient). 
    - I have an axe to grind in that respect, for it is not the
      equations which a student should have memorized, it is
      really down to how a student arrives at those equations and
      how those equations are utilized for the solution of other
      problems. Onward:
    - (1) The first problem in the text asks that Dirac matrices
      must be even-dimensional (Bjorken and Drell do this on
      their p.8), while the final problem (multi-step) deals with
      predicting the mass of the tau-lepton. Between that gulf
      there lies three semesters worth of material. The preface
      (for instructors) offers a guide to a shorter route
      (assailing the material in a year).
    - (2) I cannot offer a viewpoint on this text if used in an
      actual course. Therefore, I concentrate on one aspect,
      a phrase in the preface: “if, however, you are reading this
      book on your own…” That is, its efficacy for self-study! 
    - Is Srednicki useful for self-study ? 
    - In my opinion, he scores high on that account (confessing,
      that the 2014 Lancaster and Blundell is even simpler for
      self-study). Repetition of material is an effective
      strategy and Srednicki utilizes it often. That is, you
      learn the material, you do not memorize equations. 
    - One has merely to progress through the chapters of quantum
      electrodynamics to see the efficacy of this strategy. Read:
      “the action we seek should be Lorentz invariant, gauge
      invariant, parity and time-reversal invariant, and no more
      than second order in derivatives,” (p.337) 
    - ... then “we can follow the analysis of section five line
      by line to deduce the LSZ formula for photons, “ (p.344)
      finally “using the methods of section eight we will
      evaluate the path integral for photons.” (p.349). The
      methodology is effective!
    - (3) We progress up the ladder numerically: from spin zero
      to spin one-half to spin one. If you have seen T.D. Lee’s
      text (1981) you will see that he takes the same approach (a
      pity that T.D. Lee’s book is not included in the
      bibliography). Here, you get path integrals early (p. 43),
      T.D Lee gets there (path integrals) on his p.465. Steven
      Weinberg gets there (his volume one) at p.376, Peskin and
      Schroeder, p.275. George Sterman gets there early (p.58),
      and Anthony Zee, earlier still (p.7). 
    - The big question is when to present path integrals, earlier
      or later ? 
    - Reading Steven Weinberg: “there are important theories in
      which the simplest version of the Feynman path-integral
      method, in which propagators and interaction vertices are
      taken directly from the Lagrangian, is simply wrong.”
      (p.377). 
    - I am with Steven Weinberg on this one, so later it is!
      _Shankar_ is perfect ancillary material for path integrals.
    - (4) Elementary-Particle Physics, that is the primary focus,
      so applications to condensed matter or statistical
      mechanics are absent (there are many outstanding books to
      choose from for those applications). 
    - Lagrangians: that is the primary focus (p.23). If you do
      not believe that, look at the problem sets: #3.5, #5.1,
      #8.7 (the last two are tied to the first). In fact,
      Srednicki is very much into the pedagogy of writing down
      a Lagrangian and then analyzing all its symmetries. 
    - Recall Peskin and Schroeder : “we thus consider the
      Lagrangian to be the most fundamental specification of
      a quantum field theory.” (p. 283). Recall Steven Weinberg:
      “it is easier to explore physically satisfactory theories
      by listing possible Lagrangians.” (p.299). I highlight the
      excellent chapters 26 to 28, divergences and
      renormalization.
    - (5) I recall a line from a famous text: “it is no longer
      possible to present perturbative field theory pedagogically
      in one year - the days of the standard one-year course on
      QED are gone forever.” (1990, Ramond, second edition,
      preface). 
    - That may be true, but Srednicki is pretty close to
      achieving that goal, and much more!
    - (6) Concluding: I like the pedagogic strategy here. These
      days, you can pick and choose an introductory textbook to
      your heart’s content. I will never replace my copies of
      Steven Weinberg, or Peskin and Schroeder. 
    - As a beginning choice, Srednicki is accessible (perhaps,
      even great). I would welcome a second edition, as the
      binding of the present edition suffers. 
    - Final remark: final sections (roughly 25 pages) deal with
      supersymmetry and grand unification. I confess an aversion
      to preliminary talk of so-called grand unification. Of
      course, that is simply my opinion--and, everyone is
      entitled to theirs. In any event, it can probably do no
      harm to look at it here.

### GR: an Einstein Centenary Survey (Hawking, Israel)
    - plenty enough for a lifetime of thought
    - Published 1979. Heres a book that I acquired long ago
      (1981). At that time there was no conceivable way
      everything in this Survey book would have made sense to me.
    - Reading these essays today (2019) I achieve a new-found
      appreciation for the riches contained between these covers.
    - We have a “ Who’s Who” of contributors, including such
      luminaries as Stephen Hawking, Steven Weinberg and Bryce
      Dewitt (singled-out only because I've studied their works
      more extensively than the others). Time for a tour (and, an
      update):
    - (1) An introduction (Hawking and Israel). I like to
      supplement the survey with the lovely non-technical
      article: The Quantum Mechanics of Black Holes (Scientific
      American, 1976). Hawking and Israel: “The eventual hope is
      to find a Grand Unified Theory which will incorporate all
      particles and all interactions.” (p.23).
    - (2) Clifford Will presents the confrontation between
      experiment and theory. Read: “GR predicts two independent
      sates of polarization. GR is probably unique in that
      prediction; every other known viable metric theory of
      gravity predicts more than two polarizations for the
      generic gravitational wave.” (p. 63). 
        - 2014: This material is updated (ArXiv.org 1403: 7377).
    - (3) Braginsky and Douglas offer a semi-qualitative
      discussion of gravitational radiation experiments. Nearing
      their conclusion (p.129) mention of interferometers: “The
      direction of the sources of gravitational radiation could
      be inferred from phase differences, or arrival times; and
      correlation of pulses with optical observations could be
      used to determine the speed of propagation.” (p.137). 
        - 2016 Update: Observation of gravitational waves from
          a binary black hole merger (Journal Physical Review
          Letters, LIGO Scientific Collaboration and Virgo
          Collaboration.
    - (4) Next, a tough essay, Marsden and Fischer. Read: “there
      are no known theorems about PDE's which can be applied to
      resolve the Cauchy problem in GR.” (p.184).
        - Reference is made to an essay by Moncrief (1978). The
          essay, Quantum Linearization Instabilities, is quite
          fascinating and freely accessible from the Gravity
          Research Foundation webpage. 
        - An update: 2002 Cargese conference: “50 years of the
          Cauchy Problem in GR” is available online (audio and
          video).
    - (5) Geroch and Horowitz provide a wonderful article on
      global structures in spacetime. The repertoire: ten
      definitions, eight methods of proof, seven techniques,
      examples, exercises (answers, p.293). This is a lovely
      essay. Robert Geroch offers more on his academic website
      (copious lecture notes).
    - (6) Brandon Carter presents a wonderful survey of Black
      Hole properties. It is almost self-contained (but,
      background assumed Misner, Thorne, Wheeler). His notation
      uses differential forms. 
        - Note the interesting remark regards teleology: “rather
          bizarre feature…” (p.311). 
        - Supplement: Thorne and MacDonald: Black Holes, The
          Membrane Paradigm (1986). I highlight this remark and

          the surface of the horizon.” (p.326).
    - (7) Chandrasekhar offers a masterful survey of Kerr metric
      and perturbations. Read: "The formalism of Newman and
      Penrose is peculiarly adapted to its study." (p.370).
        - A great article, it makes perfect companion to his
          later book: The Mathematical Theory of Black Holes
          (1983). 
        - He concludes: “It is a matter for some astonishment
          that the entire set of Newman-Penrose equations
          governing gravitational perturbations can be solved
          explicitly.” (p.453).
    - (8) I see my review is getting out-of hand, therefore, jump
      ahead to Quantum Gravity (Bryce DeWitt) and Ultraviolet
      Divergences (Steven Weinberg).
    - (A) Bryce DeWitt: 
        - “According to GR, spacetime itself is the medium” and
          “in principle, gravitational radiation could be used as
          a propellant.” (p.681). 
        - Also: “GR must be wedded to the quantum theory if

          assured” 
        - and “therefore one may correctly speak of the black

        - Learn: “The one-loop approximation in field theory is

        - and “the Feynman integral is widely believed to have

          702 and 721, respectively). 
        - A fantastic essay of sixty pages! We can all learn from
          Bryce Dewitt.
    - (B) Weinberg: 
        - “GR is a prime example of a theory with dangerous
          interactions.” (p.790) 
        - and “It has been shown that massless particles of
          spin-two must be described by an effective field theory
          satisfying the Principle of Equivalence.” (p.796) 
        - and “it is somewhat of a mystery why the cosmological

        - In any event, supplement Weinberg with Weinberg:

        - A veritable feast!

### Dynamical Theory of Groups and Fields
    - "it has no analog in the world literature"
    - Lets read from an essay in honor of the 60th Birthday of
      Bryce Dewitt: "But, the book cannot be found. When it first
      appeared, no one could understand anything of it. Now,
      twenty years later, The Dynamical Theory of Groups and
      Fields has long been out-of-print and everybody finds they
      need it. Nothing in its place can be recommended except
      a second edition, as it has no analog in the world
      literature." (p.195, 1984, Vilkovisky, The Gospel According
      to Bryce Dewitt, Quantum Theory of Gravity).
    - There can be no better recommendation than Vilkovisky for
      its perusal!
    - Happily, I own a copy, so here are a few words from Bryce
      Dewitt, taken from this monograph:
    - (1) "We shall adopt an over-all space-time view from the
      outset and ignore canonical formulations." (p.1). Chapter
      one will introduce functional derivatives. Learn this:
      "Quantum theory is basically a theory of small
      disturbances." Learn here why Dewitt makes that statement.
    - (2) Read: "It appears, therefore, that in choosing
      physically admissible fields, one is in relativistic
      quantum theory severely limited; namely, to fields
      describing particles of unique spin satisfying dynamical
      equations of no higher than second order." (p.59). An
      interesting discussion of electromagnetic and linearized
      gravitational fields presented (p.69). Here, free fields
      (no interactions) will be presented initially (to p.76),
      then read:
    - (3) "It appears that the only non-compact Lie group which
      plays a direct role in a physical theory is the Lorentz
      group, which enters the theory in a slightly different way
      from the groups associated with the Yang-Mills field."
      (p.89). 
        - The definition for the terminology "Yang-Mills field"
          will be expounded a bit later (p.99, "a universal
          field").
    - (4) DeWitt: "The metric tensor may evidently be taken as
      symmetric, since any anti-symmetric part would make no
      contribution to the distance concept." (p.110) and the
      "formalism of tensors allows one to abandon the Euclidean
      origins of the metric concept once it has outlived its
      usefulness as an initial guide." (p.111). 
        - You will be introduced to the so-called vierbein
          formalism, spinor fields will be introduced and "the
          introduction of vierbein field is essential for this."
          (p.115). 
        - Read: "Observables, themselves, must be quantities of
          the commuting type." (p.123).
    - (5) Geodetic interval defined, p.149. This is essentially
      Synge's "world-function." If you want more regards Synge’s
      word-function, read his text on General Relativity. DeWitt
      presents a brief account (pp.148-151) because “all of the
      above geometrical quantities enter directly into the
      structure of the Green’s function for arbitrary fields.”
      (p.152).
    - (6) Read: “When space-time possesses no symmetries, laws of
      conservation of energy-momentum and angular momentum can be
      obtained only by restoring dynamical degrees of freedom to
      the gravitational field and imposing suitable boundary
      conditions.” (p.165).
    - (7) Time reversal, an interesting discussion: “the
      essential sign ambiguity associated with the definition of
      time; hence, theory of canonical transformations must make
      a definite choice which gives time a ‘one-way’ character.“
      (p.169).
    - (8) Schwinger variation principle, next. Read: “We now
      begin the final task of casting the full QFT into a form
      from which physical information may be readily extracted.
      One of the most useful preliminary tools for this purpose
      is the Schwinger variation principle.” (p.173). 
    - Of scattering: “the general scattering process may be
      regarded as a sum over all possible scatterings.” (p.206).
    - (9) Finally: the problems. They occur within each section.
      There are more than 100 of them. Few are merely routine.
        - Problem #77: "...suggests that geometry should perhaps
          form the foundation for all of physics." (p.139). 
        - Regards that problem, Chris Isham writes: "the
          self-help exercises form an integral part of the
          course...problem 77 requires the student to work out,
          what is in effect, the non-Abelian version of the old
          Kaluza-Klein theory. Another striking anticipation of
          later developments." (p.31, Quantum Theory of Gravity,
          1984, Adam Hilger).
    - (10) If you are unable to locate this book, locate a copy
      of Relativity, Groups and Topology (1964). That earlier
      book contains the original 1963 Les Houches lectures.
    - Bryce Dewitt is both challenging and rewarding.



### Lie Groups and Algebras with Applications to Physics,
Geometry, and Mechanics (Sattinger)
    - "a course between descriptive and abstract mathematical
      approaches"
    - Before you read page one of this slim text, you need the
      basics of group theory behind you. Reading the preface:
    - "familiarity with the basic ideas of group theory which are
      traditionally taught at the undergraduate level." Now, that
      was written 1986 (publishing date of the book). I did not
      know then (1986) nor do I know now (2019), which basics of
      group theory are "traditionally taught" at the
      undergraduate level (at least, as far as physics is
      concerned). The book I recommend reading for basics is
      Survey of Modern Algebra (Birkhoff and MacLane).
    - (1) The text is 200 pages, parts A through E. There is
      a three-page bibliography. The notation: Groups are
      capitalized, Algebras in small font (excellent pedagogy).
      There are exercises, these conclude every section (that is
      good). Here is a sample problem #4 (p.eight):
        - "Projective three-space is the set of lines through the
          origin in R^4, open sets are open cones of lines. Show
          that projective three-space is homeomorphic to the
          three-sphere in R^4 with antipodal points identified.
          Then show that this sphere is homeomorphic to the solid
          ball in R^3 with antipodal points identified, that is,
          to SO(3)." 
        - As the reader can ascertain, this is a challenging
          textbook.
    - (2) Enjoy the brief exposition of Mobius group (pages 8-10)
      and continuation in the discussion of its subgroup "that
      corresponds to rotations of the Riemann sphere." (pages
      10-16). Terminology: quotient group and cosets, will be
      used in the discussion of Lorentz group. You will utilize
      polar decomposition of a matrix (problem #4, p.18).
    - Exercises are interlinked: for an example of this pedagogic
      device, see p.15 (exercises one through three).
    - (3) Chapter two (Lie Algebras) will be easier to assimilate
      than the first chapter. You will need information from p.26
      for the discussion on p.121 (ideals of Lie algebra). Do not
      to skip anything in this book! 
    - Now, chapter two contains no exercises. Third chapter,
      a continuation of the second, focusing upon matrix
      representations of Lie groups. Here is the scenario thus
      far: groups, algebra, then combining the two. Section
      twelve (p.38) will "take a closer look at the relationship
      between a group and its algebra." A nice discussion.
    - (4) Mathematics for three chapters. Now, Physics. A brief
      tour of classical dynamics, rigid-body-rotation,
      Schwinger's "idea of using creation and annihilation
      operators" (p.51) which also will be utilized in chapter
      twelve. Though a brief exposition, all is lucidly
      accomplished. The exposition is dense with material, so
      read each line carefully.
    - (5) Geometry, is next. We read: "the operations of vector
      analysis are tied to the Euclidean metric of R^3, whereas
      the exterior derivative is not." (p.65). You use the
      so-called "chain rule" often. Frame invariance emphasized
      (p.72).
    - (6) Sixth chapter, a favorite: Symmetry groups of
      differential equations. Meet jet bundles, prolongations.
      Symmetries and conservation laws concludes this chapter.
      Reference is made to the excellent book by Olver:
      Applications of Lie Groups to Differential Equations.
    - (7) Seven is a continuation of six, that is, more geometry.
      We abstract from linearity to "any Lie group." (p.95). We
      abstract from exterior derivative to "the more general
      notion" of Riemannian connection. (p.99). Reference is made
      to the excellent book by Barrett O'Neill: Elementary
      Differential Geometry. Chapter eight, said to be an
      optional chapter, presents confluence of the big ideas:
      groups, algebras, differential geometry. It offers
      comparison of classical to modern approaches. Excellent
      pedagogy!
    - (8) Geometry behind us, Algebra ahead of us (chapter nine).
      One typo mars the discussion (p.120, line five, that
      properties 2.5 and 2.6 should be changed to properties 2.7
      and 2.8, refers back to p.28). After which, the reader is
      able to follow along to the derivations as presented on
      this p.. Meet terms such as: diagonalizeable (semisimple),
      commutation, eigenvectors. Read: "Lie's theorem may be
      viewed as a generalization of the theorem that an algebra
      of semisimple operators is simultaneously diagonalizeable
      if, and only if, it is abelian." (p.125, notation alert:
      iff).
    - (9) Chapter ten is interesting: Structure of semisimple
      algebras. Begin with the concrete, su(2) and sl(3,C).
      Afterward, the discussion moves to abstraction. Read:
      "every semisimple algebra can be reconstructed from its
      vector figure." (p.141). Pay attention to notation (p.
      148): "we consider nXn matrices, whose entries are 2X2
      matrices."
    - (10) Representation Theory. A discussion beginning with
      a concrete example, then onward to abstraction. Tensor
      products (section 41) are lucidly described (pages
      175-179). We read: "Lie derivatives act as derivations on
      tensor products." A brief discussion of Spinors and
      Clifford algebras follows. Onsager theory of phase
      transitions is referenced as an application of Lie algebra
      (although, not itself discussed in any manner). We conclude
      with a brief discussion of spontaneous symmetry breaking
      (pages 200-207). All in all, a satisfying place to end this
      crisp text.
    - (11) Concluding my review: I rather enjoyed this brief
      text. Excellent exercises. Terse discussion of a few
      applications.
    - Always proceeding from concrete examples to mathematical
      abstraction. This is a challenging text, yet rewarding.
    - Highly recommended for enrichment.

      Preface: "this book is also designed to be used for
      self-study."
    - That is a tall order. I believe the author succeeds in that
      endeavor. While the exercises do not include complete
      answers, they are (in many cases) accompanied by hints at
      how to arrive at the answer.
    - (2) It is true that you will not get applications to such
      topics as grand unification (always a dubious enterprise).
      Thus, there is little duplication of such things as can be
      found in Georgi's book (first edition). Reading Tung: "the
      material we have covered should provide adequate background
      and concrete experience for the reader to explore both the
      more general mathematical theory and the diverse physical
      applications." (p.290). Therefore, I suggest perusing
      Tung previous to perusing Howard Georgi (I could hardly do
      that in 1982, as Tung's book was only written after the
      first edition of Georgi.).
    - (3) Tung eschews discussion of crystal lattices. If you
      desire such material, Morton Hamermesh is a great place to
      get it. Also, quite enlightening on that score is
      Sternberg: Group Theory and Physics (1994).
    - (4) Some exercises can be located in other books, for
      instance: "Consider the dihedral group, which is the
      symmetry group of the square...enumerate the group
      elements, the classes, the subgroups, the factor groups..."
      (#2.8, p.26). If you need the correct answer, you can
      locate the answer in a number of resources. Howard Georgi,
      as an exercise, in his 1982 book: Lie Algebras in Particle
      Physics (see p.15), asks the student "to prove Schur's
      Lemma..." Now, if you can prove Schur's lemma on your own,
      after studying the previous fourteen pages of Georgi's
      exposition, you do not need Tung's exposition. Mere
      mortals, though, turn to Tung who expounds upon it (and
      proof, his pages 37 and 38).
    - (5) I provide another example which can be found in other
      resources: "We apply the group-theoretical notions to
      a familiar system, a single particle in a central
      potential. We shall see that a number of important results
      can be inferred from symmetry considerations..." (p.
      110). Now, exercise #7.3 (p.124) reads: "from
      geometrical considerations, derive the following result
      which describes the effect of the rotation on an arbitrary
      vector..." Sattinger and Weaver, in their exercise #5 (p.
      15), Lie Groups and Algebras with Applications, ask the
      reader to "...prove..." the same formula in different
      guise. This brings up another point: Tung's is
      a physicist's approach, inclined to a more physical
      approach, while Sattinger and Weaver is pitcheded to the
      mathematically mature. Morton Hamermesh (elementary) and
      Eugene Wigner (advanced) are also physically inclined
      expositions.
    - (6) A few highlights (as I perceive them): Tung works-out
      the details, in detail! Almost all intermediate steps are
      in plain sight (for instance, Young diagrams, not at all
      mysterious here: see chapter five). An instance where Tung
      has made his mark: relation between representations of the
      Lorentz and Poincare groups, relativistic wave functions,
      fields and wave equations (see pages 202-209).
    - (7) Concluding my review (conceding that I have barely
      scratched the surface of this wonderful textbook), Tung
      really does satisfy the needs of the self-study student and
      serves as springboard to advanced material. If Steven
      Weinberg points to this book as reference, one does best
      not to ignore his advice.
    - Highly recommended textbook!
    - Physics Today: Wu-Ki Tung, " among the most influential
      theoretical high-energy physicists of his generation," died
      on March 30th, 2009.



### Symmetry in Physics: Principles and Simple Applications Volume 1 (Elliott)
    - finally, a truly elementary and lucid exposition!
    - I have, of late, spent an inordinate amount of time and
      energy digesting the various expositions of Group Theory.
    - There are two varieties: the primarily mathematical
      textbooks, then physics textbooks (that is, physics
      textbooks which attempt the impossible: teaching both
      topics, group theory and physics, simultaneously). I must
      say, for the most part, the mathematicians win in this
      endeavor (of writing exceptional expositions of Group
      Theory). However, there is one elementary text which
      I found exceptional, the two-volume work of Elliott and
      Dawber. Volume One (principles) will serve to whet the
      appetite, as it is preliminary for the second volume. Let
      us look closer:
    - (1) Elliott and Dawber begin ever so gingerly. They do not
      rush into things (as I fear Georgi, 1982, does). Elliott
      and Dawber do not rush into elementary particle theory (as
      I fear Georgi, 1982, does), nor do they rush into
      external-symmetry of crystals (which Hamermesh accomplishes
      quite well) or condensed matter (as expounded in Tinkham).
      That is not to say those things are not here: We reach
      elementary particles on p.222 and crystal fields p.
      183, that is chapter nine and ten of volume one.
    - (2) I confess, Hamermesh is difficult to surpass as
      a one-volume, introductory, physics-oriented exposition (I
      like Hamermesh very much). However, in the final analysis,
      I prefer a two-pronged (two-volume) approach. I confess, if
      you can muster the time and strength, Hamermesh plus
      Elliott and Dawber should be studied concurrently. Elliott
      and Dawber are a bit more spiral in methodology:
      abstraction is introduced slowly and successively.
    - (3) Perhaps my prime reason for preferring Elliott and
      Dawber lies in their Exercises for the student. Topping it
      off, solutions to the exercises are given at the
      end-of-the-book. Here is a problem: "a hypothetical
      molecule consists of four identical atoms at the corners of
      a plane-rectangle. Find the characters for the vibrational
      modes, and hence classify them by symmetry. Then, discuss
      the infrared and Raman spectra." (exercise #6.1 and 6.3,
      p.124).
    - (4) The initial chapter describes symmetry, the next three
      chapters are mathematical. Quantum Mechanics will be
      introduced (reviewed) in chapter five. Much of chapter five
      will be met later in the volume. If hungry for more, read
      Eugene Wigner (Group Theory, chapter four, five and
      six,1959). Regardless of intent, Wigner makes excellent
      study! Chapter ten, Isospin and SU(2), will build upon
      chapter seven (rotations in three-dimensions). Couched
      between seven and ten will be a chapter of angular momentum
      (applied to atomic structure). SU(3) is next (chapter
      eleven). Now, that material can be found in Georgi's Lie
      Algebra text (1982, beginning p.60 ). Elliott and Dawber
      is a nice entree to the more advanced Howard Georgi (a
      confession: the 1982 text of Georgi turned me off to the
      subject).
    - (5) What else recommends this book above other
      physics-oriented introductions ? The introductory,
      mathematical, chapter here echoes Eugene Wigner's
      (advanced) exposition. That which Wigner does in his
      chapter one (vectors and matrices) is accomplished here in
      chapter three (linear algebra, vector spaces; Hamermesh
      sections 3-1 to 3-3). Tinkham relegates the material to an
      appendix (review of vectors, vector spaces and matrices).
      Pick and choose.
    - (6) It is difficult to recommend only one textbook on
      applications of Group Theory (I have not seen Zee). But, as
      far as physics textbooks are concerned, I believe Elliott
      and Dawber is both elementary and understandable. What is
      not here can be found in Hamermesh (another truly
      elementary exposition, high on my list of recommendations).
      If you want to go further, Eugene Wigner is an excellent
      next-step (after volume one of Elliott and Dawber). Suffice
      it to say, Wigner is more advanced (but, do not eschew it).
      Wigner remains my favorite advanced exposition.
    - (7) How do I prepare ? Mathematically speaking, study
      Birkhoff and Mac Lane, A Brief Survey of Modern Algebra
      (chapters six through nine). Read chapter twenty: groups
      and other algebraic systems, volume three of Mathematics,
      Its Content, Methods and Meaning. Mathematical
      prerequisites are now behind you!
    - Then, concentrate on the physics.



### Abstract Algebra and Solution by Radicals . (Maxfield)
    "structure in algebra" I quote from this remarkable book:
    "...the student of high-school-algebra leaves with the
    impression of algebra as a collection of a heterogeneous
    collection of tricks...we see from Galois theory that much of
    algebra can be reasoned from a set of definitions and
    postulates, and what seemed to be unrelated techniques in
    elementary algebra proceed from a few underlying principles."
    (p.159). There you have motivation for study of this slim
    text. (1) You will acquire experience with methods of proof:
    Direct(p.71), Induction (p.22), contradiction (p.
    30), construction (p.190). Of course, these methods are
    utilized throughout the discourse. (2) You will acquire
    experience with the definition-theorem-proof format.
    Sequence: Groups, Rings, Fields, Mappings. (3) You learn
    terminology: equivalence relation (p.28, very well done
    and emphasized throughout--say, p.63), isomorphism (p.
    34) and homomorphism (p.79). (4) Groups: congruence,
    cosets, and especially quotient groups (pages 70-77).
    A two-pronged approach: elementary then more advanced (that
    is, chapter one and chapter three). Chapter three tells us
    about "a way to take a group apart and look at its inner
    structure." (p.47). Chapter two (abstract algebras)
    reinforced chapter one. (5) Chapter Four: Mappings that
    preserve relations. The diagrams, including the proof,
    delightful (p.82)! Learn: "the symmetries of an
    icosahedron provide a physical realization for a group of
    permutations of five roots of a quintic (fifth-degree)
    equation." (p.87). Primes, that comes next. That is,
    groups of prime order. Chapter four is beautiful. (6)
    One-hundred pages behind us (groups, abstract algebras,then
    more groups), we arrive to Polynomials. This will be the part
    of the book where heterogeneity (arithmetic, graphing,
    finding roots) is not only reviewed (from elementary algebra,
    say, long division--p.110) but also shown to be connected.
    Read: "we emphasize that the irreducibility of a polynomial
    depends on the Field." (p.117). (7) Of course, we finally
    reach Galois Theory. We learn of "linking the study of groups
    to the permutations of the roots of an equation." (p.144).
    Concluding, the authors write: "It provides an answer, at
    last, to the question that nags us in elementary algebra,
    "Why ?" (8) The appendixes are a potpourri of material,
    intended for a variety of goals. For example, appendix
    D simply reviews complex numbers in the so-called Argand
    plane. You will find a discussion of Lattices (appendix E)
    and you learn of "impossible constructions" (for instance,
    a twenty-degree-angle, p.197, reviewing bits of
    trigonometry). (9) Concluding my review: We have here a slim
    book (160 pages without the appendices) whose theme is one of
    unification and codification. What you have learned in
    high-school algebra is reviewed, and, more importantly,
    justified. You acquire experience with methods-of-proof,
    terminology, and increasing mathematical maturity.
    - Usually, these types of things are relegated to an
      introductory 'analysis' course. This brief book allows for
      that experience much earlier in the curriculum. The
      exercises are excellent. Selected answers and hints are
      there, too.
    - This slim text reminds me of another slim text: First
      Concepts of Topology, by Chin and Steenrod-- they, too,
      offer an efficacious exposition of an advanced mathematical
      topic which can be studied much earlier in the curriculum.
    - A fine addition to the literature of mathematics.


### Elementary Differential Geometry Paperback Barrett O'Neill
    - unsurpassed: elementary, modern, pedagogic
    - I have long desired to write a review of this textbook.
      I began to write a review three separate times, I never
      finished. Each time I felt that my review had fallen short.
      This attempt will hardly be different: nothing I can write
      will be able to convey how incredibly satisfying this
      textbook is for a beginning student! However, in order to
      set things in motion, I extract this line from the review
      of the website of Mathematical Association of America:
    - "For instructors looking for an undergraduate textbook, the
      most problematic aspect of this book will be O'Neill's
      decision to use the language of tangent vectors,
      differential forms, and covariant derivatives..." (With
      that, I disagree). Now, that line from that (mostly
      positive) MAA review pertains to the second edition. If
      a student for whom this text is addressed (preface:
      "students who have completed standard first-courses in
      calculus and linear algebra" ) is unable to fruitfully
      study the contents of this introductory exposition, then
      time to review prerequisites. If an instructor is unable to
      successfully "teach" a course utilizing this textbook, I am
      at a loss, as the text is a hallmark of erudition!
    - Let us take a closer look into this beautiful textbook (as
      it is not a treatise nor a monograph):
    - (1) A Spiral Approach: the student revisits earlier
      material later-on in the book. What you learn in earlier
      chapters is placed into service again, at increasing levels
      of abstraction. If you do not care for that methodology,
      seek another source. Read: "No matter what form
      differentiation may take, it will always have suitable
      linear and Leibnizian properties." Also, "We use curves at
      every stage, to gain an understanding of mappings." (p.
      33) and this,
    - "A mapping that has an inverse is called a diffeomorphism."
      (p.38). Thus, the initial chapter leads up to the
    - inverse-function theorem (p.39, stated without proof).
      Exercises #10 and #11 (taken as a set) will first ask you
    - to supply a proof, then will ask for a computation (one to
      one, onto, inverse). A summary concludes chapter one.
    - (2) Frame Fields, second chapter. Learn why "the geometry
      of Euclidean space can be derived from the dot-product."
      (p.42). Parametrization, orientation, these are
      emphasized. Covariant derivative introduced. (p.77).
    - Regards frame fields: "in studying curves and surfaces in
      Euclidean-three-space, we shall be able to choose
      a frame-field specifically adapted to the problem at hand."
      (p.81). Next, connection one-forms and structure
      equations.
    - (3) Chapter three, revisit Euclidean geometry via
      isometries. Revisit vector fields (encountered second
      chapter).
    - (4) Chapter four: Surfaces. You revisit parametrization "in
      first importance in practical computations." (p.135).
    - Jacobian matrix, this will be utilized time and time again.
      Linear Independence, much utilized (pages 45, 126, 136).
    - Read: "we now begin the exposition of calculus on a surface
      in Euclidean-three-space. We shall follow the order of
      topics in chapter one." (p.143). There you have it. Now,
      review chapter one, because chapter four is a continuation,
      of sorts. Chapter one introduced one-forms, chapter two
      used them for the structure equations. Now, in an
      additional three sections, we will learn of "forms on
      a surface." (pages 152-176). A dose of topology (p.176)
      precedes a dose concerning manifolds (p.182). A summary
      concludes the chapter. How does one improve upon this
      exposition ?
    - (5) Chapter two showed "shapes" of curves. Chapter five
      revisits "shapes" (shape-operator), here for surfaces.
    - A spiral approach! Read: "For a shape operator, its
      characteristic values and vectors, its trace and
      determinant,
    - all turn out to have geometric meaning of first importance
      for the surface." (p.193). Gaussian curvature and
      determinant (p.203). The chapter concludes with a nice
      section of surfaces of revolution (pages 234-242).
    - (6) Continue from "shape" to intrinsic geometry: "How much
      of the geometry is independent of its shape ? "
    - That is the question which we will answer in chapter six.
      Cartan's frames (chapter two) will be called upon for the
      answer. Isometries (met in the third chapter) will be
      revisited: "use its infinitesimal form as definition."
      (p.264).
    - Recall one to one, onto, inverse function theorem (we
      utilized those in chapter four). I highlight an excellent
      discussion of integration and orientation (you met that
      once in chapter four). Surfaces of revolution, you get it
      again (p.279)!
    - (7) Next up, Riemannian geometry. We free ourselves from
      the geometry of Euclidean-three-space. We generalize the
      "dot-product." From there, of abstract surfaces. Reading:
      "each geometric surface has its own covariant derivative."
      (p.318), and so we generalize that notion (first met in
      chapter two). Geodesics, we generalize straight lines.
    - Recall, the triangle-inequality (p.347): "intrinsic
      distances satisfy the triangle-inequality." Beautiful
      material!
    - Learn another proof that a sphere of radius 'r' has
      Gaussian curvature K=1/(r-squared). Here, bottom of p.
      360.
    - Conclusion of chapter seven is an introduction to
      Gauss-Bonnet theorem: "that the influence of Gaussian
      curvature penetrates to the ultimate topological
      conformation of the surface." (ages 372-385). A summary
      concludes the chapter, as it does every chapter (and, every
      chapter begins with an introductory survey of things to
      come).
    - (8) I will conclude my review. I have not included
      everything I wanted to include, there is too much of
      efficacy.
    - The textbook is well-written, introductory and fairly
      modern. Answers to the odd-numbered exercises are included.
    - An answer booklet exists for the even-numbered exercises
      (which I have). Hints are provided for many others.
    - The exercises are so well-written and so
      clearly-enunciated, that with a study of the text there
      seems but few reasons that each and every exercise can not
      be completed with the utmost confidence in your result.
    - By the way: No tensors, no Christoffel symbols (for that,
      examine the introductory text of Kreyszig).
    - By the way, this is a mathematics textbook, so very little
      physics is given any mention.
    - By remaining focused upon mathematics, Barrett O'Neill has
      offered a beautiful exposition.
    - A first-rate exposition which every mathematics and physics
      student (third-year) should study!





### Advanced Calculus: A Differential Forms Approach (Edwards)
    - I started this when I was a note taker in 2015
    - unique and innovative: a pedagogic masterpiece
    - As I have stressed time and time again, the field of
      mathematics is populated by numerous gems of exposition.
    - Pick a topic, any topic, and you are likely to encounter
      one of those expository gems. This book is one of them!
    - As I have stressed time and time again, a textbook which
      does not fall neatly into homogenization for a course is
      unlikely to find widespread adoption; such a text suffers
      in popularity as a result. This book is one of them!
    - Thus, before commencing, I implore: Secure a copy of this
      book. Read every word in this book. Attempt, or solve,
    - each exercise. Return to it as often as time permits. No
      matter that it is not a standard text for coursework.
    - It is better than just that! The nearest in approach to
      this text is Creighton Buck's Advanced Calculus (1956).
    - Both textbooks are elementary. (Buck begins with topology,
      not so with Edwards. Buck does not have Lebesgue integral,
      Edwards introduces same). Happily, both textbooks (Buck and
      Edwards) are complementary. Let us read:
    - (1) Read: "One of the main objectives of this book has been
      to point out once again the superiority of the Leibniz
      notation," and "...mathematics is active, deductive, and
      computational, and that the important thing in mathematics
    - is to define the logical and computational relationship
      between the terms employed." (see p.459 and p.462).
    - (2) Chapter two, about Integration (beginning p.22,
      because we move away from constant-forms to non-constant
      forms). Look at one problem: "In the text the condition
      'the integral diverges' is defined to mean the negation of
      the statement 'the integral converges.' State precisely
      what this condition says about the approximating sums, in
      other words reformulate the denial of the Cauchy criterion
      as a positive statement." (problem #8, p.37). Now, if
      that type of exercise is not your 'cup-of-tea' you may not
      care for the book. I find such an exercise inspiring!
    - (3) First chapter was brief (algebraic, geometric, constant
      forms), Second chapter introduced non-constant forms and
      thus, integration. Third chapter will put those two pieces
      together (differentiation and integration). That is, we
      arrive at Stoke's theorem (summary of all, pages 72-74).
      The first three chapters: algebra, derivative, integral,
      amalgamate!
    - (4) A survey of linear algebra arrives on scene (chapter
      four, fifty pages). You begin at the beginning
      (algebra--solving linear equations for unknowns--review of
      high-school material) which leads to (again) constant
      k-forms. So, what you were introduced to in chapter one
      (constant forms) is approached via linear algebra and
      axiomatized (pages 86-93). Nice! The chapter concludes
      with section of Abstract Vector Spaces (beginning p.113,
      concluding p.124). Nice!
    - (5) Now, a resummation of chapter two arrives chapter five:
      Derivatives at a higher level of abstraction. That is, we
      are going to "k-forms in n-variables." (p.142). Chain
      rule, Implicit function theorem, pull-backs: again and
      again.
    - The exercises are quite instructive: directional derivative
      (#7, p.149), tangent planes (#8), spherical coordinates
      are introduced (#9, parts a through d). More involved
      proofs are relegated to a separate sections (As #5.3, pages
      151-159).
    - (6) We saw a recapitulation of derivatives, it is time to
      accomplish same for integration, that in chapter six.
    - Thus, k-dimensional volume (p.196). And recapitulation:
      parametrization, orientation, again, Stoke's theorem.
    - Chapter concludes with a summary and generalization of all
      that came before, general properties (p.219).
    - Read: "More generally, a k-form is what physicists call
      a 'field.' The meaning of the 'field' is that it is an
      integrand whose integrals are physically meaningful
      quantities. " (p.225). Let us all all repeat that line
     ! Beautiful!
    - (7) We have completed approximately half of the book
      (excluding appendices and solutions to the problems).
    - Approximations and introductory differential equations have
      arrived (chapter seven) as applications. This chapter can
      be read at any time one chooses. As Edwards writes:
      "chapter seven is almost entirely independent of the
      others."
    - (8) We continue with applications. We revert to vector
      notation. Read: "much of the literature of mathematical
      physics is, regrettably, written in this vector notation of
      grad, curl and div, so students of physics must become
      acquainted with it...this notation only applies to
      three-dimensions." (p.268). Repeat that line, again and
      again! Many of the application found here (harmonic
      functions, electrodynamics) are in Flanders. The difference
      is that the material is presented here in a more sprawling,
      introductory, vantage (compare, for instance, heat
      equation). Worth recalling: "real numbers have an order
      relation, complex numbers do not." (p.291).
    - (9) Finally, chapter nine: further study of Limits. Read:
      "this requires a re-examination of real numbers." (p.
      357).
    - Almost one-hundred pages encompass this re-examination.
      Sequences, series, uniform continuity, interchange of
      limits,these are a few of the topics introduced. The
      exercises introduce much more! Examples:
    - "The volume of an n-dimensional ball is...." and " the
      Cesaro sum of a series is defined to be..."
    - Chapter nine is an excellent prelude to more real analysis.
      This is a fantastic chapter!
    - (10) Concluding: A rather unique exposition. Excellent
      exercises (answers/full solutions: pages 468-503).
    - Also, of efficacy for self-study (alongside "Second Year
      Calculus", by Bressoud).
    - A masterpiece of mathematical literature.
    - Highly recommended!



### Spacetime Physics (Taylor, Wheeler)
    - Idiosyncratic, pedagogic, distinctive. How to begin?
    - First, note that the textbook: Quantum Field Theory and the
      Standard Model (Schwartz, pages 10-27, 2014, ),
    - it summarizes in span of seventeen pages the entire
      discourse of Edwin Taylor and John Archibald Wheeler.
    - Second, note the textbook: A Traveler's Guide to Spacetime
      (Moore,1995), it duplicates much, albeit compactly.
    - For those who believe that Taylor and Wheeler is below
      their standards, by all means peruse Schwartz.
    - For those who believe that Taylor and Wheeler is too
      verbose, by all means read Moore.
    - For those who desire a truly introductory and pedagogic
      account, continue on with study of the _1st edition_ by
      Taylor and Wheeler (better yet, the 1971 red-paperback
      edition).
    - Read: 2017 Nobel Prize Winner Kip Thorne: "In our opinion,
      the best elementary introduction to special relativity is
      the first edition of Taylor and Wheeler." (p.88, Modern
      Classical Physics, 2017, Princeton University Press).
    - Read: James Hartle: "A lively and insightful presentation
      of special relativity at an entry-level, stressing the
      spacetime point of view." (p.563, Gravity, An Introduction
      to Einstein's GR, 2003).
    - Read: Magic Without Magic, "The exercises of Spacetime
      Physics were designed to be as comprehensive as possible,
      and to present the properties of spacetime over and over
      again, in many contexts: paradoxes, puzzles, technological
      applications, estimates, precise calculations, and
      philosophical difficulties. The qualifying examinations for
      the Princeton doctorate were culled through, along with the
      journal literature." (p.478, Freeman).
    - Look at content (utilizing the 1971, red paperback
      edition--as it contains solutions to the problems):
    - (1) 
        - Analogy, that is the pedagogic bridge utilized
          throughout the entire discourse. 
        - For example: "proper time is to a worldline in Lorentz
          geometry what length is to a path in Euclidean
          geometry." (p.33). Also: "the expression for the
          squared-magnitude of the momentum/energy four-vector is
          the exact analog of the formula that gives the squared
          magnitude of the spacetime interval between two nearby
          events..." (p.118).
    - (2) 
        - You learn primary concepts: Interval (and its
          invariance, p.three), covariance (focus on coordinates,
          p.44),
        - momentum (defined so as to be conserved), mass
          (velocity-independent quantity, p.109), four-vector
          (p.111).
    - (3) 
        - You eliminate ambiguous concepts: "It is right to say
          that time and space are inseparable parts of a larger
          unity.
        - It is wrong to say that time is identical in quality
          with space." (p.37).
    - (4) 
        - You learn something new: "Every application of the law
          of conservation of momentum and energy is a statement
          about a polygon built of four-vectors in spacetime."
          (p.131). Repeat that line time and time again! Or this:
          "In reality, increase of energy with velocity
          originates in geometric properties of spacetime
          itself." (p.137).
    - (5) 
        - You learn to utilize analogy, symmetry, linearity,
          hyperbolic functions, binomial expansion, in service of
          physics.
    - (6) 
        - Recurring issues: Rigidity (problem 67, p.151),
          center-of-mass (problem 59, p.143), and synchronizing
          clocks (problem 29, "it is inappropriate for any simple
          description of what goes on in spacetime." p.73 or,
          problem 98).
    - (7) 
        - By now, experiment has caught up with theory (p. 121):
          neutrinos, as we now know, do not travel at lightspeed,
          and gravitational radiation does, indeed, travel at
          lightspeed: Gravitational Waves and Gamma-Rays from
          a Binary Neutron Star Merger (2017 Astrophysical
          Journal, LIGO results).
    - (8) 
        - I say this: read it very carefully and more than once.
          Attempt, or complete, every exercise (solutions are
          available: 1971 red-paperback). 
    - I will conclude my review. The book originated from
      a course designed for Freshmen at Princeton University. (it
      was supplemented with Halliday and Resnick). 
    - The style is not for all (your professor has probably never
      studied it--certainly, my professors never did so).
    - Study the classic FIRST EDITION. Taylor and Wheeler
      provides for an invaluable learning resource.



### Introduction to Special Relativity (Rindler)
    - I here review the second edition (1991, though my copy is
      1993 printing of 169 pages). Rindler writes:
    - "Care must be taken not to regard spacetime as
      a straightforward generalization of ordinary Euclidean
      three-space to four dimensions, with time as just one more
      dimension, the time coordinate is not on the same footing
      as the three space coordinates." (p.51). That quote, in
      and of itself, needs to be repeated time and time again. It
      being but one of the positive attributes of Rindler's
      exposition. Of course, there is much more:
    - (1) Rindler is a nice follow-up to Edwin Taylor and John
      Wheeler (red-cover, paperback,1966 first edition). Rindler
      is comparable in scope and level to: Resnick (1968, Wiley),
      Dixon (1978, Oxford) or Barton (1999, Wiley). For contrast,
      Resnick includes more historical material, Dixon is
      a little more mathematical, Barton is a mixture of the
      three texts (that is, a mixture of Rindler, Resnick,
      Dixon).
    - (2) With a plethora of textbooks to choose from, how to
      make a choice ? I have studied from all of those textbooks
      (and, quite a few more besides). At this level and for its
      intended audience of "upper undergraduate level onwards,"
      Rindler remains the number one reference. Not a wasted
      word. Neither too simplistic nor too mathematically
      advanced. Precision and economy of exposition are the
      hallmarks. The speed-of-light, "c", is retained throughout
      the exposition (that is, "c" is not set equal to one).
      Lower-case font for three vectors, upper-case capital
      letters for four-vectors. That is a very useful pedagogic
      strategy!
    - (3) Isotropy, homogeneity, symmetry, linearity: Those
      concepts will be utilized time and time again throughout.
    - Coordinatization, events, locality, causality: Those
      concepts will be utilized time and time again throughout.
    - Analogy, so-called chain-rule, conservation laws: Those
      precepts will be utilized time and time again throughout.
    - (4) Read: "the accelerated observer principle...which
      asserts that an accelerating observer, using ideal clocks
      and measuring rods, makes the same local time and distance
      measurements as an inertial observer momentarily comoving
      with him." (p.41). That is another statement that is
      neglected in introductory courses! As Misner, Thorne and
      Wheeler have written : "accelerated motion and accelerated
      observers can be analyzed using Special Relativity."
    - (1973, Gravitation, p.163).
    - (5) The initial seventy-pages of Rindler are a focus upon
      space-time structure and kinematics. There is a thoughtful
      discussion of "mass," where we read: "particle physicists
      tend to make due without the concept of relativistic mass."
      (p.74). Learn of the zero-component Lemma: "the
      universal vanishing of one component of a four-vector
      implies the vanishing of all the others." (p.76). You
      met that earlier, Problem #6, of chapter four (p.66).
      Learn of the "linkage between the centroid theorem with
      three-angular-momentum conservation to the relativistic
      four-angular-momentum." (p.88). This is a beautiful
      exposition, utilizing analogy (pages 87-90, angular
      momentum).
    - (6) Learn that SI-units "blur the distinction between the
      electric and magnetic field." Read more about that (MKS) in
      Schwartz: Electrodynamics (1972): "is completely
      antithetical to the entire notion of relativistic
      invariance."
    - Rindler, nevertheless, retains SI/MKS-units, but apologizes
      for that just the same!
    - There are a few interesting identities which permeate the
      discussion: trigonometric (see #18.3, p.43) and
      algebraic (equation #41.4, p.116). Hints allow the
      exercises to be attacked with fervor, for example:
      "remember the sine-rule for triangles" (#4, p.46) or
      "appeal to three-tensor invariance under rotation." (#6,
      p.148). Those helpful hints are copious! Excellent!
    - (7) Concluding: The exercises are a rich source of
      additional material (hints and solutions provided). The
      mathematics is not too advanced (you do get a ten-page
      survey of tensors in an appendix). Wolfgang Rindler is
      terse (169 pages in total), but not too much so! Precision
      of thought is exemplary. This is a first-rate exposition.
      Rindler references the 1978 monograph of Dixon (footnote,
      p.89, and Preface). Dixon is also a first-rate
      exposition.
    - There is no shortage of books (poor or good or great) on
      Special Relativity.
    - Rindler should be required reading (students and
      professors). It is great.


### Understanding Quantum Mechanics (Omnès)
    - update and simplification of Omnes' previous monograph
    - I enjoyed Omes' previous publication, The Interpretation of
      Quantum Mechanics (Princeton University Press).
    - If you have read the earlier book, you do not need this
      one. The earlier book provides for more detail.
    - Additionally, the earlier publication is more satisfying.
      Allow me focus on this later "understanding" :
    - (1) We note the unfortunate typo on p.five: units, of
      Planck's constant (it is "energy times time," not the
      "energy divided by time," as the book reads
      "Joules/second"). Omnes writes: "there is nothing better
      than the papers by the pioneers." Therefore, if you have
      a copy of the Wheeler & Zurek compendium (1983), keep it
      handy for reference.
    - (2) Historical insight pervades initial four chapters
      (thirty pages): "Harmonic oscillators were central..."
      (p.10).
    - Read: "The Bohr-Sommerfeld theory was a bridge over
      a chasm..." (p.21) and "in quantum mechanics simplicity
    - is often more or less an illusion." (p.22). Third
      chapter is quite useful, a survey of Heisenberg's matrix
      mechanics.
    - (3) About Max Born and probability: "Born's introduction of
      probabilities in quantum mechanics reveals an essential
      feature...that had been practically ignored until then."
      (p.35). My opinion: Max Born remains underappreciated.
    - (4) Here is the author's statement: "The aim of the
      interpretation of quantum mechanics...must involve, in one
      way or another, a radically new manner of thinking and
      understanding." (p.49). You will read of the so-called
      reduction of the wave-function (pages 52-54). The best is
      yet to come, chapter six with John Von Neumann. I highlight
      chapter six!
    - (5) Omnes writes: "The wave-function is not an objective
      physical quantity, but a compendium of information" and
      "perhaps the fuzziest idea of quantum mechanics is the
      concept of 'object' ." (p.71). Read Isham for more
      (1995).
    - (6) John von Neumann reappears time and time again. Ninth
      chapter, von Neumann and the Projection Postulate.
    - Nice! I do find ambiguity in this comment: "a moment
      later, its wave-function is divided into two parts." (p.
      104). That is, I question the belief that the
      "wave-function is divided into two parts."
    - (7) You will meet Wigner and Weyl (distributions, p.
      112). A bit more of them in Esposito, Marmo, Sudarshan
      (2004). There you will see where "it shows the sense in
      which quantum mechanics is non-local." (p.544).
    - (8) A central theorem, due to Egorov, will be referred to
      often (pages 125-131). This will be new to many a reader,
    - even if they have had a first course in quantum mechanics.
      This chapter, eleven, is simply too brief for my tastes.
    - (9) Next, Histories. We read: "they are as intuitive as
      quantum physics can tolerate." (p.140). Read: "but sense
      versus non-sense is a matter of logic." then progressing to
      the excellent thirteenth chapter: "the state of a system."
      Max Born is given advertisement again (p.145) and we
      learn of "state preparation." John von Neumann is also
      reintroduced (p.150). Gleason is introduced ("the most
      abstract one"). I defer to Isham's text: "Gleason's Theorem
      places strong constraints on any attempts to modify the
      standard quantum formalism." (1995, p.211).
    - (10) Histories keep you occupied for another three
      chapters. Omnes writes: "reality can certainly not be
      defined."
    - Decoherence keeps you occupied for another three chapters.
      Learn of the close relation between dissipation and
      decoherence ("macroscopic interferences"). Read: "by the
      word 'real' I mean that the measurement devices are
      macroscopic and that they show decoherence." (p.221).
      Notes and comments concludes the publication.
    - (11) Concluding: This is a thoughtful and well written
      exposition. It is within grasp of its intended audience, an
      audience of beginning students. The book will not
      replace--nor was it intended to replace--a standard course.
    - It should be followed-up with a few others (for instance,
      Omnes' earlier monograph or Isham's lecture-text).
    - Well worth the investment in time and effort. Not the final
      word, yet, an encouraging word.



### Differential topology with a view to applications (Chillingworth)
    - Chillingworth: "should be read more in the spirit of
      a novel, than as a textbook"
    - My first exposure to the topic, differential topology, came
      from perusal of chapter nine of Misner, Thorne & Wheeler,
      their textbook, Gravitation (first printing, 1973). Their
      chapter nine is brief, so I have always desired to learn
      more. This 1976 publication is an excellent supplement to
      Misner, Thorne & Wheeler. There are other monographs to
      peruse: I own copies of Guillemin and Pollack (it being
      intermediate to advanced), Wallace's First Steps (it being
      introductory), Milnor's lectures (advanced) and Penrose's
      Techniques of Differential Topology in Relativity
      (fascinating, yet advanced). But, I return to
      Chillingworth. Situated within the context of the
      above-mentioned books, this one requires the fewest
      prerequisites ( "familiarity with derivatives and partial
      derivatives in elementary calculus," see Preface). However,
      it does not encompass everything (for instance, integration
      on manifolds; see Guillemin & Pollack's chapter four).
    - But, what Chillingworth does cover, is covered well. I will
      highlight a few attributes:
    - (1) A leisurely introductory chapter of Topology (without
      the 'differential'):
    - From functions to metrics, then continuity. The triangle
      inequality is much utilized (p.13, and more often).
    - Learn terminology (homeomorphism, p.25). Proofs, these
      are often presented through multiple methods
    - (for instance, p.27). Analogy and generalization, these
      are consistently utilized for pedagogic purposes
    - (for instance, p.29 & 78). An excellent first chapter.
      This first chapter will be excellent preparation for
      Guillemin & Pollack's initial twelve pages (also, those
      first twelve exercises in G&P will be well within grasp--
      after this first chapter of Chillingworth). Equivalence
      Classes: time and again (see p.257).
    - (2) If you made it through the first chapter, I surmise
      that the rest of the book will flow smoothly.
    - Then, you are ready to begin the 'differential' part. So,
      more terminology. And, this is where this text begins to
      get interesting: Normed linear spaces, inner products and
      Hilbert spaces. Foremost here: Linearity (p.64) and
      chain rule. More terminology: diffeomorphism (p.69),
      germs and jets (p.80 and throughout, being an
      exceptionally lucid exposition). An elementary calculus
      topic is approached via this new concept, germs (p.82).
    - Read: "the inverse-function-theorem (IFT) is in some sense
      the key theorem of calculus" (p.95). Germs, again,
      utilized to uncover the IFT. One notes that the initial
      100-pages constrained itself to finite dimensions.
      Infinite-dimensional generalizations are remarked upon. You
      get Morse (critical points): notice that Chillingworth has
      Morse on p.104, G&P on p.42. (That is a clue:
      Chillingworth is much more leisurely than Guillemin and
      Pollack).
    - (3) Finally, we reach Manifolds. There are many examples
      (as is found in most of the other texts, also). You will
      see the chain rule again and again (pages:123,166,174,205).
      Read: " the point here is that the 'differential' in
      'differential manifold,' refers to a relative property of
      overlap maps." (p.126). Pendulums utilized for
      applications (pages 30 and 200). Projective Plane, always
      a learning experience, occurs p.141. And, if you need to
      learn of Bundles (vector bundles) you will meet them time
      and again herein (pages: 154, 162, 171, 245). Meet
      transversality (this occurs p.176, Guillemin and Pollack
      occurs on p.27).
    - (4) Final chapter, qualitative theory of dynamical systems.
      Read this chapter in conjunction with Hirsch and Smale
      (their excellent 1974 text, Differential Equations,
      Dynamical Systems and Linear Algebra) and as prerequisite
      to that other classic, Arnold's 1973 Ordinary Differential
      Equations.
    - (5) In conclusion: The only drawback here is the absence of
      "homework problems." On the other hand, if you do follow
      the book in linear fashion, taking pains to verify the
      examples as they arise, I believe that any problem
      presented in Guillemin and Pollack will be quite painless
      to complete. The typography is a bit distracting
      (typescript) and a few typos do crop up (so, pay
      attention). Yet, if studied as a more extensive first step
      (than Wallace) and more of an introduction (than Guillemin
      and Pollack) and, as a prelude to advanced works (Milnor)
      this one is hard to beat.
    - Recommended with reservations.



### Mathematics Form and Function (MacLane)
    - MacLane: "a fresh view of the variety of mathematics"
    - As has already been duly noted, this survey-book should be
      required reading for students majoring in mathematics.
    - I will take that one step further: this survey-book should
      also be required reading for students of physics. Happily,
      the book is lucidly written and neither subset of students
      (mathematics or physics) should face any difficult in
      perusing the exposition. Its efficacy for mathematics
      students accepted, I concentrate upon my reasons for
      suggesting this unique exposition for the later category of
      students. That is, physics students.
    - (1) Begin with the fascinating ninth chapter, Mechanics;
      allow me to quote Saunders Maclane: "Understanding is not
      easy...it has taken me over fifty years to understand the
      derivation of Hamilton's equations." Learn of "the
      difficulty of getting to the bottom of it all." References
      for this chapter include: Osgood's 1937 Mechanics textbook,
      available from web-archive, Pars' Treatise on Analytical
      Dynamics and Maclane's self-penned journal article
      "Hamiltonian Mechanics and Geometry." (American
      Mathematical Monthly,1970). As an adjunct to Maclane: the
      exposition presented in Burke's Applied Differential
      Geometry (1985) is quite enlightening.
    - (2) What do we learn of Cauchy's Theorem ? Read: "that an
      integral of a holomorphic function is independent of path
      is a direct extension of the fundamental theorem of
      calculus, and is another representation of the idea that
      the whole is the sum of the parts."(p.322). Meet Riemann
      surfaces, later germs and sheaves. Beautiful!
    - (3) Much of the exposition focuses upon the concept of
      "function" (p.126). In one manner, or another, whether
      it be sets, categories, functors, the concept of function
      prominent throughout. Read: "the clarity which is achieved
      when all the relevant concepts are defined firmly and
      formally." (p.413). Again, suggested reading for physics
      students!
    - (4) A topic fast-approaching a favorite of mine is
      Projective Geometry. There is a discussion of that topic
      here (pages 234 and 240). For more enlightenment read
      Hilton & Griffith's A Comprehensive Textbook of Classical
      Mathematics (1970, chapter 17) where they write: "A
      beautiful example of the growth of a theory, for theory of
      conics." Another elementary exposition on the topic:
      Greenberg's Euclidean and Non-Euclidean Geometries (1993,
      third edition).
    - (5) Tensors: learn why tensor algebra is a "graded
      algebra," in Maclane's chapter (seven) of Linear Algebra.
    - Differential Forms, read: "they need not be mysterious"
      (p.211). Learn more of the functorial viewpoint.
    - A highlight: the section entitled "Collapse By Quotients,"
      a process for constructing linear maps. Beautiful!
    - (6) Finally, I mention, as all others have done before me,
      the interesting and lucid exposition of categories and
      functors. The discussion serves as prelude to yet another
      text: Dodson's Categories, Bundles and Spacetime Topology
      (1980). Dodson writes: "the right way to view it all is
      through the theory of categories, and increasingly the
      language of this theory is appearing in the literature of
      Physics." (Robert Geroch, Mathematical Physics, is yet
      another source).
    - (7) Hopefully I have convinced, through these examples, why
      this book is as useful for physics students as it is for
      mathematics students. The book is beautifully written and
      dense with information of continuing efficacy.
    - Highly recommended!


### Differential Equations with Applications and Historical Notes (Simmons)
    - excellent for self-study
    - There are three introductory differential equations
      textbooks which I find of lasting utility:
    - (1) Hirsch and Smale, Differential Equations, Dynamical
      Systems and Linear Algebra (1974, Academic Press).
    - (2) Hubbard and West, Differential Equations, A Dynamical
      Systems Approach (1991, Springer).
    - (3) Simmons, Differential Equations with Applications and
      Historical Notes (1991, second edition).
    - As an adjunct, one can hardly ignore Dieudonne's
      Infinitesimal Calculus (1971, chapter eleven, Hermann).
    - Now, my first introductory course in differential equations
      occurred late 1996, where not one of the above mentioned
      texts was ever referenced. What a pity! Now, regarding
      Simmons:
    - (1) I begin with Chapter 13. Yes, I begin with chapter
      thirteen! Read: "One of the main recurring themes of this
      book has been the idea that only a few simple types of
      differential equations can be solved explicitly in terms of
      known elementary functions." That line should be the first
      statement met by a new student to the subject. It is
      interesting that Simmons situated existence and uniqueness
      (chapter 13) ahead of numerics (chapter fourteen), whereas
      Hubbard and West accomplish the same in reverse (numerics
      chapter three, existence and uniqueness chapter four).
      Simmons shows "logical dependence" of chapters (p.xxi),
      you will notice that thirteen and fourteen stand
      independent.
    - (2) If you enjoy practicing problem-solving skills, this
      book is helpful. Thirty pages of solutions to those
      problems are part and parcel of the enterprise (although,
      temptation to look at the solution ahead of time should be
      admonished). Hints are provided (for instance, p.309, #3
      d, "use the trigonometric expansion," or, p.479, #1:
      "write conditions...in the form...and use polar coordinates
      to deduce a contradiction."). Excellent pedagogy!
    - (3) Appendices offer further historical, bibliographic
      information, but accomplish much more than just that.
      A highlight: Appendix B, proof of Lienard's theorem (pages
      497-501). The proof here is as lucid as any other
      exposition (compare: Hartman, p.179, Ordinary
      Differential Equations). A nice detour, the discussion of
      Lagrange multipliers. Read: "This technique has two major
      features important in theoretical work, it does not disturb
      the symmetry of the problem and it removes the side
      condition." (p.516).
    - Meet Euler (see Appendix A, pages 136-145), which segues
      into a discussion of continued fractions and graph theory.
    - (4) You get practice with the computational (the usual
      assortment of separation of variables, laplace transforms,
      etc.), you get physical applications (Newton's laws plus
      Hermite polynomials and quantum mechanics,then Lagrange and
      Hamilton), finally you get some abstraction--but, not too
      abstract (see pages 282-284, those famous inequalities of
      Schwarz and Minkowski). In any event the mix of historical,
      applied, and theoretical engagingly espoused throughout.
    - (5) A highlight: those special functions of mathematical
      physics, Legendre and Bessel, especially pages 348-380.
    - A highlight, the survey of Nonlinear Equations (chapter
      11): "without the aid of sophisticated mathematical
      machinery, requiring little more than elementary
      differential equations and two-dimensional vector algebra."
      (p.441). Complementary to this discussion, please study
      Sokolnikoff and Redheffer (1966, Mathematics of Physics and
      Modern Engineering, chapter two, nonlinear differential
      equations).
    - (6) I can add little more to the extant reviews, as this is
      indeed a wonderful introductory textbook.
    - I still remember reading that "College Mathematics Journal"
      special issue, devoted to Differential Equations
    - (Volume 25, Number 5, November 1994). As I noted earlier,
      my first introductory course occurred 1996. Printed inside
      of this journal, names of board-of-editors for this issue.
      The name of the instructor for my 1996 course was included
      there. What a pity that the introductory course never
      referenced any one of my three favorite differential
      equations textbooks. Simmons, Hubbard & West, Hirsch
      & Smale-- are highly recommended for a first course.
    - Do not forget to read Hubbard: What it Means to Understand
      a Differential Equation, in that CMJ issue! 



### First concepts of Topology
    - pedagogical bridge and self-study guide
    - Chinn and Steenrod have written one of the finest examples
      of mathematical pedagogy I have ever encountered. They have
      presented, at a relatively elementary vantage point, an
      introduction to topology with minimum of prerequisites:
      perhaps 'Modern Introductory Analysis,' by Dolciani will
      provide sufficient preparation. In any event, I highlight
      the pedagogic strategy here employed:
    - (1) Problems for the student concludes each section (not at
      the conclusion of each chapter). Solutions are provided.
    - (2) There is a progressive build-up of abstraction (spiral
      approach). Dimensions gradually stepped up: from one, to
      two, then, more briefly, three.
    - (3) Recapitulation of certain concepts and methods is
      utilized throughout, I present a few instances: triangle
      inequality (pages 16, 21, 23, 101 & 119), similar triangles
      (pages 21 and 122 ), proof by contradiction (pages 39
      & 67), analogy (pages 81,108, & 120), fixed points (pages
      60 and 108) and generalization (pages 7 and 41). Excellent
      pedagogy!
    - (4) Introduction of mathematical concepts found to be
      efficacious for later studies, a few examples: contracting
      sequences (p.34), convexity (p.50), homotopy (p.54
      and 126 ), equivalence of the definition of mapping and of
      vector field (p.112).
    - (5) The text is structured upon two-pronged approach: An
      obvious demarcation from the line to the plane. Along the
      way, interludes: For instance, the efficacy of complex
      numbers when visiting the plane (pages 127-130). An
      elaborate discussion of open sets (pages 22-26). We read:
      "It is not easy to see in advance why the notion of open
      set should be an important concept." (p.22).
    - (6) As stated previously, there are problems
      (end-of-section) and alongside solutions (end-of-book). If
      you have studied and understood the concurrent section, the
      problems will be quite approachable. Problem #11 (p.37)
      will ask you to " prove the theorem of Dedekind about 'cut'
      of the real numbers." Problem #10 (p.52) will ask you to
      " show that the intersection of a contracting sequence of
      closed intervals is either a single point or a closed
      interval." Later in the book, you get this: " Show that
      a continuous vector field of non-zero vectors defined on
      the sphere must have at least one vector perpendicular to
      the sphere" (p.126). Read, too: "a vector is an ordered
      pair of points" (p.110).
    - (7) In conclusion: An excellent textbook of "first
      concepts." Not merely 'first' concepts of Topology, but
      first concepts of introductory analysis. Of course, as an
      adjunct in learning to devise proofs of theorems, also an
      excellent first step.
    - Highly recommended! 




### Waves (Crawford)
    - Stunning...Beautiful....Captivating...Timeless
    - Recently, having been preoccupied with quantum field
      theory, I decided upon reviewing a different topic. That
      topic is optics, in all its variety of manifestations.
      I re-read a number of books (Halliday & Resnick 1966,
      Bertolotti Masers and Lasers, Feynman's QED, and Harry
      Paul's Introduction To Quantum Optics). In other words,
      a potpourri of books at varying degrees of technicality.
      Now, you will not find much of Lasers in Crawford's book,
      so, in that respect you will need to turn elsewhere. But,
      for almost anything else your heart and mind might desire,
      this is a fantastic piece of scholarship.
    - (1) If you do not enjoy "reading"...then, this book is not
      for you. If you do not enjoy experiments...then, this book
      is not for you. If you do not enjoy solving problems,
      then.... this book is not for you. However, if you enjoy
      being able to pick any page--any arbitrary page--from
      a textbook, and upon doing so learning something new, this
      is the text for you!
    - (2) Recall Feynman's QED (1985, Strange Theory of Light and
      Matter, first chapter). If you take notice, Crawford has
      a "home experiment #5," demonstrating Feynman's "reflection
      from glass" (see Crawford, p.252). Where Crawford
      exposes the student to this effect by way of experiment,
      Feynman carries one further along in the abstraction. In
      that respect, Crawford is a bridge, a stepping-stone,
      a ladder, for further exploration.
    - (3) How about the Hanbury-Brown-Twiss experiment ? That is,
      " correlation between photons in coherent light rays." You
      will get a beautiful, if somewhat qualitative, description
      in the chapter of interference and diffraction. (p.468).
      Do not stop there, as Bertolotti has much more to say about
      it in his 1983 book: Masers and Lasers (pages 203-217).
    - (4) Oh, there is so much more! Tuning Forks--p.532.
      Here, in another "home experiment" we get a glimpse into
      the tuning fork and quadrupole radiation. A lovely
      exposition. How about torque ? Well, seldom will you
      encounter as beautiful an account as can be found on p.
      364: " Angular Momentum in Traveling Plane Wave."
    - (5) Two problems ( #9.58 and 9.59, p.538) are most
      instructive. The first-mentioned involves converting
      amplitude modulation to phase modulation in FM radio
      broadcasting. The following problem reverses that sequence.
      Lovely! Problem #7.22 walks you through a derivation:
      "angular momentum of circularly polarized plane traveling
      wave."
    - I might add, the 1968 (and, earlier) printing utilizes CGS
      units: "The MKS system tends to obscure either the
      fundamental electromagnetic symmetry of the vacuum, or the
      essential asymmetry of the sources." (p.xviii).
    - I might also implore the prospective student to read the
      Appendix (Taylor series, superposition, vector identities).
    - (6) You will get bits and pieces of Fourier analysis as the
      occasion arises. Thus, if a "spiral" approach is not to
      your tastes, then the exposition will not satisfy you.
      Also, if a phrase such as "let us solve it by bold
      guesswork" (p.74) is not to your tastes, then turn
      elsewhere (here, a discussion of normal modes and the
      solution of equation #65). Nice! That trend continues with
      problem #2.29 : "Prove each of the following numbered
      statements using two methods--the physical method that
      makes use of normal modes, and the method of Fourier
      analysis." (p.97). Beautiful!
    - (7) Concluding my review: This is a 600-p.text, so it
      will take time to peruse. The experiments will take time to
      perform. The problems (many with answers, many with hints)
      are of varying difficulty. It is not the final word on any
      topic, although it is an excellent first exposure to many
      topics. The mathematics is not particularly formidable:
    - If you understand the mathematical appendix, then, proceed
      with the text. This is a fantastic excursion, well worth
      the time and effort (the text is "linear" in the sense that
      you will get the most out of this book if you do not skip
      around--the "Teaching Notes" explain that material which be
      skipped, if one is hurried).
    - Unreservedly Recommended! 


### Galison Einsteins Clock
    - detail aplenty: a clash between concrete and abstract
    - I vacillate between a four and five star review. The
      scholarship itself is undeniably deserving of five. The
      title and the intended audience for whom it is addressed,
      that raises eyebrows. So beware: If detail aplenty is not
      to your liking, then stay far away. It is helpful for the
      prospective reader to be familiar with elementary physics.
      Also, Albert Einstein is not the sole, nor primary, focus
      of this exposition.
    - (1) Chapter one will encapsulate all which follows later in
      the book. A hurried reader could conclude study with the
      first chapter, learning more by further perusing the
      endnotes (twenty pages of endnotes concludes the book, they
      are rather fascinating). Also, the bibliography, a rich
      source of material (the bibliography occupies fifteen
      pages).
    - (2) Chapter two segues to Poincare, we read of a central
      theme: "intertwined abstraction and concreteness." (p.
      48).
    - This will be a tough chapter for the casual reader. These
      pages make reference to Poincare and his "new, qualitative,
      methods to explore classical celestial dynamics"; where the
      "abstractness" creeps in (beginning p.65).
    - Assiduous study of this chapter will be amply rewarded.
    - (3) The long chapter three has this to say: "Both
      astronomers and railroaders viewed the new technologies of
      transport and communication as disciplining time more
      effectively than any school." (p.125). Clocks and time,

    - (4) We stay with Poincare in chapter four, again with the

### QFT For the Gifted Amateur
    - pedagogically instructive bridge and self-study guide
    - I enjoyed this textbook quite a bit. If one were utilizing
      this text for self-study, be sure to visit the author's
      academic website where a list of errata can be found (most
      typos are minor and can be cleaned up with a bit of
      dimensional analysis--for instance, the -1/2 for lambda
      should be +1/2 on bottom of p.408). This points to an
      issue regarding background: Dimensional analysis can be
      incredibly helpful. For instance, you would not forget the
      `(2*pi)^4` in the denominator of equation #24 (p.6) if
      you were simply to keep your eye on dimensions. Onward:
    - (1) Mary Boas' chapters of Complex Analysis (Mathematical
      Methods In The Physical Sciences, chapter two and eleven)
      are absolutely prerequisite. Glance at appendix B of
      Lancaster and Blundell. It reviews that material. There are
      seven examples in appendix B. If those examples are not
      completely understandable, the material needs to be learned
      first. Notice that Anthony Zee's textbook, QFT In
      A Nutshell, will not review complex variables at all. So,
      already, it is clear that this textbook is pitched at
      a lower plane than Zee's insightful textbook.
    - (2) Complex variables, dimensional analysis,
      integration-by-parts, "resolution of the identity" these
      tools (and more) are your lifeblood. You might want to
      recognize the difference between Lagrangians and
      Hamiltonians. It is difficult to recommend here a mechanics
      text. I will say this: my course in junior-level mechanics
      was inadequate when it came to either Lagrangians or
      Hamiltonians. I hope instruction has since changed in that
      regard (I doubt it). In any event, it pays to recognize the
      difference between when derivatives are more useful a tool,
      as opposed to when Integrals are more useful (Begging the
      question: Why did it take ever so long until the Feynman
      path integral became part and parcel of the establishment
      ? Read Kaiser.).
    - (3) Let me survey the pedagogic attributes of this
      textbook: Margin notes to amplify textual material,
      summaries at chapter conclusion, diagrams and figures
      (cartoons) along the way, many excellent examples to
      ruminate upon, intermediate steps in the mathematical
      derivations provided, and last--but not least, excellent
      problems for student involvement and enrichment (Hints,
      too: for instance, problem #35.2, verify Gell-Mann-Low
      equations. Some are relatively easy: for instance, problem
      #30.2,"suggest a form for (4+1)-dimensional Chern-Simons
      term". It is difficult to overstate one thing:
    - (4) The Exercises! When I say an exercise is "relatively
      easy," I imply only this: If you study what Lancaster and
      Blundell have written, if you study their examples
      thoroughly, if you perform intermediate calculations on
      your own, then those end-of- chapter exercises are well
      within grasp! I am unfamiliar with another textbook which
      is quite as elementary as is this Quantum Field Theory
      textbook (and, I own almost the entire gamut of texts--
      from 1959 to 2017--I will say, the pedagogic approach of
      Anthony Zee " of letting you discover the Feynman diagrams
      for yourself " (Zee's p.44, 2010) is admirable, even
      brilliant; but his is for a different subset of learners.
      Another exercise herein: "We'll work through a famous proof
      of Goldstone's Theorem-the states linked to the ground
      state via the Noether current are massless Goldstone
      modes." (see p.246, #26.3 parts a through g). Compare
      this to Anthony Zee (p.228); although, I very much like
      the manner in which Peskin and Schroeder approach the
      Goldstone Theorem (p.351).
    - (5) Let us take a linguistic tour, that is let us read what
      Lancaster and Blundell have to say:
    - "commutation operators contain all the information about
      the states." (p.35) and "the formerly
      negative-energy-states are interpreted as positive-energy
      antiparticles with momenta in the opposite direction to the
      corresponding particle." (p.63), and, "to get around the
      infinity encountered at the end of the last section, we
      define the act of Normal Ordering." (p.105), "it may be
      helpful to think of the freedom of the choice of gauge as
      a choice of language." (p.129). Read: " a QFT which
      satisfies a fairly minimal set of assumptions--lorentz
      invariant, local, Hermitian and Normal Ordered--possesses
      the symmetry PCT." (p.139). Also, "propagators, with
      their 'from here to there definition', also have the
      appealing property that they can be drawn in a cartoon form
      showing a particle travelling from y to x. This isn't quite
      as trivial as it sounds." (p.150). Finally: "In this way
      of looking at the world, our theories of Nature are
      low-energy, effective field theories, which will eventually
      break down at high enough energies." (p.294). Each line
      quoted above is enhanced with plentiful detail within each
      chapter.
    - (6) Spin arrives late (chapter nine, p.321). Dirac
      equation arrives late. That strategy makes much sense. We
      read from Steven Weinberg: "Dirac's original motivation for
      this equation as a sort of relativistic Schrodinger
      equation does not stand up to inspection." (Quantum Theory
      Of Fields, Volume One, p.565). What Weinberg says is
      reinforced in more elementary terms here. Reiterating:
      Lancaster and Blundell pitch themselves at a more
      elementary vantage.
    - (7) This review could go on forever! For instance, the
      pedagogic approach to renormalization is multi-pronged,
      multi-chaptered. But, instead of doing that I will simply
      reiterate my view that this textbook is an excellent bridge
      for further excursion into Quantum Field Theory. It is
      difficult to be objective--Anthony Zee's QFT In A Nutshell
      is hard to beat, but it really is not truly an introduction
      (perhaps, though, if you are already brilliant). But, for
      those who aspire to get there (brilliance, that is)
      Lancaster and Blundell provide an opportunity to approach
      that goal. My personal favorites are Steven Weinberg for
      understanding (also, Anthony Duncan), Peskin and Schroeder
      for more computation.
    - However, if a truly elementary textbook is desired,
      Lancaster and Blundell almost hit the mark with perfection.
    - (You might want to utilize Shankar's Principles of Quantum
      Mechanics text for collaborative reading--for instance,
      regards Coherent States.)
    - Before study of the book: View appendix B (Complex
      Analysis) and view Example #1.2 (p.13). Do they make
      sense ?
    - If so, perhaps this text is what you are looking for. If
      not, learn that background first, then return to these
      pages.
    - This textbook is difficult to surpass, especially if
      searching for a truly elementary and pedagogic textbook. 


### Shankar Principles of QM
    - self-study guide for advanced coursework
    - One of my favorite textbooks. The first edition, 1980, was
      innovative for its time (path integrals). I purchased that
      first edition. The second edition, 1994, builds upon the
      first edition (adding another chapter of path integrals).
      I purchased the second edition and placed the first edition
      into the hands of another student, that student went on to
      receive a PhD in Physics (I hope the first edition was
      utilized). That which was innovative in 1980 was less so in
      1994 and is, now, commonplace (2018)! I believe that
      Shankar was instrumental in the progression: from
      innovative to commonplace! 
    - Regarding Shankar's textbook, I concentrate upon one goal
      as stated in the Preface: "addressed to students who are
      trying to learn the subject by themselves." Is that a goal
      achieved ? The answer is in the affirmative.
    - Let us pause to consider that one goal (self-study):
    - (1) The student will review elementary linear algebra,
      learn Dirac notation (bras/kets) and survey salient
      features of classical mechanics. That review will span one
      hundred pages (the chapter one, two and three). Pay
      attention to what transpires on p.61, it will recur on
      p.226. 
    - That highlights the first excellent pedagogic strategy
      herein: Many points in the exposition arise later under
      more general (abstract) circumstances. Especially, if
      viewed through the lens of the student exercises. Each
      exercise serves a purpose.
    - (2) Chapter 4 begins discussion of The Postulates of
      Quantum Mechanics. A table deftly contrasts the language of
      classical mechanics to the language of quantum mechanics
      (pages 116/116). Density Matrix (as digression) explored.
      We read: "it must be remembered that even though the
      wave-function can be visualized as a wave in our space, it
      is not a real wave which carries energy, momentum, etc."
      (p.150). Perhaps that line needs repeating to everyone!
    - (3) It is nice to read of the distinction between global
      and local conservation (p.165). 
    - The mathematical proof that "there is no degeneracy in
      one-dimensional bound states" is easy to follow (p.177).
      Harmonic Oscillator in energy basis is plenty enlightening
      (p.203). p.115 presented Postulate # 2, this will be
      generalized, p. 210. 
    - Yet another instance of revisiting an earlier concept (at
      elementary vantage) to its more abstract generalization
      later on in the exposition. Excellent pedagogy (spiral
      approach).
    - (4) Innovative in 1980 was chapter eight--an introduction
      to Feynman's Path Integrals. For the 1994 edition, the
      chapter was accentuated, with the addition of chapter
      twenty-one. Thus, beginning elementary and progressing to
      an advanced outlook. Treat these two chapters as a combined
      set. They are beautiful, lucid, expositions.
    - (5) Happily, you read "time `t` is not a dynamical
      variable, but a parameter" (p.245). Reiterate that phrase,
      too!
    - (6) Chapter Ten, N-degrees of freedom, is well-worth
      admiring for its clarity. You get "direct-product" more
      than once (p.248 and p.253). 
    - You get almost everything more than once: from one
      dimension to many, from one particle to many. The
      discussion of indistinguishable particles is quite
      instructive (pages 260-263). 
    - Proceeding from bosons to fermions. A discussion elucidates
      upon "when can we ignore symmetrization and
      antisymmetrization ? " (p.273).
    - (7) Symmetries get full elaboration (combined set: chapters
      eleven and twelve). Read: "if nature were not to follow the
      same rules over spacetime, there would be no rules to find,
      just a sequence of haphazard events with no rhyme or
      reason."(p.296). Nice to read: "complex-conjugation of
      wave-function performs a time reversal."(p.302). Attention
      paid to the exercise on p.317 (charged particle and vector
      potential) will pay dividends later (p.587). Attention paid
      to exercises #12.5.6 and 12.5.7 (p.333) will prove most
      enlightening. Additional, Illuminating, exercises for
      Dirac-Delta on p.342 (presented first instance, and
      elementary on p.63).
    - (8) Spin, next (chapter fourteen and eighteen). Read: "The
      reason the observed value of g is so close to the Dirac
      value of 2 is that configurations of increasing complexity
      are suppressed by increasing powers of the fine-structure
      constant in the superposition." (p.390). The exposition of
      Stern-Gerlach experiment is brief (best to turn elsewhere,
      I like to recommend Gottfried, 1966). On the other hand,
      chapter fifteen, addition of angular momenta, is a fine
      exposition.
    - (9) Approximation Methods: Here chapters: WKB, Born
      time-independent and time-dependent. Follow along with
      paper and pencil, as copious detail is provided. Exposition
      of so-called "fine structure" (p.466) is most instructive.
      The equation which is derived (#18.3.26--page 489) is
      cogently described in words as Shankar writes "the above
      series could be described by the following words" which he
      then proceeds to do! Excellent pedagogy! That approach, of
      equations described first through words, is applicable to
      the discussion of the Aharonov-Bohm effect (p.497). An
      introduction to Field Quantization concludes chapter
      eighteen--perhaps the best exposition to yet be
      encountered!
    - (10) The lucid discussion of introductory scattering theory
      includes (elementary) Green's functions, special functions,
      residue theorem. 3). Then, a glimpse into Feynman's
      approach to antiparticles that "negative energy particles
      can only travel backward in time"(see p.576)--this
      concludes chapter twenty. The book itself concludes with
      seventy-pages of material related to Feynman Path
      Integrals. 
    - Read: "the continuum notation is really a schematic for the
      discretized version that precedes it, and we need the
      latter to define what one means by the path integral. It is
      easy to make mistakes if one forgets this." (p.585). So,
      that is yet another sentence worth repeating-- time and
      time again!
    - (11) It is easy to continue. But, I'll stop here. There is
      simply too much to find worth commenting upon. This
      textbook was obviously a labor of love (aren't they all ?).
    - Shankar--in 1980--was my first serious exposure to this
      material. The book of Feynman and Hibbs was available then,
      (for path integrals), but it is not really a textbook.
      I greatly enjoyed Shankar's 1980 exposition.
    - A delight, then, that the 2nd edition turned out
      to be better than the 1st edition!
    - Commonplace, this material may be, in 2018, but, for its
      time  Shankar is difficult to surpass.
    - (Compare, too, with my other favorites: Gottfried,
      Merzbacher, Weinberg).
    - Highly recommended for self-study (that is, attempt those
      instructive homework problems!).
    - I love this textbook.

### Schutz Geometrical Methods of Physics
    - Bernard Schutz: " to develop a pictorial way of thinking "
    - Allow me a return to 1980: My initial introduction to
      geometrical methods (applied to physics) had occurred via
      Misner,Thorne and Wheeler's 1973 textbook, Gravitation. Not
      being satisfied with MTW, I turned to the pages of Choquet,
      DeWitt and Dillard's 1977 monograph, Analysis, Manifolds
      and Physics. Not being able to grasp that monograph,
      I perused Physics Reports: article entitled Gravitation,
      Gauge Theories and Differential Geometry. Suffice it to
      say, I was ready to give up! Frightening to me, these
      references were, in 1980.
    - Happily, the textbook by Bernard Schutz arrived upon the
      scene. Here was the introductory exposition for which I had
      been searching!
    - Even today (2018), Schutz remains the number one textbook
      which I recommend (being both introductory and elementary).
    - (1) The Problems: there are perhaps 150 of them, many
      multistep, many with hints and answers. We read: "the
      exercises are inserted in the middle of the text...they are
      designed to be worked when they are first encountered."
      (Preface). An excellent example, p.155, Exercise #4.32
      (parts " a through d"): Differential Forms and Frobenius'
      Theorem. You will not find that Theorem in the 1980 Physics
      Reports article (being advanced, it is assumed known).
      Choquet, DeWitt, Dillard write: "A Pfaff system is
      completely integrable in a neighborhood of a generic point
      if and only if it is closed in this neighborhood. (p.
      232, pitched at an advanced level). Compare Schutz: "Before
      proving this in the next section, let us see what it
      means." (p.155). And, so you do "see what it means."
      Which is but one reason to utilize Schutz:
    - As an adjunct to the more technical 1977 monograph
      Analysis, Manifolds and Physics.
    - (2) The exercises in Schutz are fantastic. What else
      ? Being originally published 1980, you will not encounter
      String Theory (Nakahara's 1990 text includes such, if that
      be your reason for studying geometrical methods). Schutz
      refers briefly to magnetic monopoles (one line, p.180).
      Also, brief discussion of charge and topology (p.179).
      Happily, these issues are not dwelt upon. In other words,
      the concentration is upon well-established applications of
      geometry to well-established Physics. That established
      Physics includes: Thermodynamics (Caratheodory viewpoint,
      p.166, not often found at this elementary level), fiber
      bundle structure of Hamiltonian equations (p.174),
      electromagnetism (as is usual), fluid dynamics (as is not
      usually done at this level) and, finally, Cosmology--that
      discussion, chapter five.
    - The initial four chapters build up to the applications.
    - (3) Schutz is also preparation for Gauge Theory. His book
      terminates with discussion of Gauge Theory (pages 219-222).
      The 1980 Physics Reports article begins with that
      discussion (and, so, again, Schutz is preparatory to more
      advanced work). Writes Schutz: "the reader should bear in
      mind that there is no a priori natural way of associating
      a one-form with a particular vector." Now, if exercise #2.1
      of Misner,Thorne and Wheeler has ever puzzled you, that
      statement by Schutz (p.51) should provide comfort. Thus,
      Schutz is helpful in going forward, or backward! The first
      Chapter of Schutz (mathematical preliminaries) is helpful
      when reviewing the first chapter (mathematical background)
      of the exposition in Analysis, Manifolds and Physics
      (1977). Therefore, I reiterate: Study Schutz then proceed
      to Choquet, DeWitt and Dillard!
    - (4) Reading Bernard Schutz: "Special Relativity is an
      example of a physical theory using a pseudo-norm, and we
      will look in some detail at it later." (p.16). "There
      are two aspects of fiber bundles which we should
      consider--these are their global properties and the
      importance of groups." (p.38) and, "You see, therefore,
      that whether a 'thing' is a scalar--or, simply
      a function--depends on its interpretation when the basis is
      changed." (p.64). Also, "One of the principal uses of
      Lie derivatives in physics is to express the notion that
      a tensor field is invariant under some transformation."
      (p.86). Then, "All physicists know the basis functions
      of the irreducible subspaces as spherical harmonics. Rather
      than go through their construction, let us simply try to
      understand them in terms of the present discussion." (p.
      109--Spherical Symmetry and Rotation Group)." Read: "The
      derivation is so quick, that it should make the
      memorization of the triple-cross product formula completely
      unnecessary." (p.131).
    - Finally: "There is more to theoretical physics than just
      its local differential equations! " (p.180).
    - Concluding my review: An excellent introduction.
      Approximately 150 exercises, with hints or solutions.
    - Many thoughtful words of wisdom to enliven the discussion.
      An excellent precursor to more advanced expositions.
    - Highly recommended!

    - (4) The problems span the gamut, from routine drill to

    - " I confined myself to purely mathematical examples and,

      for the student who tries to learn from books without the

    - Ostrowski has achieved that goal admirably!

### Wald Space, Time and Gravity
    - Wald: "important, basic, ideas described in depth clearly

    - We are fortunate to have fine introductory expositions of
      special and general relativity, including: General

      delivered 1976 are updated for a second edition 1992).
      Among the older expositions Peter Bergmann's Riddle of
      Gravitation and Synge's Talking About Relativity are
      memorable. These are essential reading for the layman.

    - (1) The Metric, that is the word to remember. Read: "The
      intrinsic properties of spacetime are fully described by
      the 'geometry of spacetime' as embodied in the spacetime
      metric." (p.23). Amusingly, I never encountered
      a professor who explicitly stated that in the classroom!
    - (2) The first two chapters (up to p.29) precede
      discussion of general relativity (third chapter). I like
      what we read: "The description of Einstein's equation is
      somewhat of an oversimplification, and some words of
      caution should be given." (p.35). Find out why!
    - (3) Cosmology, next. Isotropy and homogeneity, those will
      be the guideposts. Read: "They imply that there must exist
      a family of homogeneous and isotropic three-dimensional
      spatial 'surfaces' in spacetime." (p.44). Expansion of
      Universe beautifully expounded on the basis of Einstein's
      equations (p.48). Qualitative exposition of "Singularity
      Theorems" is delivered (p.55) Remarkably lucid!
    - (4) Evolution (dynamics) of the Universe, follows. If you
      wish for more, read Wald with Weinberg's First Three
      Minutes. Here we read of inflation: "such an inflationary
      era, if it occurred, might help account for why the present
      Universe is so homogeneous and isotropic." (p.61).
    - (5) Evolution of Stars. Read: "In general relativity it is
      harder to maintain hydrostatic equilibrium; that is, more
      pressure is needed to support a star than in Newtonian
      theory." (p.81). Learn why this is so!
    - (6) Next comes Gravitational Collapse. Regards the
      parameter 'R' (radius) read: " R is still called the radius
      of the sphere, even though it is no longer necessarily
      equal to the distance of the surface of the sphere from the
      origin." (p.86, discusses spherically symmetric
      spacetime). Black Hole and Event Horizon introduced (p.
      89). Cosmic Censorship ( "no naked singularities" ) given
      fine elaboration (pages 91 and 98). Kerr-Newman Solution
      (Black Hole possessing mass, angular momentum, charge) is
      lucidly described. Beautiful!
    - (7) Energy, that is the word for chapter eight. Or, rather,
      energy extraction from Black Holes. A ten p.exposition
      which is hard to surpass. We read "there is no notion of
      total energy in general relativity for two reasons..."
      Learn of those two reasons in this chapter.
    - (8) Chapter Nine, an interlude of Astrophysics: "focus on
      effects that might be produced by the presence of a black
      hole in a realistic physical environment" followed by
      chapter ten, Quantum Particle Creation. Atomic physics to
      Hawking radiation, thermodynamics, and more. Reading the
      conclusion: "It is impossible to predict the future of
      physics, but it may well turn out that further
      understanding of the black hole will provide us with a key
      to further understanding of the laws of nature." (p.
      139).
    - (9) An appendix derives Lorentz transformation formulas and
      shows invariance of interval (observer-independence).
      Beautiful! Suggestions for further reading concludes
      Wald's exegesis. While Wald does interject some
      mathematics, what is here included is useful for the
      discussion. As with Geroch (more elementary) and Ellis
      (less elementary),
    - Robert Wald is essential reading.
    - Highly recommended.

### Gravitation and Inertia (Wheeler)
    - "battle-tested geometrodynamics"
    - Upon revisiting Misner, Thorne and Wheeler's text,
      Gravitation (1973, recently re-published 2017, Princeton
      University Press) I decided to have another look at this
      book, published 1995, also with John A. Wheeler as
      co-author and also through Princeton University Press.
      Suffice it to say, if you already have a copy of
      Gravitation, the 1995 book is useful as collateral reading
      to that 1973 textbook. The Preface states that "it may be
      used as an introduction to general relativity." However,
      I would not recommend the book as an introduction. Also,
      keep in mind, almost 20% of the material between these
      covers is comprised of an appendix (mathematics), notations
      (G=c=1, geometrized units) and references. A brief tour:
    - (1) Preliminaries will occupy first chapter. In particular,
      what does it mean when one says "mass 'there' determines
      'inertia' here ? " and "the boundary of a boundary
      principle."
    - (2) Second chapter: "Einstein Geometrodynamics" You get
      towers up the ladder of "equivalence principles" (three
      towers: weak, medium, strong). This exposition is
      mathematically challenging. Glance at p.22, six
      theorems, then look ahead to the derivations of those
      formulas on p.26. The 'alternative' approach to Bianchi
      identities is enlightening (p.28). Black Holes touched
      upon: "the black-hole is so immaterial, so purely
      geometrical, that no such direct evidence for its 'spin' is
      available. Evidence for the spin...is obtainable by
      indirect means." (p.65).
    - I was here less than satisfied with the discussion of
      Hawking process (p.68): for that, qualitatively, read
      Hawking's 1977 Scientific American Article. Of dubious
      utility, the so-called Geon concept: "a collection of
      electromagnetic or gravitational energy--or, a mixture of
      the two--held together by its own gravitational attraction,
      that describes mass without mass." (p.78).
    - (3) Tests, that is experiments, next. Eighty pages
      (ignoring references). Sagnac effect: "examines propagation
      of light in rotating circuit" and, according to Ashtekar
      "represents a gravitational analog of the Aharonov−Bohm
      effect in electrodynamics" (Journal of Mathematical Physics
      16, 341,1975). Notice: "let us further refine the
      definition of simultaneity in general relativity." (p.
      104). Reading: "we have assumed that, locally, in every
      freely falling frame, the laws of physics are independent
      of the spacetime location of an event. It is a consequence
      of this assumption that all the physical constants
      (e,h,c,m. G) must be time-independent." (p.109). Think
      about that! Torsion is mentioned: "can indirectly act on
      light and spin-less test particles throughout the field
      equations that determine the metric" (p.137). Read: "One
      of the important features of the general relativistic field
      equations is its nonlinearity, different from classical
      Poisson equation." Gravitational Lensing mentioned (p.
      122), as is Laser Interferometer Gravitational Wave
      Observatory, that is, LIGO (pages 154-157).
    - (4) Cosmology, next. Background Microwave Radiation,
      introduced. Horizons and inflation theory, briefly touched
      upon (pages 214-220). A highlight is the elaboration of the
      "extrinsic curvature plus intrinsic curvature is
      proportional to energy-density" (p.226). Another
      highlight: Raychaudhuri equation (supplement: Baez, Meaning
      Of Einstein's Equations, American Journal of Physics 73,
      644, 2005).
    - (5) Fifth chapter, Initial Value Problem. This, a most
      useful discussion (thirty pages). Read: "Space, in the
      large, must be compact." (p.273). Think about that
     ! Read: "we shall demand that the spacetime manifold shall
      be spatially closed, that is spatially compact and
      spatially without boundary." Read, too: "Time
      ? Three-geometry itself is carrier of information about
      time." (pages 275-276). Feynman's sum-over-histories (path
      integral approach) briefly encountered (p.279). We read
      more of geons and gravitons (p.295): "gravitational geon
      is a special example of gravitational wave" and "head-on
      encounter of two gravitons creates matter out of emptiness
      of space." Again, I am not entirely certain that those
      statement make a lot of sense for a text intended as an
      introduction.
    - (6) Sixth chapter: Gravitomagnetism, read: "may be thought
      of as a manifestation of the way inertia originates;
    - mass-energy there, rules inertia here." Reading: "Stressing
      that despite the beautiful and illuminating analogies
      between classical electrodynamics and classical
      geometrodynamics, the two theories are fundamentally
      different." (p.324). Final chapter, an historical
      survey. J.J. Thomson's depiction of "pulse of
      electromagnetic radiation generated by an accelerated
      charge," updated to modern form ( 'analogies' highlighted
      throughout the text).
    - Recalling that diagram (in somewhat less-updated-form) in
      Misner, Thorne, Wheeler, Gravitation (1973, p.111).
      Read: "the geometry of the spacetime, and therefore local
      inertia, in the sense of local inertial frames at each
      event along the worldline of every test particle, are
      determined by the distribution and flow of energy
      throughout all of space." (p.395). Again, think about
      that!
    - (7) What are we to make of this monograph ? A potpourri of
      introductory and advanced material coupled with a potpourri
      of theory and experiment. I would not recommend it as an
      introduction. However, I would recommend
    - it as further enrichment. Be sure to have handy the
      venerable: Landau-Lifshitz, Classical Theory Of Fields.
    - The text invites comparison to Misner, Thorne and Wheeler:
      Gravitation. 



### Elementary Quantum Mechanics  (Saxon)
    - a remarkable pedagogic achievement
    - My first attempt at reviewing this text vanished from the
      Amazon reviews, thus, I rewrite and resubmit! Why bother
      ? One might ask. I do so in order to reinforce my belief
      that this is an excellent textbook which deserves wide
      readership.
    - Now, whether (or not) the word "elementary" of the book's
      title is appropriate, that I am unable to answer!
    - The preface states that prerequisites include: Hamiltonian
      methods of mechanics and Fourier series. Therefore, that is
      one thing to keep in mind. Also as stated in the preface,
      much of the text focuses on one-dimensional considerations,
      so a student can profit here, even if their prerequisites
      are shaky. Let me accentuate the positive attributes:
    - (1) Early on state functions, observables, linear
      superposition are highlighted (Pages 3 and 7). A positive
      attribute is the interwoven exercises ( "working out
      details" ) and the chapter-end problems ( "amplify the
      text" ).
    - (2) We read: " Specification of the state function at
      a given instant is entirely analogous to the classical
      specification of the initial position of a particle." It is
      here, second chapter, where the Born probability "rule" is
      first enunciated (p.19). David Saxon reiterates
      a progression: From state function, to observable, to
      linear superposition, to probability. The first two
      chapters (thirty pages) reinforce those considerations.
    - (3) That the state function (a terminology Saxon prefers,
      instead of "wave function") is complex, that is enunciated
      third chapter. Momentum is here introduced and Fourier
      considerations utilized. Dirac-Delta summarized (See
      Exercises for proofs, p.54). It is here that operators
      ( omentum and position) are expounded upon; commutation and
      uncertainty relations, too. We read: "The specification of
      a particular state function implies that the system in
      question has been prepared by a sequence of operations."
      (p.47) and "the uncertainty relations are automatically
      built into quantum mechanics." (p.51). An end-of-chapter
      problem ( #7 ) prompts one to "...use the uncertainty
      relation to estimate the ground state energy of the
      following systems: particle in box of length L, harmonic
      oscillator of frequency w, and particle sitting on a table
      under influence of gravity" ( p.55).
    - (4) Free particle, next. Wave packets, group velocity,
      propagators in position and momentum space. It is here that
      one falls under the spell of the Schrodinger equation (p.
      66, "Schrodinger operator equation"). And, it is here, that
      probability conservation is 'derived.' We read: " When we
      generalize from the free particle case to that of
      a particle under the influence of external forces, we shall
      turn the argument around." Excellent pedagogic strategy
     ! If one is so inclined, what Saxon writes regarding
      introduction of complex numbers..."a matter of convenience,
      not of necessity " pages 70-72... can be compared to David
      Bohm's text (1951, pages 84-86 ) or, Merzbacher (1970, p.
      16). Fourth chapter concludes with calculation of
      propagator for particle in a box. The chapter summary
      reinforces the steps: operators, probability conservation,
      correspondence principle, "features which recur again and
      again in subsequent developments."
    - (5) Fifth chapter reinforces the fourth: combining
      probability conservation and correspondence principle. It
      is here that we meet Hermitian operators: "any operator
      which represents physical observables," and "Hermitian
      conjugation can be regarded as the operator analog of
      complex conjugation for ordinary numbers." Eigenfunctions
      and eigenvalues introduced. We read: " Observables take on
      definite and precise values only in their eigenstates."
      (p.106). We get propagators and the chapter concludes
      with the all-important summary of the "postulates of
      quantum mechanics" (p.111). Let us pause to review: The
      first five chapters (115 pages) reinforce: operators,
      superposition, probability and correspondence principle.
    - (6) Moving forward from The Postulates, David Saxon
      introduces symmetry (parity operator). From there to
      harmonic oscillator. Power series solution (first)
      alongside factorization solution (second) and then creation
      operators. It is here
    - (p.138) where Saxon departs from the norm. Utilizing the
      harmonic oscillator as springboard (from factorization) he
      proceeds to develop "the creation operator representation,"
      ten pages of required reading. Propagators again!
    - The chapter concludes with introduction of numerical
      solutions in preparation for chapter six, which will
      entail:
    - (7) Approximation Methods. Read: "the structure of the WKB
      result is easy to understand." (p.176).
    - Progressing from WKB to Rayleigh-Ritz, "a variational
      method" where "Schrodinger's equation is the Euler's
      equation of the variational problem" and "note the
      competition between the kinetic and potential energy" (p.
      189). Proceeding from there to perturbation theory: "the
      most important method for obtaining approximate solutions."
      allotting thirty pages to such. Interesting, too, that
      Saxon introduces matrices in this chapter (p.201).
    - Placement here directly attributable to the use of them
      while pressed into service for perturbation theory.
    - (8) Systems of particles: Canonical transformations
      followed by coupled harmonic oscillators. Identical
      particles followed by symmetrization and pauli exclusion
      principle. An excellent qualitative and physical discussion
      ensues (pages 245-249). Eight chapters ( concluding on p.
      261) have elucidated one-dimensional considerations.
    - Time, now, for three-dimensions:
    - (9) Free particles, Hamiltonians, operators, probability,
      propagators, again. So, a reiteration and reinforcement of
      all that came before, this time for three-dimensions. Then,
      we 'separate-variables' as prelude to spherical coordinates
      and angular momentum. All this in preparation for--what
      else--first introduction of the hydrogen atom. A problem
      ensues: "Consider the hydrogen atom in its ground state.
      Suppose the Coulomb interaction is suddenly turned off, and
      the electron moves off as a free particle. Treating the
      proton as infinitely massive for simplicity, find the
      probability that a measurement will yield..." (p.296,
      parts a through d).
    - (10) Spin Angular Momentum. We read: " the spin of
      a particle may be regarded as an internal degree of freedom
      which is in some sense associated with the internal
      structure of these particles," and " although spin is an
      internal property...it must be coupled in some way to the
      external world if it is to have any physical significance."
      (p.317). Excellent and challenging problems concludes
      the chapter.
    - Saxon: "...many problems are too difficult for the student
      to master in his first attempt." That may be the only
      stumbling block! On the other hand, that is also an
      advantage for those who retain textbooks after the first
      attempt! So, do not discount Saxon merely on the basis of
      his challenging problem sets.
    - (11) From hydrogen to Helium to periodic table to
      scattering.,here (scattering) read: "The Born approximation
      is the physicist's absolutely indispensable qualitative
      guide," after which an introduction to Dirac equation and
      density matrix. A suitable conclusion it is, as the Dirac
      formalism here paves the way to relativistic
      considerations, while the density matrix formalism paves
      the way for mixed states (that is, not 'pure' ). The door
      is left open for field theory and measurement theory.
    - (12) Finally, I ask the student to compare and contrast
      David Saxon's 1968 textbook to Dicke & Wittke's 1960
      textbook.
    - Whereas they both conclude upon the density matrix, the
      later elaborates considerably into quantum statistical
      mechanics. The Dirac equation,however, is not introduced
      into the later. In fact, I make a stronger statement--Saxon
      and Dicke & Wittke are complementary. (Neither uses the
      Dirac Bra-Ket notation and they both preclude use of
      Feynman's path integrals). Dicke summarizes Hamiltonian
      mechanics (pages 77-89) whereas Saxon assumes such as
      prerequisite.
    - Saxon writes that his book is based upon a third-year
      undergraduate lecture course. Probably, too optimistic!
    - Despite the fact that Saxon's is not an entirely
      " elementary" introduction (and, Dicke's is not an entirely
      advanced introduction), both textbooks are recommended for
      further, if invaluable, enrichment.

      paradoxical dualism that requires the use of both wave and
      particle models to describe the system." (p.74).
    - (2) "In an atom, the effective index of refraction varies
      in such a way that the parts of the wave at larger
      distances from the center move faster than those at smaller
      distances..." (p.75).
    - (3) "The wave-function has often been called
      a wave-of-probability, but a more accurate term is a 'wave'
      from which many related probabilities can be calculated."
      (p.96).
    - (4) "If we wish to refer to light quanta as photons, one
      must use this word very cautiously, since it implies a more
      definite type of particle nature than is really possessed
      by light energy." (p.108).
    - (5) "...for this reason, the term electron will hereafter
      denote neither a wave nor a particle...." (p.118).
    - (6) "...what appears to have taken place is that when the
      position of the electron was observed, the wave function
      suffered a collapse from a broad front down to a narrow
      region." (p.120).
    - (7) "...the wave-function is in a closer correspondence
      with the state of the electron than it would be if it were
      a simple classical probability function." (p.126)
    - (8) "...this behavior of the wave-function leads to
      a qualitative description of the properties of matter in
      terms of incompletely defined and mutually incompatible
      potentialities, which can be realized more fully only in
      interaction with a suitable system in the environment."
      (p.143).
    - (9) "...the lack of uniqueness of the Schrodinger equation
      is also a useful thing to know because it shows us where
      modifications may be made, if necessary, to produce
      agreement with nonclassical experimental results." (p.
      196).
    - (10) "...we therefore conclude that in quantum theory, as
      well as in classical theory, a gauge transformation leads
      to no new physical consequences." (p.359).
    - (11) "In a process of measurement, the system of categories
      is, in general, actually transformed, and this mathematical
      transformation is reflected physically in transformations
      between particle-like behavior to wave-like behavior...in
      these intermediate systems of categorization, the system
      spreads over a range of positions and momenta." (p.386).
    - (12) "...at the quantum level, the mathematical description
      provided by the wave-function is certainly not in one to
      one correspondence with the actual behavior of the system
      under description, but only in a statistical
      correspondence." (p.619).
    - Concluding: My perusal of Bohm's text throughout the years
      (1981 to present day) reinforces my opinion that this text
      is not all that elementary, nor is it really an
      introduction. If utilized as a reference, David Bohm's text
      is exceptional for its exposition of Scattering Theory and
      Process of Measurement.
    - Additionally, Bohm writes: "It has been the author's
      purpose throughout this book to present the main ideas of
      the quantum theory in non-mathematical terms." (preface).
      However, I do not believe that the goal, as Bohm has set
      out for himself, has been achieved.
    - The wonderful book entitled: The Odd Quantum (Sam Treiman,
      1999, Princeton University Press), comes closest to
      achieving that particular goal (a nonmathematical quantum
      mechanics exposition).
    - Max Jammer writes: "...the critical reader of Bohm's book
      could not have failed to note that some explanations did
      not fully reflect the spirit of Bohr's philosophy."
      (Philosophy Of Quantum Mechanics, Wiley, 1974, pages
      278-296). 


### Weintraub - Differential Forms
    - pedagogic and mathematically oriented introduction
    - By now (2017) there are numerous texts on the market from
      which to learn the topic of differential forms.
    - Many years ago (1976) my first introduction to forms was
      inspired by Gravitation MTW. Steven Weinberg's text,
      Gravitation and Cosmology, was valuable in this regard too.
      Regrettably, neither of those textbooks satisfied my need
      for an introductory account. I very much enjoy both of
      those textbooks and both are (still) useful.
    - However, I remain dubious as to the efficacy of physics
      textbooks trying to present mathematics piecemeal--or, of
      mathematics books trying to teach physics piecemeal! Of
      course, many of my favorite textbooks do just that.
      (Alternating between the physical and the mathematical ).
      Weintraub has taken an alternative route, as he writes:
      "...looking at this material more mathematically actually
      simplifies it...." (preface). Acquire the book, and see if
      you agree. Quite frankly, I agree with that statement.
      Thus, Weintraub's path is decidedly more mathematical and
      less physical and yet a simpler approach to differential
      forms!
    - (1) Firstly, the book is quite computational: Therefore,
      the examples and student exercises are as concrete as
      required for manipulative proficiency. For the physicist or
      engineer, this text will provide much in the way of
      manipulative skill. Read: "...vector calculus only works in
      R^3...best to stick to differential forms themselves, which
      are naturally defined in all dimensions." (p.31). The
      second chapter introduces Manifolds: "...we can integrate
      differential forms over oriented manifolds, this is the
      reason we are interested in them here." A 25-p.heuristic
      introduction which paves the way for the following chapter
      of 'One-Forms.' We arrive to the third chapter: This
      chapter places the previous two in perspective. We read:
      "The requirement that differential forms be alternating
      comes from the fact that we need to keep track of
      orientation." (p.68). Meet pull-backs: " a tricky idea,"
      (p.73). Twenty pages of pull-backs and push-forwards (pages
      73-93) are enlightening, presenting examples and providing
      for excellent practice exercises. A bonus, of pedagogic
      utility, are the answers supplied for the exercises.
      A useful complement, indeed. Now, we have embraced the
      simpler aspects (that is, chapters one, two and three)--it
      is time to get serious:
    - (2) That is, chapter four: integration. Read: "...line
      integrals are nothing new, this is one instance of our
      basic theme, that vector calculus all comes from
      differential forms." (p.109). Integrals: from line, to
      surface (flux, p.130)
    - to volume (3-form over a solid body--p.160). Seventy-five
      pages presented as lucid as possible. And, we reinforce
      pull-backs (from the previous chapter) with another
      go-around here, reading: " We will now reformulate
      integration in these terms.You will see that we have been
      using pull-backs in disguise, now we're removing the
      disguise." (p.163). An excellent pedagogic strategy!
    - (3) Fifth chapter: Generalized Stokes theorem. Again,
      concrete and computational supersedes theoretical,
      providing for refreshing alternative to the usual
      abstraction. In fact, Weintraub points out "...we have
      tried to write this book in such a way that you can skip
      the proofs without loss of continuity..." (preface). The
      author has achieved another of his stated goals. Final
      Chapter (six) surveys more advanced offerings (cohomology)
      and reinforces the previous textual material at an ever
      more advanced vantage. Again, excellent pedagogy! Not only
      are we given a brief forward look (cohomology), but,
      a backward glance (of earlier material) at an increased
      level of abstraction.
    - (4) My only quibble with the text is the absence of
      a bibliography and a rather abbreviated subject index.
      Otherwise, this text, " A complement to vector calculus"
      delivers exactly what it promises. A bridge to more
      advanced offerings, this introductory mathematics text is
      difficult to ignore.
    - Highly recommended! 

### Lanczos Variational Principles
    - pedagogic, accessible, relevant: the inimitable Cornelius
      Lanczos
    - Quoting from the fine book review by Michael Berg,
      Mathematical Association of America: " a wonderful
      experience to encounter a treatment of this material in
      a style that is now all but absent, evincing historical
      scholarship and riddled with discussions of roots and
      connections, with all of it presented both leisurely and
      thoroughly. " It is difficult to improve upon that MAA
      review, but I would like to append a few highlights of this
      beautiful book:
    - (1) In contradistinction to many a modern author, Lanczos
      shows the link between Quaternions and Special
      Relativity.(pages 304-314). In another break with many an
      author, the three spatial components are multiplied by the
      imaginary unit, the temporal component remains real! (p.
      302). If that is not enough to whet the appetite, there is
      progressive build-up to General Relativity: from scalar to
      vector field, concluding with the tensor field (pages
      314-331). The exposition of special and general relativity
      alone is worth the price of admission! Read: " in the
      light of relativity the application of the calculus of
      variations to the laws of nature assumes more than
      accidental significance." (p.xxv).
    - (2) Lanczos pays homage to mathematician Elie Cartan! He
      makes reference to Elie Cartan's 1922 "Lecons sur les
      invariants integraux." Happily, the modern student can do
      the same, as the publication is available and free-to-read,
      on the Archive.Org website. Compare and contrast. Perusal
      of Lanczos will pave the way to understanding Cartan's
      beautiful monograph! Get hold of it.
    - (3) Cornelius Lanczos interweaves mathematics and physics
      plus a smattering of philosophy. Historical antecedents not
      eschewed. Is Lanczos out of date ? I am uncertain as to the
      actual meaning of the term "out-of-date." If that term
      merely implies an older copyright date, surely that is
      a mis-characterization of the usefulness of any scientific
      work. Nothing, and I reiterate in strongest terms--nothing
      in this text needs to be re-learned, or discarded. Lanczos
      only increases appreciation of analytical mechanics as
      evidenced through the work of Lagrange, Hamilton and
      others. Henri Arzelies reminds us: " It is necessary to
      prevent research physicists from connecting the value of
      a research paper with its date." (Relativistic Point
      Dynamics, 1972, p.xi).
    - (4) Lanczos, as few others are do, explains the meaning of
      the term "apparent force" writing "The name is misleading,
      however, if it is interpreted as a force which is not as
      'real' as any given physical force." (p.98). Read, of
      Legendre Transformation: "...every Hamiltonian problem can
      be associated with a corresponding Lagrangian problem."
      (pages 161-171). Pontificating upon Energy: "...the dualism
      of kinetic and potential energy is a puzzling problem to
      philosophical thinking." (p.131). And our author is
      careful to elaborate upon many a side issue. (Keeping in
      mind his terminology: 'potential energy as work function,'
      and differential forms are implicit ).
    - (5) Few discussions, based upon the works of D'Alembert,
      Euler and Gauss and their contributions to mechanics, are
      as elementary, or insightful, as presented herein. Read:
      " By this device, dynamics is reduced to statics...we have
      deduced these differential equations by static
      considerations." (p.89).
    - (6) Read: " Postulate A is actually the only postulate of
      analytical mechanics, and is thus of fundamental
      importance--that is: The virtual work of the forces of
      reaction is always zero for any virtual displacement which
      is in harmony with the given kinematical constraints."
      (p.76). Chapter three, virtual work, elaborates on that
      postulate. (Keep in mind: equalities versus inequalities).
    - (7) Lanczos, similar to another classic, Analytical
      Dynamics (Whittaker) values analysis over geometrical
      pictures. That is, few diagrams to be found. Cornelius
      Lanczos : "...one of the foremost intentions of the
      author...to give the student a chance to discover for
      himself the hidden beauty of these theories." With that
      goal, he has succeeded. A more-or-less historical route is
      followed. Thus, his exposition is not necessarily the
      fastest route to any of the topics discussed. However, one
      does get to 'live through once again' what the masters of
      the past were able to accomplish in their own time.
      Pedagogic attributes: Boxes enclose important formulas
      (these always derived within the text).
    - Exercises (of a simple sort) are sprinkled throughout.
    - (8) Lanczos serves as springboard and collateral reading to
      other material, beginning with Jourdain's Principle of
      Least Action (1913). Simpler aspects of Lagrangian
      mechanics are found in Byerly's Generalized Coordinates
      (1916). For differential forms and applications, Burke's
      Applied Differential Geometry (Cambridge,1985). For
      advanced perspective (terse): Mandelstam and Yourgrau
      Variational Principles in Dynamics and Quantum Theory
      (Pitman,1968).
    - (9) A well-rounded approach can not ignore Richard Feynman:
      Chapter 19, Volume Two, Lecture on The Principle of Least
      Action. Lanczos (pages 54-59) presents nothing less than
      mathematical version of that which Feynman presents in his
      physical exposition. In any event, there are resources
      aplenty. Regardless of background (mathematics or physics)
      Cornelius Lanczos can serve as entree to many an aspect of
      variational principles of mechanics.
    - Highly recommended to all students. 


### Maxwell Matter Motion
    - James Clerk Maxwell Motivates Classical Mechanics
    - Of James Clerk Maxwell--as an educator-- we discover:
    - "... had too much learning and too much originality to be
      at his best in elementary teaching.
    - For those however who could follow him his teaching was
      a delight."
    - (Fleming, p.113, in Maxwell Commemoration
      Volume,Cambridge 1931).
    - Of this "classical tractate on the principles of Dynamics,"
      one ascertains the accuracy of the statement by Fleming,
      Maxwell's student.
    - However, do not feel intimidated; do not let trepidation
      prevent you from perusal of this great historical
      monograph.
    - A sad commentary on the education of today's student, one
      is unable to find reference to this document in any
      Mechanics textbook.
    - (Neglected in six textbooks that I could check:1995
      Knudsen,1980 Goldstein,1971 Symon,1970 Marion,1966
      Kibble,1949 Synge).
    - Granted that much of this treatise is at an elementary
      vantage (Elementary Algebra and Geometry being dominant).
    - Granted there are few examples, and no exercises to be
      solved for student awareness.
    - Even so, much here is of continuing utility. I personally
      derived great enjoyment in its perusal.
    - (Then again, if James Clerk Maxwell isn't your 'cup of
      tea,' then by all means choose to ignore it!).
    - Let us read from the tract:
    - (1) " All our knowledge of both time and place
      is,essentially, relative..." (p.12).
    - (2) "...it is impossible to speak of a body being at rest
      or in motion except with reference,
    - express or implied, to some other body." (p.22).
    - (3) Newton's Laws--introduced p.27--expounded upon for
      the next twenty pages.
    - (4) Angular Momentum--p.51. Conservation thereof, next
      p..
    - (5) Work--Energy (Potential and Kinetic)--is Chapter Five.
      Note the algebraic form of Impulse-Momentum Theorem (p.
      58).
    - "...when the interval of time is so small that we may
      consider the force as constant during that time.."
    - A wonderful explication (algebra/geometry) of kinetic
      energy and center of mass for two particles.(Pages 61-63).
    - (6) Sixth Chapter, Maxwell summarizes--with words, not
      equations-- the previous expanse of 75 Pages.
    - (7) Finally, the introduction of Newton's Gravity. Thirty
      Pages of beautiful exposition:
    - Geometry and the simple pendulum (p.97),Geometry and
      Kepler's Laws (Pages 105-111) both highlights of the text.
    - Experiment, too--Cavendish and the Torsion Balance--are
      beautifully elaborated upon (Pages 116-119).
    - (8) On the Cause of Gravitation, we read (p.121): "The
      attempts which have been made since the time of Newton
    - to solve this difficult question are few in number, and
      have not led to any well-established result." (recall,this
      is 1877).
    - (9) Final Chapter, Nine. A reprint of a chapter from the
      Treatise on Electricity and Magnetism: Here, Lagrange
      introduced.
    - We read: "The aim of Lagrange was to bring dynamics under
      the power of Calculus." (p.123).
    - A wonderful excursion this turns out to be!
    - There are two (more advanced) appendices--written by Larmor
      in 1920: Relativity and Principle of Least Action.
    - An excellent conclusion to a beautiful book.
    - This historical document makes an excellent adjunct to any
      modern-day exposition of classical mechanics. 



### Physics of Atoms and Molecules; An Introduction to the
Structure of Matter (Fano)
    - outstanding pedagogic introduction
    - How to categorize this textbook ? Unusual, yes
     ! Refreshing, yes! Pedagogic masterpiece, yes!
    - Everyone has their favorites (amongst mine: Feynman's
      Lectures Volume Two and Wichmann's Quantum Physics).
    - The above references are as different from each other as
      Fano's is different from them. Masterpieces all.
    - What, then, differentiates this textbook from the others ?
    - (1) Regarding intent: "We wish to keep in sight as much as
      possible the connection between each mathematical step and
      its physical substrate." (In that regard it falls in line
      with Feynman and Wichmann).
    - (2) At six-hundred pages of length, twenty-two chapters,
      the text is longer than most introductory accounts.
    - The Problems for student involvement are priceless (not
      least, due to the availability of the solutions provided).
    - (3) The twenty p.Appendix A--Linearity, Invariance,
      Fourier Analysis--is one of the most lucid I have
      encountered. Read: "We stress here the correspondence
      between the characteristics of physical systems and those
      of the mathematical models that describe them." Appendix
      B--Normal Modes--presents a wonderful five-p.synopsis,
      as does the Appendix on Statistical Distributions. If one
      delights in the content offered in these Appendices, the
      entirety of the main text will be 'icing on the cake.'
    - (4) Background presumed of the reader: Mathematical Methods
      in the Physical Sciences, by Boas, provides needed
      mathematics. The text Fundamentals of Statistical and
      Thermal Physics, by Reif, more than ample for
      physics.(Although, both of those textbooks contains more
      than is strictly needed in order to peruse Fano's
      textbook).
    - (5) Now, onward to Fano and Fano! You will learn of early
      experiments in chapter one. Avogadro's number,
      cross-sections, scattering--touched upon. You will apply
      these early concepts early, in the Problem Sets for chapter
      one, these Problems are excellent. Interesting it is to
      realize how far one can get merely utilizing dimensional
      analysis.
    - If at all familiar with Enrico Fermi's style, you will note
      homage to him for completing these problems!
    - (6) Next, Electromagnetism. We read: "...it is actually
      possible to achieve a fit of the experimental values with
      the analytic expression..." (p.43). Photoelectric effect
      is beautifully presented, read: "The energy transferred in
      an elementary process is proportional to the oscillation
      frequency of the incident light and of the interatomic
      currents that absorb the light. This type of relationship
      prevails throughout quantum physics." (p.65). The
      chapter concludes with some beautiful (experimental)
      problems with an outline of their (straightforward)
      solutions.
    - (7) With the fourth chapter read: "Radiation propagates in
      a straight line through a homogeneous medium,
    - but is deflected, or scattered, by inhomogeneities. Changes
      in the direction of propagation imply changes in momentum."
      The toughest calculation in chapter four utilizes solution
      to Poisson's equation, read: "...a connection between the
      scattering of electrons and that of X-rays is provided by
      the Poisson Equation..." (p.108). The relationship is
      beautifully outlined.
    - (8) The brief (eight-page) chapter five--Statistical
      Aspects of Atomic Physics--is simply delightful to read. In
      so doing: "Randomness in the time sequence of atomic events
      is qualitatively familiar to anyone who has listened to the
      irregular clicking of a Geiger Counter." (p.121). You
      will delight in six problems (pedagogic) concluding the
      chapter. Read: "Physics can make predictions only about
      those features of the outcome of experiments which are
      reproducible." (p.126). That concludes part one, it has
      a decidedly experimental emphasis.
    - (9) Part Two (one-hundred pages) will succinctly expound
      the basics of introductory Quantum Physics, therefore
      decidedly theoretical. Energy, momentum, light
      polarization. If ever there were a more lucid, elementary,
      explication of Stern-Gerlach analysis, I am at a loss to
      recall such. Read: "Qualitatively this result is quite
      analogous to the corresponding result for light
      polarization." (p.145) and "we start here from two basic
      concepts--the incompatibility of experimental analysis by
      magnets of different orientation and the symmetry of space
      about the direction of the magnetic field." (p.148).
      Next chapter, elaborates upon...
    - (10) Spin. We read: "A clue to the more practical extension
      of these laws is provided by the remark that the
      probabilities are non-negative quantities, like the light
      intensity--we shall see that quantum mechanics is
      simplified formally by representing probabilities as
      quadratic expressions in terms of suitably defined
      amplitudes." (p.171). Elaborations upon those remarks
      takes us far into two chapters (eight and nine). Those are
      then generalized (beyond two-state systems) in the
      following chapter. This is a tour de force of exposition.
      Read: "Our main goal is still to account for the size and
      stability of atoms" and "the main step is to determine the
      probability amplitudes that connect eigenstates of position
      and eigenstates of velocity." (p.211). About parity:
      "Notice that this mode of expression treats a symmetry as
      a physical quantity like the momentum. This way of treating
      symmetry is characteristic of quantum physics."
    - (11) Operator language is introduced (p.229) in
      preparation for a complete derivation of uncertainty
      relations: "...analogous to a standard formula of
      statistics." (pages 230-233). The second part concludes
      with Schrodinger equation and a few important solutions.
      More importantly, we learn: "This mathematical
      characterization of an eigenstate has familiar analogs
      throughout physics." (p.239).
    - (12) Part Three: Mechanics of Single Atoms; Part Four:
      Aggregates of Atoms. Much too much to review!
    - The motivation: "Maximizing the contact between
      mathematical symbols and the observations they represent
      seems worth an additional effort in an introductory study
      of atomic physics." (p.276). A Problem: "The NH-3,
      Ammonia, molecule, has the shape of a pyramid with an
      equilateral triangle of three hydrogen atoms at its base.
      Show that two of the principal moments of inertia of the
      molecule are equal, thus verifying that the levels of its
      rotational spectrum are given by the 'symmetric top'
      formula." (chapter twenty, p.492). Check your
      understanding against the solution!
    - (13) Concluding: We have here an undergraduate textbook,
      replete with experiment and theory, with homework problems
      and their solutions. This is a distinctive exposition, it
      is simultaneously qualitative and quantitative.
      Mathematically and physically this textbook tends to fill
      the gaps found in more advanced expositions.
    - Not too elementary, not too advanced. A remarkable
      pedagogic achievement.
    - Another text not nearly as well known as it deserves to be.
    - Highly Recommended to all students. 

### Bamberg Maths 1 and 2
    - Unique and Innovative Mathematical Pedaogy
    - Preface: "course is aimed at students with an interest in
      Physics who have a good grounding in one-variable Calculus"
      and "our pedagogical approach follows the Spiral Method,
      wherein we cover the same topic several times at increasing
      levels of sophistication and range of application." (This
      is an excellent pedagogic strategy!). Let me commence with
      proclaiming that I love these two volumes. I proffer
      evidence in support of my view regards volume one:
    - (1) We noted that answers for the exercises are not
      provided. Yet, there are "hints" supplied for quite a few
      of these exercises (see: p.54, #20; p.75, #15; p.
      114, #7,13,17; p.269, #8 &10). The student will locate
      more hints. Might I remark that many of the exercises are
      not too difficult (see: p.48, #5, 7 and 13; p.73, #1,
      4, 5, 6 and 7; p.112, #1, 6 and 7....the list can be
      continued). Solutions can be verified on one's own! If
      asked to verify the inverse of a 2X2 determinant, a routine
      matter (via matrix multiplication) to "verify" the answer
     ! Solutions to problem #11, parts a through f, p.116
      (differential equations) can certainly be verified on one's
      own.
    - (2) Chapter Three--Linear Differential Equations in the
      plane--is as lucid as one is likely to find at an
      elementary level.
    - (3) Study p.128, it is easy enough to follow along to
      the explicit construction using the Gram-Schmidt process,
      this but one example of the interplay between the abstract
      and the concrete permeating the textbook.
    - (4) Special Relativity, beginning Section #4.8 (pages
      148-166) is an interesting account. The account encompasses
      twenty pages, where we learn: "twin paradox is, of course,
      no paradox, just an immediate corollary of the reverse
      triangle inequality."
    - (5) Chapter Five: Calculus in the Plane, Section# 5.6,
      pullback notation, is incredibly informative (pages
      209-215).
    - (6) Chapter Six--theorems of differential calculus:
      Lagrange multipliers, as lucid an exposition as any
      encountered!
    - The Section #6.3, inverse function theorem, along with
      Newton's algorithmic method, are given beautiful
      discussion.
    - (7) Line integrals and differential forms are introduced
      next (beginning p.246): This is a fantastic Chapter!
    - Note that Problem 12 a&b is one which many Physics students
      should already have mastered. Note: orientation,
      parameterization---understanding these concepts are
      absolutely central and essential to understanding the
      textbook.
    - (8) Double integrals, next up: The chapter begins with
      exterior derivative, then two-forms, Once again, pages 284
      and 285 render that which is abstract to that which is
      concrete--the mere computation of an Integral. Section
      # 8.7 deserves highlight, a nice discussion between
      differences of two-forms and densities. Green's theorem the
      highlight concluding this great chapter.
    - (9) Tenth chapter will place everything previously
      assimilated within the context of the axioms for vector
      spaces.
    - The usual properties (commutative, associative, identity,
      inverse), dual spaces, quotient spaces (equivalence class)
    - and linear transformations, concluding with adjoint
      transformations make this the most challenging part of the
      book.
    - (10) Finally, determinants. From axioms to concrete
      computations, a rather satisfying conclusion to volume one.
    - Not forgetting that the exercises for this chapter (pages
      399-400) are a mix of the routine (a, b, c and d) and the
      innovative ( the remaining exercises!). Here, then,
      a unique textbook which can be profitably perused by both
      mathematics and physics students. Unfortunate, then, that
      it is not more often utilized by students and instructors.
    - This text does offer comparisons to Burke (Applied
      Differential Geometry) and Bressoud (Second Year Calculus).
    - While it has much in common with those textbooks, all three
      texts should be studied!
    - The textbook is sufficiently interesting and innovative as
      to be heartily recommended for further enrichment.
    - I am a proponent of the "spiral method" of teaching, thus,
      not completely unbiased.
    - Highly recommended! 



### Classical Field Theory: EM and Gravitation (Low)
    - advanced enrichment
    - A shame that Low's text is apparently much disliked.
      Francis Low may not have written a pedagogic textbook, but,
      he has written a fascinating treatise stamped with his
      inimitable imprint. As such, it deserves consideration.
      Keep these words in mind: "field" and "theory." As he
      states in his preface, the book is targeted for graduate
      students. Therefore, Griffiths (at least) should have been
      assimilated previous to this text. Is this book for you
      ? I suggest a look at the appendices: Vectors & tensors,
      spherical harmonics and orthogonal polynomials. There you
      will find thirty-three exercise problems. Can you solve an
      appreciable number of those exercises ? If so, then onward.
      If not, turn away, and learn the mathematics of these
      appendices---without such you will be completely lost! Are
      you comfortable dealing with Dirac delta functions and all
      that that concept entails ? If not, this poses another
      hurdle. Gaussian units are utilized. If perched in MKS
      units for classical electrodynamics, then, another hurdle
      to overcome. In fact, perusal of Chapter One of Garg (2012)
      Classical Electromagnetism In A Nutshell, will prepare one
      well. Assuming the necessary (not obviously sufficient)
      prerequisites, the book offers much of value:
    - (1) We meet Green and his function (p.17). This gives
      indication that the text will be challenging! A nice
      discussion regarding generalized forces and keeping track
      of signs in a system of conductors (p.22). The exercises
      for the first chapter are indicative of the entire
      textbook. Physics is the least of the problems; more than
      likely it will be insufficient background in mathematics
      which poses barrier to their solution. Problem #1.7 ( parts
      a, b & c) should be accessible to a graduate student (p.
      37). Problem# 1.35 is quite an interesting problem--this,
      a variational calculation (p.44). First chapter behind
      us, we soldier on to the next chapter.
    - (2) The treatment of magnetic dipoles (p.50) offers
      comparison to Garg's chapter four. I like them both. The
      vector manipulations are either second-nature, or not. If
      not, then one needs to refresh one's memory (Reiterating:
      study Garg). Section 2.5, motional electromotive force and
      induction, is rather interesting to peruse (pages 66-69).
      Exercises at the end of chapter two are challenging and
      interesting. I like section 2.15 (p.79), reference to
      invariance of force and change of coordinate system.
      Francis Low describes time-averaged flux in a careful
      manner, so as not to lose the reader (p.105).
    - (3) Green, utilized in the following chapter: Radiation.
      Careful consideration--that is, paper and pencil on hand
      throughout each intermediate step--will serve one well
      throughout this chapter. Read: "We find, after some
      algebra..." (p.167). Can you "do the algebra" ? It
      is--after all--merely algebra, I presume such would be
      known to the intended his audience. Discussion of
      scattering occupies approximately sixty pages. It is here
      where our esteemed author has made his mark. We start with
      scalar wave equation. Pay attention to Equation #5.2.0
      Read: "we shall use that procedure from now on." Expansion
      in Legendre polynomials (p.198, equation #5.5.5) Learn
      them now. Again, we are warned that Equations 5.10.9 and
      5.10.10 can be solved algebraically (p.219) thus, an
      admonition to do the algebra. A tough chapter, to be sure;
      Happily, we have a master to lead us by the hand.
    - (4) The same can be said for the Problems. If you have
      progressed this far in the text, the next two chapters will
      hold your interest: Invariance and Special Relativity.
      Lucid as any--though, admittedly of a formal nature
      ("Schwingerian").
    - Especially Section 6.6 (pages 271-277) proved most
      enlightening--conservation of electric charge, the
      symmetric stress tensor. We read: "This is as close as one
      can come in a relativistic theory--the center of energy
      moves with constant velocity. None of the other center of
      mass theorems of non-relativistic mechanics hold." (p.
      277).
    - (5) My favorite chapter follows: Lagrangian field theory.
      The chapter ends with a most fascinating demonstration of
      the conservation of the stress- energy- momentum Tensor.
      Follow along very carefully, in reality the author leaves
      very few details obscured. Thus, it can be assimilated. The
      chapter-end problems make for some of the more challenging
      of the book. Dare I say, research topics ? Finally,
      Gravity: The homage to Schwinger is apparent. As with
      Feynman, Gupta, Thirring, " we will be led to a theory that
      will turn out to be a linear approximation to Einstein's
      Theory..." (p.339). Fifty pages of essential reading
     ! The book, as is apparent, is an advanced one. Probably,
      it should not be labeled a textbook. More so, than not, it
      is an interesting rendering of advanced material from
      Francis Low's perspective. The book is different enough
      from the usual texts so as to bring something new to the
      table.
    - By all means, the prerequisites should be firmly in
      hand--second nature, even. (Griffiths and/or Garg ). If
      not, then surely this text will be inaccessible. Obviously
      it is not a first initiation to any of its topics. With
      those reservations in mind, I do recommend this book to
      those with an interest in these things. Quoting from
      Physics Today:
    - " Despite these criticisms, I feel that Low's book provides
      a valuable introduction to electromagnetism and gravitation
      at the graduate level and will be of particular use to
      aspiring theoretical particle physicists." (Robert M.
      Wald). 


### Complex Variables Dover (Flanigan)
    - Outstanding Introductory Pedagogic Masterpiece
    - My review is made all the easier, due to the
      number of outstanding reviews already written.
    - This book is my favorite introductory account. Certainly,
      it stands apart from the crowded market of undergraduate
      textbooks.
    - ( I have studied from: Ablowitz, Bak, Boas, Hille, Needham,
      Polya....to name merely a few in alphabetical sequence).
    - Ignore Flanigan at your own peril. Anything less than five
      stars does an injustice to this outstanding pedagogic
      masterpiece.
    - (1) First, we look at the Problem Sets. Already, Section
      1.1.2, one is asked to establish the Triangle Inequality,
      the author's hint: Sketch!
    - Next, "Prove that a plane set is bounded if and only if its
      closure is bounded." So, excellent problems--varying
      degrees of difficulty--abound.
    - (2) Green's Theorem, Green's Identities, conclude the first
      chapter. Green's Theorems--in three guises--given
      meaningful exposition:
    - First, as two-dimensional version of integration by parts.
      Second, as symmetrization of the first. Third, as
      steppingstone to Poisson's integral formula. A pedagogic
      treat for all students. Problem #7 is nice: " Write down
      a line integral over the boundary which shows how
      a harmonic function is determined inside... by information
      relating to the boundary....use one of Green's Identities."
      (p.66, paraphrased).
    - (3) Chapter One--Calculus in the Plane--is a delight to
      peruse. From Real (Chapter One) to Harmonic (Chapter Two),
      Flanigan provides pithy commentary throughout-- alongside
      Morals. For instance: "On domains with holes, necessary
      conditions for conservatism need not be sufficient. The
      same is true for exactness of differentials." (p.74).
      Note the comment regarding the process of learning about
      Harmonic Functions: "What are we doing then? A little bit
      of qualitative theory of partial differential equations--
      studying the behavior without knowing the solutions
      explicitly." (p.79). And, so it goes throughout this
      wonderful textbook.
    - (4) Pay particular attention to the section on the
      Logarithm (that is, section 3.4.2); An Appendix presents
      the Riemann Surface for log z.
    - Well done! Chapter Four, Integrals of Analytic Functions.
      Here, a three-pronged approach: (1) Line Integrals, (2)
      Basic Theorems,(3) Results which are "complex analytic
      versions of the qualitative theorems about harmonic
      functions." Note the comment that " the true purpose of the
    - ML-Inequality is to prove theorems." (p.157). Problem
      # 12, " How have we used Green's Theorem? " We converted
      a given line integral of {P dx + Q dy} and then invoked
      Green's Theorem to obtain a double integral (p.157),
      Retrace our steps for each of the following
      situations....inside/outside theorem, curl and circulation,
      divergence theorem and the Cauchy integral theorem...."
      Excellent pedagogy!
    - (5) Fundamental Theorem of Algebra (Section 4.8);
      Commentary: "...is an existence theorem" and "roots always
      exist, but it may be very difficult to write them down."
      A Section on Power Series ends with nine true/false
      Questions (p.208) and, The Moral, "Convergent Power
      Series yield Analytic Functions, in the next section we
      will learn that Analytic Functions yield Convergent Power
      Series!"
    - (6) One could continue describing the book in this vein:
      The exposition is lucid. The pedagogy is fresh. The
      problems are workable, many with hints and answers. The
      author offers pithy comments, brief remarks, morals, plus,
      examples and proofs--- all delightful to peruse.
    - This is a masterpiece of mathematical literature. Stranded
      on a desert island ? Keep this book handy while there.
    - Highly recommended to all students.
