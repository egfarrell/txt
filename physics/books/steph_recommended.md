### Mathematical Background
    - Readers wishing to brush up on multivariable calculus may
      want to consult:
        > Basic Multivariable Calculus: Marsden, Tromba Weinstein

    - or the more sophisticated,
        > Vector Calculus, Linear Algebra and Differential Forms:
        > A Unified Approach, by J. H. Hubbard and B. B. Hubbard

    - Another classic for multi-variable calculus as well as well
      as differential equations, with a brief elementary section
      on planetary motion is
        > Calculus, Volumes I and II, 2nd Ed by Apostol

    - Excellent source unifying mathematical and physical ideas:
        > A Course in Mathematics for Students of Physics,
        > Volumes I and II, by Bamberg and Sternberg,


### Differential Geometry
    - Some books limit themselves to submanifolds of Euclidean
      space. A clear, easy-to-read advanced undergraduate level
      textbook with an introduction to differential manifolds and
      differential forms is
        > Analysis on Manifolds. (Munkres)

    - Slightly dry but also very reliable is
        > Differential Geometry of Curves & Surfaces. (do Carmo)

    - Others develop the theory from a more general point of
      view. For an abstract development one can read
        > Riemannnian Geometry. (do Carmo)

    - Three books that bring physics into the discussion
      explicitly are
        > Topology, Geometry, & Gauge Fields (Naber)

    - a solid mathematical text that gives careful exposition of
      the relevance of the math to the physics;
        > The Geometry of Physics: An Introduction, Frankel

    - a crash course in the advanced mathematics underpinning
      much of modern mathematical physics;
        > Spacetime Geometry and Cosmology. William L. Burke
        - (discusses the geometry of one-forms explicitly)
        - See also: $MY_NOTES/physics/books/Schoenagel-burke-1-forms.txt


### Symplectic Geometry
    - Two senior-to-graduate level books treating all of the
      mathematical topics in these notes are:
        > "An Introduction to Lie Groups and Symplectic Geometry"
        > Bryant, in Geometry & Quantum Field Theory, Freed & Uhlenbeck, AMS

    and
        > Applications of Lie Groups to Differential Equations 2e
        > Olver

    - Slightly more sophisticated are:
        > Introduction to Symplectic Topology.
        > by D. McDuff and D. Salamon

    and
        > "Symplectic Geometry,"
        > by V. I. Arnold and A. B. Givental,
        > in: Encyclopaedia of Mathematical Sciences,
        > Volume 4, V. I. Arnold and S. P. Novikov, eds.

    - Advanced discussions of the symmetries of the two-body
      problem and related topics are given in
        > Global Aspects of Classical Integrable Systems,
        > Cushman and Bates

    and
        > Variations on a Theme of Kepler
        > Guillemin & Sternberg.


### Classical Mechanics
    - Serious students of mechanics should familiarize themselves
      with the modern classics, the easiest of which is
        > Classical Mechanics, by H. Goldstein

    - Readers ready to use more elbow grease will find beautiful,
      modern treatments of the most important topics in
      mathematical mechanics, from basic to esoteric, in:
        > Mathematical Methods of Classical Mechanics. VI Arnold

    - A comprehensive development of the mathematical theory
      underlying mechanics, covering all the topics discussed in
      these lecture notes (and more) in efficient notation is:
        > Foundations of Mechanics (Abraham and Marsden)


### Celestial Mechanics and Gravitation

    - The proofs in Newton's Principia are not easily accessible,
      but the introductory "Definitions" and "Axioms, or Laws of
      Motion," are beautifully clear. A recent translation is:
        > The Principia Isaac Newton
        > I. Bernard Cohen (Trans), Anne Whitman (Trans),

    - The formidable task of reading proofs in Principia has been
      made more pleasant for the modem reader by a commentary:
        > Newton's Principia for the Common Reader
        > (Chandrasekhar)

    - An introductory-level physics treatment of gravitation and
      planetary motion, including an elementary derivation of the
      inverse-square force law from the assumption that orbits
      are circular, and the justification for treating celestial
      bodies like point masses, can be found in Chapter 11 of:
        > Physics, by Alonso and Finn

    - Kepler was an exuberant and unusual character. Arthur
      Koestler has written an amusing and compelling biography
      which portrays a dogged, obsessive quest for geometric
      order in the heavens:
        > The Watershed: A Biography of Johannes Kepler

    - A more technical, yet moving and easy-to-read account of
      Kepler's extraction of his laws from the earthbound data
      collected by Tycho Brahe is available in Chapter 7 of:
        > Project Physics.
        > G. Holton, FJ Rutherford and FG Watson

    - At the advanced undergraduate level, physicists' treatments
      of the two-body problem, Hamiltonians and other related
      problems appear in
        > Newtonian Dynamics
        > R. Baierlin (1983)
    and
        > Classical Dynamics of Particles and Systems.
        > Marion and Thornton (1995)

    - An especially charming book at this level, including an
      ebullient introduction and an annotated list of major
      reference books, including many original sources, is:
        > Adventures in Celestial Mechanics:
        > A First Course in the Theory of Orbits.
        > VG Szebehely (1989)

    - There is even more symmetry in the two-body problem than we
      have described in this book. The associated conserved
      quantities are:
        - the axis and
        - the eccentricity of the ellipse encoded in the
          Runge-Lenz vector.
        - A lovely treatment (in French) is available in:
        > Les origines du calcul symplectique chez Lagrange
        > P. Iglesias, in: Le journal de maths des e/eves,
        > Volume 1 (1995), No.3, pp. 153-161.
