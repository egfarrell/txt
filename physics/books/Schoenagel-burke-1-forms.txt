# William Lewis Burke
    - Review 1: 1980: Spacetime, Geometry, Cosmology ("The 1 forms book")
    - Review 2: 1985: Applied Differential Geometry

Reviewer: G. A. Schoenagel

### Burke's Subtitle (for his masterpiece) : "The 1-Forms Book"

- Having recently revisited Burke's later (1985) book, 'Applied
  Differential Geometry', I was compelled to revisit his
  wonderful, earlier book.

- To a certain extent they're complementary - study both!

- Both are unique, if idiosyncratic. As for this text, Burke
  states: "...this book lies midway between Spacetime Physics
  (Taylor and Wheeler) and Gravitation (Misner,Thorne, Wheeler)."
  That assessment is quite accurate.

- And, of prerequisites, Burke states: "...brief previous
  introduction to Linear Algebra and ordinary differential
  equations.... an introductory physics course and introduction
  to Special Relativity." Again, quite accurate.

    ### Overview
        - Four Chapters: SR, Geometry, Gravitation, Cosmology.
        - Problem Sets End each separate section.
        - Copious Diagrams enliven nearly every page.
        - Page Margins enlivened with additional pithy remarks.

    ### Here is Burke, in his own words:
        - "...the rigid rod plays no part in any of our
          discussions in this book." (Page 39).
        - "...for pedagogical reasons, we'll often consider
          theories that VIOLATE LORENTZ INVARIANCE, as it is
          useful to consider alternatives in order to fully
          understand what SR really means." (p. 50).
        - "The 1-forms at a point are linear approximations to
          functions at that point." (p. 89).
        - "This recognition that Force is a 1-form immediately
          dispels the mystery of the Principle of Virtual
          Work."(p. 97).
        - "We do not add Gravity to our theory, instead we go
          back and build it in right in the foundations." (p.
          193).
        - "Light propagation in this spacetime behaves as if the
          index of refraction were anisotropic. Since spacetime
          distances are measured in terms of light signals, this
          anisotropy appears in our discussion of geometry, and
          leads to the non-euclidean effects that we call
          curvature." (p. 203).
        - "Once one admits a dynamic universe, then a special
          time coordinate is singled out. Use any changing
          property of the universe, and you have a 'clock'." (p.
          246). 
        - And, the final line of the book:
            - "The guess today is that only a fully quantum
              treatment can resolve these questions about these
              singularities."(p. 322).

    ### What of the homework problems ? A few beauties:
        - Give a definition of 4-acceleration analogous to that
          for 4-velocity. (p. 60).
        - Verify Kepler's law for the Earth-Sun system, in
          geometric units. (p. 197).
        - Find the components of the Riemann tensor everywhere on
          a sphere. Use symmetry, not brute force. (p. 235).
        - Invent a spacetime that is homogeneous, but not
          isotropic. (p. 246).
        - If the neutrinos became free particles at an earlier
          redshift than the microwave photons, What is their
          present temperature ? (p. 313).

    - In conclusion, I don't know any physics book which presents
      this material at such an introductory, lucid, level. For
      Physics students, its a gem.

    - You'll delight in perusal of this text: The prose, the
      problem sets, the beautiful diagrams. As an added bonus,
      even the final chapter of Cosmology needs little updating.

    - So, get this book. Devour its contents. A masterpiece in
      its genre. Highly recommended to all students.


### Burke: 1985: Applied Differential Geometry
    - idiosyncratic and inspiring
    - One could consider this 1985 book to be 'Part One' of
      a two-part opus. His earlier text: 'Spacetime, Geometry,
      Cosmology' (1980) is an ideal supplement to this later
      text!
    - That being said, his earlier book is easier than the
      later, but that really depends on one's perspective.
    - This text is a goldmine of thought. From the frontispiece
      we read of Burke's motive for writing: "to all those who,
      like me, have wondered how in hell you can change `dq/dt`
      without changing `q`."
    - Indeed, a question that has bugged me for many a year, not
      least because Goldstein adds little clarity, but that
      professors never feel the need to explain that point!
    - Again, reading Burke: "...helps us to avoid the formal
      symbol manipulations that often lead to the horrible
      calculus of variations manipulations and mistakes in
      Goldstein." (preface).
    - In any event, Burke offers here his perspective on
      a subject which at the time (1985) was unique.
    - The topic: applications of differential forms to
      theoretical physics: dynamics of particles and fields,
      thermodynamics, electrodynamics, SR, GR.
    - Background: undergraduate mathematics curriculum and some
      physics, then the text should be within compass (partial
      derivatives, simple differential equations, a bit of vector
      analysis is sufficient). Diagrams are frequently utilized.
    - Preface: "...often they can be substituted for the verbal
      hints that sometimes constitute a proof." There are
      innovations of pedagogy:

    - (1) Uses of Covariance: here, a one-two-three build-up,
      beginning with Doppler Shift formula beautifully justified.
        - Follow through as, step-by-step, Burke proceeds from
          that shift to aberration to dihedral products to
          spacetime products.

    - (2) Leibniz rule for derivatives of products alongside
      Taylor expansions lead effortlessly to Lie Derivatives. 
        - We read: "...there is a vector concealed in the second
          derivatives..." (p. 69).

    - (3) Remember the rotating disc ? Sometimes used to justify
      going from special to general relativity.
        - Here, Burke shows how the 'velocity-space' is isometric
          to the 3-pseudo-sphere previously defined (p. 75).
        - Reading: "This metric is curved. It is not a spacetime
          metric, however, and its curvature has no more to do
          with Einstein's field equations than does the curvature
          of the velocity-space metric."(p. 77).

    - (4) Bundles are everywhere.
        - You will delight in the discussion of cotangent
          bundles, fiber bundles, tangent bundles.
        - Tour de force is a discussion of thermodynamics.
        - Read: "Thermodynamics is a contact bundle." (p. 109).

    - (5) Calculation of Lie derivative of a vector field is
      approached twice.
        - As Burke writes: "I will first calculate in a totally
          pedestrian manner. A more elegant calculation will then
          follow." (p.s 124-125).

    - (6) Contact Transformations are put to use:
        - transform nonlinear partial differential equation into
          a linear one.
        - Again, beautiful applications to SR (p. 140) and
          thermodynamics (p. 141).

    - (7) The meat of the mathematics is chapter four:
      Differential Forms.
        - Read: "The antisymmetry of forms is important and
          useful." (p. 148) and "Tensors are linear operators."
          (p. 155).
        - We learn of Laplace's equation, linear wave equations,
          where "exterior calculus provides a natural language."
          (p. 166).
        - The highlight of the chapter is the discussion of
          block indices, segue to beautiful exposition of
          determinants.
        - Also, we learn of Weyl's introduction of twisted
          tensors. This leads into the discussion of twisted
          differential forms.
        - Integration (p.s 194-201, section 29), is interesting.
          The discussion concludes with cohomology (too brief,
          I fear). What we learn in chapter four is placed into
          service for chapter five:

    - (8) Applications: A wonderful excursion.
        - It is difficult to overestimate the efficacy of this
          chapter. Burke: "It is a bit of physics lore that to
          every symmetry there corresponds a conservation law and
          vice versa....a conservation law always does lead to
          a potential, however." (p. 264).
        - Concluding the chapter, read: "Use the right tool for
          the job, even if it is encrusted with indices."

    - (9) Electrodynamics
        - Here, `c` is set equal to one and Gaussian units
          utilized.
        - Note: A typo in the last equation, second term, p. 278,
          which is then rectified on next line of p. 279!
        - Burke: "Note how important it is that the inhomogeneous
          equations involve twisted forms." (p. 282).

    - (10) Classical Mechanics. Lagrange and Hamilton.
        - Read: "The first step is to get into the right space."
        - We read: "One goal of this book is to eliminate all
          such funny deltas." That is, compare Burke to
          Goldstein!
        - Note p. 313 and 314, where Burke says: "Goldstein is
          wrong," regards anholonomic constraints.
        - Read: "It is often said that Lagrange's equations are
          `n` second-order equations, whereas Hamilton's
          equations are `2n` first-order equations. This is
          nonsense." (p. 320).
        - The chapter concludes with exposition of wave-packet
          dynamics. More about that can be found in his earlier
          book.

    - (11) I highlight: Calculus on Fiber Bundles, "...covariant
      differentiation ... that name can be misleading." (p. 347).
        - From linear connection to parallel transport, then to
          curvature. All a prelude to the final chapter on
          gravitation.
        - The final two chapters form somewhat of a concerted
          sequence (as with chapters 4 and 5). If the final two
          chapters leave you wanting more, proceed to his
          earlier, even more inspiring, 1980 text.

    - (12) I discovered few typographic errors. None that would
      deter from the efficacy of the book.
        - It is essential the reader read with paper and pencil
          in hand, filling in the intermediate steps. The
          derivations are often left to the reader.

    - A few informative problems for student involvement are
      scattered throughout the prose, examples:
        - "Are there any conformal symmetries of the heat
          equation ? " (p. 213)
        - "What conservation law comes from the field equation
          with source terms ? " (p. 269).
        - "Where is the angular momentum in a circularly
          polarized plane electromagnetic wave ? " (p. 293)
        - "Repeat the discussion of the conducting fluid in
          a magnetic field using differential forms." (p. 304).

    - Burke offers insight on every page: whether a pithy
      statement or well-drawn geometric figure! Highly
      recommended for collateral enrichment.
