### 1. First point: What is this “energy operator” exactly?

Maudlin starts by questioning whether we even know that the CFT’s
energy operator corresponds precisely to a bulk energy operator.

_ In AdS/CFT, we talk loosely about this correspondence: the CFT
  Hamiltonian (which generates boundary time translations) equals
  the ADM energy at infinity in the bulk.
_ But Maudlin notes that in quantum theory there is no “time
  operator,” and the link between energy, Hamiltonian, and time
  translation always depends on a _chosen coordinate system_ or
  _symmetry_.

He’s saying: before we rely on “energy = boundary Hamiltonian,”
we must specify what “time” means in the bulk spacetime, which is
messy if there’s no global time symmetry.

This is a fair point: in evaporating black holes (non-stationary
spacetimes), energy is not globally well-defined. The physicists
often use the asymptotic boundary to define a conserved energy
— but that already assumes a stationary boundary and global
timelike Killing vector, which evaporating spacetimes lack.


### 2. Second point: energy in non-stationary spacetimes

He continues this theme: energy conservation (ADM, Bondi, etc.)
only works in _asymptotically flat_ or _asymptotically AdS_
spacetimes with certain fall-off conditions. He suspects the
evaporating black hole spacetime in Hawking’s diagram doesn’t
meet those conditions.

So his question is: _what exactly is this “energy operator on the
boundary” in such a case?_ If the definitions (ADM or Bondi)
don’t apply, then BHG’s argument might rest on undefined
quantities.

This is a technical but fair criticism: indeed, defining “Bondi
energy” for a fully evaporating black hole in AdS space is
subtle, because the spacetime may not settle into a stationary
configuration at infinity.


### 3. Third point: questioning the “energy conservation”
intuition

Now he turns to the intuitive physical story: Hawking radiation
carries positive energy outward, so the black hole’s mass-energy
inside the horizon must decrease correspondingly — hence the
black hole shrinks.

Maudlin doubts this argument. He says he doesn’t believe one can
rigorously prove that an evaporating black hole _must_ shrink in
this way, though he defers that fight to another paper. For the
sake of argument, he grants it.

So: assume the interior energy goes to zero as the hole
evaporates; fine. Then Σ₂_in (the baby universe) has zero energy.


### 4. Fourth point: “but then what does it mean to consider the
vacuum on Σ₂?”

This is a key critique. BHG’s argument imagined taking the
exterior Σ₂ to be in the _vacuum_ state. Maudlin says: nonsense
— that slice Σ₂ has inherited all the energy from before (since
total energy was conserved). You can’t just call it “vacuum”
without changing the geometry; in GR, the energy content and
geometry are coupled by Einstein’s equations. So the phrase
“vacuum on Σ₂” is incoherent if the slice still contains
gravitational energy.

He’s pointing out an inconsistency: BHG’s hypothetical “take the
vacuum outside plus arbitrary interior state” assumes we can
freeze geometry and vary matter energy independently, which you
cannot do in GR.

That’s a good conceptual catch — although in AdS/CFT, “vacuum”
doesn’t mean literally flat geometry; it means the lowest-energy
configuration consistent with the boundary conditions. So this
might be more a mismatch of language than a real contradiction.


### 5. Fifth point: even granting all that, the “unique vacuum”
idea is questionable

Here Maudlin grants the premises for the sake of argument:
suppose both the outside and the baby universe have zero energy.
Then, yes, you can make many combinations of states that sum to
zero energy.

But he questions whether the _CFT_ dual must be a unique vacuum.
His questions:

_ Do we actually know the dictionary well enough to say that
  a zero-energy bulk state corresponds to a vacuum state on the
  boundary?
_ Maybe “zero energy” in the bulk doesn’t always mean “vacuum” in
  the CFT.
_ Even if the CFT vacuum is unique, why should that force
  uniqueness of zero-energy bulk states?

He then gives a physical reason to doubt uniqueness: in
semiclassical Hawking radiation, energy conservation involves
_negative-energy flux_ falling into the black hole (the so-called
“partner” of the outgoing Hawking radiation). Those
negative-energy excitations can produce many different internal
states whose _total_ energy sums to zero. So, zero-energy bulk
states can be _many_, not one.

Thus, he says, the inference “zero energy ⇒ vacuum ⇒ unique
state” fails in the bulk. The bulk could have many distinct
zero-energy configurations even if the boundary has only one
vacuum.


### 6. How to interpret Maudlin’s reply overall

In essence, Maudlin is pressing a series of skeptical questions:

1. Is the definition of “energy” in AdS/CFT precise enough for
evaporating spacetimes?
2. Are we justified in assuming global energy conservation here?
3. Can we even define a “vacuum” on a dynamic slice?
4. Even if the boundary vacuum is unique, why must the bulk’s
zero-energy states be?
5. Therefore, BHG’s claim that baby universes imply “infinite
zero-energy degeneracy” is far from proven.

This is Maudlin’s style: he doesn’t so much rebut the physical
argument as refuse to let it rest on loosely defined terms. He’s
forcing physicists to clarify the _conceptual underpinnings_ of
their claims — and he’s not wrong that many of those
underpinnings are indeed fuzzy.


### 7. Where physicists would push back

A physicist sympathetic to BHG would reply something like:

_ In AdS/CFT, the “energy operator” _is_ well defined: it’s the
  generator of global time translations at the boundary, and its
  expectation value is given by the Brown–York stress tensor.
_ The “vacuum” means the lowest-energy state consistent with
  those boundary conditions; it’s not literally flat, but it’s
  unique because of supersymmetry and unitarity of the CFT.
_ The evaporating black hole can be treated as a process within
  an overall stationary AdS background, so the total energy (the
  boundary charge) is constant.
_ Negative-energy modes inside the horizon don’t create new
  zero-energy states of the _whole system_; they’re part of
  intermediate configurations, not orthogonal states at zero
  total energy.

So from that point of view, BHG’s objection still stands: the
CFT’s Hilbert space has no room for independent “baby” degrees of
freedom that cost no boundary energy.


### 8. The deeper philosophical divide

This exchange shows the cultural gulf:

_ Physicists (BHG): start from a theory (AdS/CFT) that is trusted
  as exact; use its internal consistency to constrain what’s
  possible.
_ Maudlin (philosopher): start from conceptual clarity; demand
  that every physical term (energy, time, vacuum) be sharply
  defined before drawing conclusions.

They’re both right in their own domain. Maudlin exposes the
handwaving in holographic arguments; physicists point out that if
the duality is correct, those definitions _are_ precise in its
formalism.


So, in short: Maudlin’s reply doesn’t _disprove_ BHG’s point but
_deconstructs_ it. He’s saying: until you show me exactly what
“energy,” “vacuum,” and “uniqueness” mean in this context
— mathematically, not metaphorically — I won’t accept that your
argument “rules out” baby universes.

That’s the 50% you felt you were missing — the other half is that
he’s right that “vacuum” and “energy” are slippery in dynamic GR,
but in AdS/CFT the physicists have more structure than he gives
them credit for.
