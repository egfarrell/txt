- Maudlin’s 2017 paper “(Information) Paradox Lost” argues that
  the so-called black hole information loss paradox never existed
  in the first place. His case is almost mischievously simple
  once you untangle it.

### 01. What the “paradox” is supposed to be

- The usual story goes:
- Hawking’s 1975 work showed that black holes radiate thermally
  (Hawking radiation). If the radiation is purely thermal, it
  carries no detailed information about what fell in.
- When the black hole evaporates completely, the universe is left
  in a mixed thermal state, while it began in a pure quantum
  state. That looks like a `pure → mixed` transition, which
  violates unitarity and therefore information conservation.

- So people concluded either quantum theory fails, or something
  exotic (firewalls, complementarity, wormholes) must fix it.


### 02. Maudlin’s opening move: “There was never any paradox.”

- He claims the entire problem rests on two elementary
  confusions:
    - one mathematical
    - one conceptual
- about how determinism and unitarity work in relativistic
  spacetimes.
- To see why, we need to remember what a Cauchy surface is.
- A Cauchy surface is a spacelike slice of spacetime that every
  inextensible timelike worldline crosses exactly once.
    - Simpler: "A Cauchy surface is a spacelike surface that
      every possible object’s entire worldline intersects once
      and only once."
    - See: $MY_NOTES/physics/spacetime/Cauchy-surface.txt

- In a globally hyperbolic spacetime, giving the complete state
  on one Cauchy surface determines (and is determined by) the
  state on any other — evolution is deterministic and
  retrodictable.
- But if your “surface” isn't Cauchy, you cannot expect
  determinism, unitarity, or information conservation.
    - That’s not a violation of physics; it’s a misuse of the
      concept of “state”.


### 03. Wald’s observation: The “after evaporation” surface isn’t Cauchy

- In Hawking’s Penrose diagram for an evaporating black hole, the
  late-time slice `Σ₂` (after evaporation) does not intersect
  every timelike curve. Worldlines that fell into the singularity
  never hit it.
- Therefore, `Σ₂` is not a Cauchy surface.

- And once that’s noticed, the supposed paradox evaporates.
  There’s never been any reason to expect the state on `Σ₂` to be
  a complete record of the universe.
- QM only guarantees unitarity from Cauchy surface to Cauchy
  surface, not from a Cauchy surface to a non-Cauchy one.

- So “information loss” is just “you looked at an incomplete
  slice”.

### 04. Where the information actually is

- If you want the complete state after evaporation, you must
  include both:
    - `Σ₂(out)`:
        - the slice outside the horizon
        - (where Hawking radiation lives), and
    - `Σ₂(in)`:
        - the slice inside the horizon,
        - up to the evaporation event.

- Together, Σ₂(in) ∪ Σ₂(out) is a full Cauchy surface. The full
  state on that joined surface is unitary, deterministic, and
  retrodictable. The information about what fell in remains
  inside `Σ₂(in)`.

- So nothing is lost. It’s just somewhere you stopped looking.

### 05. But “the black hole no longer exists!”

- People then object: after evaporation, the black hole “no
  longer exists,” so how can information still be “inside” it?

- Maudlin points out that in relativity, statements like “X still
  exists” are tricky. Whether something “still exists” depends on
  your light cone, not on global time slices. The entire
  black-hole interior lies outside your past light cone even
  after the evaporation event, so in a perfectly good
  relativistic sense, it still exists.
- The balloon analogy fails: horizons aren’t material surfaces
  that pop; they are global geometric features of spacetime.


### 06. The topology trick: Cauchy surfaces change shape

- To foliate the evaporating-black-hole spacetime into Cauchy
  surfaces, the slices before evaporation are connected, but
  after the “evaporation event” each slice must split into two
  disconnected pieces (inside and outside).

- Normally, topology changes like that are forbidden by Geroch’s
  theorems—but only for genuine manifolds. At the evaporation
  event the manifold structure breaks down (the horizon shrinks
  to a point), so the theorem’s conditions fail.
- Hence the topology change is legal. No paradox, just a slightly
  weird geometry.


### 07. “External observer” confusion

- The paradox often smuggles in an extra demand: that an external
  observer must, in principle, be able to recover the infalling
  information from the radiation.
- But this is never a general requirement of physics.
- QM does not promise that information about a system’s initial
  conditions can be reconstructed from partial observations. The
  observer’s epistemic limitations are not the universe’s
  problem.


### 08. Evaporation event versions

- There are two ways to model the “evaporation event”:
    - EE-in:
        - Keep the evaporation event as a real (though
          non-manifold) point in spacetime. Then the foliation
          into Cauchy surfaces works fine.
    - EE-out:
        - Delete the point entirely. That makes the spacetime no
          longer globally hyperbolic (no Cauchy surfaces at all).
          Then unitarity and determinism don’t even apply, so
          again no paradox—just a different geometry.

Either way, there’s no fundamental violation.


### 09. The “not enough room” objection

- Some argue that as the black hole shrinks, there isn’t enough
  volume to store the information inside. Maudlin dissects that
  too:
    - “Information” doesn’t have a definite spatial location.
      (Think of an entangled pair: neither particle alone “has”
      the information.)
    - The interior’s spatial volume is not uniquely defined in
      curved spacetime.
    - And, in any deterministic theory, all Cauchy surfaces
      contain the same total information, regardless of their
      shape or size. So volume doesn’t bound information in any
      meaningful way.

### 10. The moral

- The “paradox” vanishes once you respect the distinction between
  Cauchy and non-Cauchy surfaces and stop treating partial data
  as complete. QM plus GR already contain the resources to make
  sense of evaporating black holes.

- In Maudlin’s memorable finale: if a paper treats a non-Cauchy
  slice as if it were Cauchy, “commit it to the flames; for it
  can contain nothing but sophistry and illusion.” (Hume
  reference)
