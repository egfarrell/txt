# Introduction: The winding Road to Quantum Gravty
- by Abhay Ashtekar
- (January 1990)

> Traveler, there are no paths; Paths are made by walking.
> Antonio Machado
    - Hmmm, similar to:
    - Hans Urs von Balthasar's “Holy Saturday” theology
        - Easter: He Walked Where There Was No Path

- GR and quantum theory are among the greatest intellectual
  achievements of this century. Each has profoundly altered our
  conceptual understanding of the physical world.
    - Furthermore, each has successfully described the physical
      phenomena in its own domain to an astonishing degree of
      accuracy. And yet, they offer strikingly different pictures
      of physical reality.

- Indeed, at first one is surprised that physics could keep
  progressing in the face of so deep a conflict. The reason of
  course is the "accidental" fact that the fundamental constants
  in our universe conspire to make the Planck length so small and
  Planck energy so high compared to laboratory scales.

- Because of this we happily maintain a schizophrenic attitude,
  using the precise, geometric picture offered by GR for cosmo
  and astro phenomena, and the QM world of chance and intrinsic
  uncertainties when dealing with atomic and subatomic particles.

- Although this strategy is appropriate for practical purposes,
  it is highly unsatisfactory from a conceptual viewpoint.
  Everything from our previous physics experience indicates that
  both pictures must be approximations; special cases that arise
  as appropriate limits from a single, universal theory.

- That theory must therefore represent a synthesis of GR and QM.
  This would be the quantum theory of gravity.
    - Not only should it correctly describe all known physical
      phenomena, but it should also adequately handle the Planck
      regime.
    - This is the future theory that we invoke when faced with
      phenomena, such as the big bang and the final state of
      black holes, where the worlds of GR and QM must unavoidably
      meet.

- Research in QG dates back at least to the thirties when the
  necessity of _quantizing the gravitational field_ was first
  analyzed in some detaiL

- However, QG really only "took off" in the late fifties. The
  general developments since then loosely represent THREE STAGES,
  each spanning roughly a decade. The purpose of this chapter is
  to present a broad sketch of these developments.

### First stage

- Firstly, there was EXPLORATION. The goal was to treat gravity
  like any other physical field. The EM field had been
  successfully quantized using two approaches:

    - _CANONICAL_ approach:
    - electric and magnetic fields obeying Heisenberg's
      uncertainty princIple are at the forefront, and quantum
      states arise naturally as gauge-invariant functionals of
      the vector potential on a spatial three-slice.
        - Another view:
        - $MY_BOOKS/Physics/QG/Rovelli-Brief-History-QG.pdf
        - The canonical line of research is the attempt to
          construct a quantum the- ory in which the Hilbert space
          carries a representation of the operators corresponding
          to the full metric, or some functions of the metric,
          without background metric to be fixed. The program was
          set by Bergmann and Dirac in the fifties. Unraveling
          the canonical structure of GR turned out to be
          laborious. Bergmann and his group, Dirac, Peres,
          Arnowit Deser and Misner completed the task in the late
          fifties and early sixties. The for- mal equations of
          the quantum theory were then written down by Wheeler
          and DeWitt in the middle sixties, but turned out to be
          too ill-defined. A well defined version of the same
          equations was successfully found only in the late
          eighties, with loop quantum gravity


    - _COVARIANT_ APPROACH:
    - one first isolates and then quantizes the two radiative
      modes of the Maxwell field in space-time, without carrying
      out a (3+1)-decomposition, and the quantum states naturally
      arise as elements of the Fock space of photons.
        - NOTE: The term "covariant" is thus somewhat misleading.
          It may nonetheless be used to distinguish this approach
          from the canonical method since it does not involve
          a (3+1)-decomposition of space-time.
        - Another view:
            - $MY_BOOKS/Physics/QG/Rovelli-Brief-History-QG.pdf
            - The covariant line of research is the attempt to
              build the theory as a QFT of the fluctuations of
              the metric over a flat Minkowski space, or some
              other background metric space. The program was
              started by Rosenfeld, Fierz and Pauli in the
              thirties. The Feynman rules of GR were laboriously
              found by DeWitt and Feynman in the 60s. t’Hooft and
              Veltman, Deser and Van Nieuwenhuizen, found firm
              evidence of non-renormalizability in the 70s. Then,
              a search for an extension of GR giving
              a renormalizable or finite perturbation expansion
              started. Through high derivative theory and
              supergravity, the search converged successfully to
              string theory in the late eighties.

- Attempts were made to extend these techniques to GR. In the EM
  case the two methods are completely equivalent. There is only
  a change of emphasis when going from one to the other.

- However in the gravitational case, the difference is profound.
  This is not accidental. The reason is rooted in one of the
  essential features of GR:
    - namely the dual role of the space-time _metric_.

- To appreciate this let us begin with field theories in
  Minkowski space-time, say Maxwell's theory.
    - Here, the basic dynamical field is represented by a tensor
      field `F_μν` on Minkowski space.
    - The space-time geometry provides the kinematical arena on
      which the field propagates. The background, Minkowskian
      metric provides the light cones and the notion of
      causality.
    - We can foliate this space-time by a one-parameter family of
      space-like three-planes, and analyze how the values of the
      electric and magnetic fields on one of these surfaces
      determine those on any other surface.
    - The isometries of the Minkowski metric let us construct
      physical quantities carried by EM waves such as fluxes of
      energy, momentum, and angular momentum.

- In GR, by contrast, there's no background geometry. The
  space-time metric itself is the fundamental dynamical variable.

- On the one hand, it's analogous to the Minkowski metric in
  Maxwell's theory;
    - it determines space-time geometry, provides light cones,
      defines causality, and dictates the propagation of all
      physical fields (including itself).

- On the other hand, it is the analog of the _Newtonian
  gravitational potential_, and therefore the basic dynamical
  entity of the theory, similar, in this respect, to the `F_μν`
  of Maxwell's theory.

- This dual role of the metric is a precise statement of the
  heart of GR:
    - the _equivalence principle_.
    - It is this feature that is largely responsible for the
      powerful conceptual economy of GR, its elegance and
      aesthetic beauty.
    - However, this feature also brings many problems with it.
        - We can already see in the classical theory several
          manifestations of them.
        - Eg, it is because of the absence of a background
          geometry that it is so difficult to analyze
          singularities of the theory and to define the energy
          and momentum carried by gravitational waves.
    - Since there is no 'a-priori' space-time, to introduce basic
      notions such as causality, time, and evolution, one must
      first solve dynamical equations and construct a space-time.
    - As an extreme example, consider black holes, whose
      definition requires the knowledge of the causal structure
      of the ENTIRE space-time.
        - To calculate if the given initial conditions lead to
          the formation of a black hole, one must first obtain
          their maximal evolution, and, using the causal
          structure detennined by _that_ solution, ask if its
          future infinity has a past boundary. (???)

- Because there is no longer a clean separation between the
  kinematical arena and the dynamics, substantial care is
  therefore already needed in the classical theory when
  formulating even basic physical questions.

- In QM the problems become even more serious.
    - Recall that because of the uncertainty principle, already
      in nonrelativistic QM, particles don't have well-defined
      trajectories;
    - time-evolution only produces a probability amplitude `Ψ(x,
      t)`, rather than a specific trajectory, `x(t)`.

- Similarly, in QG, even after evolving an initial state, one
  would not be left with a specific spacetime.

- In the absence of a space-time geometry, how is one to
  introduce habitual physical notions such as causality, time,
  scattering states, and black holes?

- So the canonical and the covariant approaches have adopted
  dramatically different attitudes to face these problems.

- In the CANONICAL approach, one notices that, in spite of the
  above conceptual difficulties, the Hamiltonian formulation of
  GR is well-defined, and one attempts to use it as a stepping
  stone to canonical quantization.
    - One wants to construct the mathematical framework first and
      then use it to suggest a physical interpretation.
    - The fundamental canonical commutation relations are to lead
      us to the physical uncertainty principle.
    - The MOTION generated by the Hamiltonian is thought of as
      TIME EVOLUTION.
    - The fact that certain operators on the fixed ("spatial")
      three-manifold commute is supposed to capture the
      appropriate notion of causality.
    - The emphasis is on preserving the geometrical character of
      GR, retaining the beautiful fusion of gravity and geometry
      that Einstein created.
    - In the 60s, the Hamiltonian formulation of the classical
      theory was worked out in detail, and a precise quantization
      program was laid down.
        - However, this path which was so successful in particle
          physics, meandered away from the mainstream QFT
          developments.
    - In the canonical program, it's hard to see how gravitons
      could emerge, how scattering matrices are to be computed,
      how Feynman diagrams are to dictate dynamics and how
      virtual processes are to give radiative corrections.
    - To use a well known phrase, the emphasis on geometry of the
      canonical program "drove a wedge between GR and the theory
      of elementary particles."
    - More importantly, due to difficult technical problems, many
      internal goals of program itself remained unattained. The
      quantum version of Einstein's equations turned out to be
      too hard to solve exactly.
    - Since no perturbation expansion preserving the spirit of
      the program was available, much of the discussion remained
      rather fonnal, and most of the conceptual problems of QG
      remained unsettled.

    - Summary:
    - Uses Hamiltonian GR to quantize geometry while preserving
      Einstein’s spacetime fusion.
    - Builds a formal framework: canonical commutators define
      uncertainty; Hamiltonian motion gives time evolution.
    - Commuting spatial operators encode causality.
    - Though developed in the 1960s, it diverged from QFT,
      failing to recover gravitons or scattering physics.
    - Technical barriers left quantum Einstein equations unsolved
      and the program largely formal.


- In the COVARIANT approach the emphasis is just the opposite.
  Field-theoretic techniques are to be applied even if it means
  sacrificing the geometric beauty of GR. Indeed, even the
  dynamics given by Einstein's equations are not sacrosanct.

    - The first step, is to split the spacetime metric `g_μν`
      into two parts:
        - `g_μν = η_μν + G∙h_μν`
        - `η_μν` is a background, kinematical metric (often flat)
        - `G` = Newton's constant
        - `h_μν` = the deviation of the physical metric from the
          chosen background, the dynamical field.

    - The two roles of the metric tensor are now split.
        - With splitting, most of the previous conceptual
          problems seem to melt away.
        - Only `h_μν` is quantized when transitioning to quantum
          theory. The quanta of this field propagate on the
          classical background spacetime with metric `g_μν`.

    - If the background is in fact chosen to be flat, one can use
      the Casimir operators of the Poincare group to show that
      the quanta have spin two and rest mass zero. These are the
      gravitons.

    - The Einstein-Hilbert Lagrangian tells us how they interact
      with one another. Thus, in this program, quantum GR was
      first reduced to another QFT in Minkowski space.

    - One could apply to it all the machinery that had been so
      successful in particle physics. Now we had a definite
      program to compute amplitudes for various scattering
      processes. Unruly gravity appeared to be tamed and forced
      into the mould created for the other forces of nature.

    - It was in the 60s that this procedure was first spelled out
      in detail. The quantum effects of gravity could now be
      visualized vividly in terms of exchanges of gravitons.
        - As in other field theories, calculations of physical
          amplitudes could be carried out using Feynman diagrams.
        - Consistent Feynman rules for computing
          graviton-graviton interactions were discovered.
        - Differences between abelian and nonabelian couplings
          were noted.
        - Covariant QG was shown to be perturbatively unitary.
        - Thus, by the early 70s, the covariant approach had led
          to several concrete results.

### Second Stage

- We now come to the second stage of development.
    - This was marked by enthusiasm and hope.
    - Motto: Go forth, perturb, and expand!

- The enthusiasm was first generated by the discovery that
  Yang-Mills theory coupled to fermions is renonnalizable if the
  masses of gauge particles arise from a spontaneous
  symmetry-breaking mechanism.
    - This led to a successful theory of electroweak
      interactions. Physics witnessed a renaissance of QFT.

- The enthusiasm spilled over to gravity; it reenforced the hope
  _perturbative methods_ would be applicable to quantum GR too.

- Consequently, much 70s gravity research was within the
  covariant approach. Courageous calculations were perfonned to
  estimate radiative corrections.

- However, research soon ran into its first road block.
- Detailed calculations revealed GR IS PERTURBATIVELY
  NON-RENORMALIZABLE at two loops.

- To appreciate the significance of this result, let us return to
  the quantum theory of photons and electrons.
    - This theory is perturbatively `renorrnalizable`.
    - Although individual terms in the perturbation expansion of
      a physical amplitude may diverge due to radiative
      corrections involving closed loops of virtual particles,
      these infinities are of a specific type;
        - they can be systematically absorbed in the values of
          free parameters of the theory, the fine structure
          constant and the electron mass.

- Thus, by renonnalizing these parameters, individual terms in
  the perturbation series can be systematically rendered finite.
    - (However, the series when summed almost certainly diverges,
      a point that will become relevant later)

- In quantum GR, such a systematic procedure is not available;
    - infinities that arise due to radiative corrections are
      genuinely troublesome.
    - Put differently, when one includes Feynman diagrams with
      two or more loops of gravitons and attempts to extract
      finite answers, the quantum theory acquires an infinite
      number of underdetermined parameters.

- It therefore has no predictive power at all!
    - However, buoyed by the success of perturbative methods in
      electro-weak interactions, the community was reluctant to
      give up in the gravitational case.

    - In the case of weak interactions, it was known that the
      observed low-energy phenomena could be explained using
      Fermi's simple four-point interaction.
        - The problem was this Fermi model led to
          a non-renonnalizable theory.

    - The new, renormalizable theory of weak interactions
      referred to above does reduce to the Fermi model in the
      low-energy regime, but differs from it at a fundamental
      level.
        - It marshals new processes that are significant only at
          high energies, but which cure the problem of
          non-renormalizability.
        - It was natural to look for similar processes in the
          gravitational case.

- This led to higher-derivative theories, theories in which the
  Lagrangian contains curvaturee-squared terms with appropriate
  coupling constants, in addition to the Einstein-Hilbert term.
    - The new terms play a significant role only at high
      energies, and leave the low-energy or long-distance
      behavior of the theory unaltered.
    - Thus, in the classical, macroscopic regime, the new theory
      would basically reproduce GR.
    - For appropriate choices of coupling constants, the theory
      does become renonnalizable. In fact the theory is
      asymptotically free; at high energies, it behaves as if it
      were a free field theory.

    - However, it fails to be `unitary` unfortunately!

    - In the perturbative treatment, the Hamiltonian is unbounded
      from below, signaling a dramatic instability.
    - Thus the idea of mimicking the theory of weak interactions
      finally fails, although it was promising at first.

- So far, these approaches generally considered the gravitational
  field in isolation. But the unification of the electroweak
  interactions led to another suggestion:
    - Perhaps a viable theory would result _only_ when gravity is
      coupled to suitably chosen matter fields.

- The most striking example of this is probably `supergravity`.
    - The hope was that the infinities of the bosonic fields,
      including gravity, would be cancelled by those of the
      fermionic fields, giving a renonnalizable theory of gravity
      interacting vith matter.
    - Much effort went into the most sophisticated version of the
      theory, `N=8 supergravity` in the hope it could be employed
      as a genuine GUT.

- It turns that some infinity cancellations do occur, and that
  supergravity is indeed better behaved than GR coupled to matter
  the standard way.
    - Furthermore, the Hamiltonian is manifestly positive and the
      theory is unitary. Unfortunately, the theory again turns
      out to be non-renormalizable.

- Thus unlike QFT in curved spaces, the results regarding gravity
  in the 70s and early 80s were mainly negative in character,
  albeit important.

- Perturbative methods revive once again in the 80s, due
  to the renaissance of string theory.
    - In string theory, one abandons point particles and the
      corresponding local fields and considers extended objects
      (strings) as fundamental.

- It is remarkable, however, that the familiar physical
  particles, including the spin two, rest mass zero graviton, do
  in fact arise naturally in the theory as various components of
  string excitations.
    - What's more, the theory only has _one free parameter_.(??!)
    - All couplings are therefore fixed automatically; very
      little is fed in from the outside.

- Consequently, hope was expressed that this theory would
  incorporate all the information about the physical world; it
  would be a TOE.

- Technical successes of the theory generated a great deal of
  enthusiasm. The theory is unitary and, although a conclusive
  proof is yet to be published, the general consensus among
  experts is the theory is finite, not just renonnalizable
  order-by-order perturbation theory.

- Unfortunately, however, when summed, the series diverges and
  does so uncontrollably. At first One might wonder if this
  should be regarded as a serious failure of the theory. After
  all, even in QED the series is believed to diverge.

- However, QED is an inherently incomplete theory. It ignores
  many processes that must come into play at high energies or
  short distances.
    - In particular, it completely ignores the microstructure of
      space-time, and simply assumes that space-time can be
      approximated by the smooth continuum of Minkowski space at
      arbitrarily small distances.
    - Therefore, it can blame the divergence of its perturbation
      series on all these effects that lie outside its realm.

- A TOE on the other hand, cannot escape so easily. It must face
  the energy regime squarely, including the complications at the
  Planck scale.
    - It must incorporate all these effects. It cannot plead
      ignorance and shift the burden of infinities to yet another
      theory.

### Third Stage

- Thus, we have now entered the THIRD stage of development.
    - The mood is markedly different. The heady enthusiasm gave
      way to deeper reflection.
    - The conceptual questions of the 60s are again a focal
      point. The key lesson from the second stage is that
      perturbative, field-theoretic methods are not viable QG.

- The assumption that space-time can be represented by a SMOOTH
  CONTINUUM at arbitrarily small length scales simply leads to
  inconsistencies.
    - We can neither ignore the microstructure of space-time nor
      presuppose its nature. We must let QG unravel it.

- Irrespective of whether we prefer strings or local fields, GR
  or higher derivative theories, we must face the problem of
  quantization _NONPERTURBATIVELY_.

- As a well-known poetic expression goes, if we begin by
  "steam-rollering" space-time into flatness and linearity, we
  will learn nothing by "waving the magic wand of quantum theory
  over the resulting corpse."

- Once again the orientation of research has shifted.
  Nonperturbative ideas are being explored in string theory.
    - For, in spite of the divergence of the perturbation series,
      it may well exist as an exact theory.

- Furthermore, nonperturbative effects may be crucial in removing
  the current ambiguities, particularly in the reduction of the
  theory to four space-time dimensions.
    - In quantum GR itself, the canonical program has been
      revived using new technical and conceptual tools.
        - Some exact solutions to the quantum Einstein equations
          have been obtained.
        - These have given us an inkling as to how different the
          true microstructure of space-time is likely to be from
          a smooth continuum.

- Furthermore, gravity is no longer treated separately, divorced
  from particle pllysics. A unified mathematical framework seems
  to bind it to other basic interactions.
    - At the end of the 70s, the path-integral or
      sum-over-histories was launched using Riemannian metrics
      (ie, positive-definite).
    - The program is being vigorously applied to minisuperspaces
      that arise in cosmology. Conceptual questions of cosmology
      are being analysed using fresh ideas.
    - Finally, new numerical initiatives have been launched to
      test if GR can exist nonperturbatively as a field theory.

- Had perturbative methods worked, we'd have been left with
  a rather simple picture space-time, and many of the conceptual
  problems that arise from the dual role of the metric would have
  simply disappeared, or been simplified enormously.

- Now we _must_ face those questions seriously.

- Is it appropriate in QG to work with a fixed space-time
  manifold?
    - Shouldn't the topology also be subject to quantum
      fluctuations?
    - Is there then a _quantum theory of topology_?
    - Can the topological fluctuations representing worm holes be
      responsible for the laboratory values of coupling
      constants?
    - In particular, do they force the cosmological constant to
      vanish?

- Another set of questions concerns a quantum theory of the
  universe as a whole. Is such a program viable or even
  meaningful?
    - These questions have revived interest in quantum
      measurement theory.
    - Is it possible that some aspect of QG is in fact
      responsible for the "collapse of the wave function", the
      key ingredient of the Copenhagen interpretation?

- A related question is: Why does the world around us look so
  classical? Is it because of some special initial conditions?
    - Normally, in physics, initial conditions are simply
      supplied externally.
    - However, we are now attempting to construct a "complete"
      theory.
    - Should there be a place in it for a THEORY OF INITIAL
      CONDITIONS?
    - Are there laws that govern the initial state of the
      universe? The final state of an evaporating black hole?
    - What is the role of time in QG?
    - Is the unitarity of evolution that we take for granted in
      QM exact, or can small deviations occur due to QG effects?
      If so, how small?

- Finally, most of the work to-date assumes the rules of QM are
  to remain intact in its unification with GR.
    - Why should this be?
    - Isn't there a need to generalize the basic framework of QM
      itself to accommodate the absence of a background
      space-time?

- One cannot help but be struck by how qualitatively different
  these questions are compared to those pursued in the 70s.
    - In retrospect, it appears that a complacent attitude
      towards such issues had then developed in the false
      security of perturbative methods.

- Indeed, in most circles it wasn't even respectable to show
  one's unease with quantum measurement theory, or suggest that
  rules of QM aren't absolutely sacrosanct.
    - The 80s brought a growing realization that one cannot
      escape from such issues indefinitely.
    - The goal of this volume is to summarize the current status
      of these conceptual problems of QG.
