# Einstein and Quantum Mechanics (John Stachel)

### Some lessons from GR (p. 32)
    - Lesson 1:
        - don't break asunder what G-d has put together:
        - inertia and gravitation
    - The first lesson Einstein learnt is the fundamental
      importance in any gravitational theory of the equivalence
      principle, which he expressed as inertia and gravitation
      being 'wesensgleich', the same in essence, or essentially
      _the same_.
    - There is no absolute DISTINCTION between gravitation and
      inertia, but it depends on the _frame of reference_
      employed.
    - Once the appropriate mathematical tool had been developed,
      this lesson can be expressed by saying the affine
      connection of spacetime cannot be split-up uniquely into
      two parts,
        - one of which is an `inertial` connection,
        - the other a `gravitational` field tensor
        - (remember, the difference between two symmetric
          connection fields is a tensor field).
    - Till the end of his life, Einstein stressed the
      significance of `affine connection` as the correct
      mathematical representation of the gravitational-cum
      -inertial field.
    - [Interesting discussion then about the importance of the
      affine connection, more than the Riemann tensor...]

    - Lesson 2
    - This involves the meaning of 'general covariance'.
    - Einstein's interpretation of this concept is connected to
      the reason why 3 years elapsed between him deciding the
      metric tensor was the correct mathematical representation
      of gravitation (late 1912) and finally adopted the
      generally covariant equations for the gravitational field,
      now called the Einstein equations (late 1915).
        - The main obstacle was his failure to realize that in
          a generally covariant theory, although the points of
          a manifold are mathematically individuated by
          a coordinate system, they are NOT PHYSICALLY
          INDIVIDUATED before/until one has a metric tensor field
          on a manifold. (to use modern terminology)

    - Lesson 3
    - Originally, Einstein had considered the second solution to
      be physically distinct from the first, and hence rejected
      the generally covariant field equations because they didn't
      provide a physically unique solution for the gravitational
      field corresponding to a given source distribution (for
      more discussion of the "hole" argument see Stachel 1986b,
      1987~,1989)
    - After two years he finally realized that two such solutions
      could be considered as two mathematically distinct
      exemplifications of the _same physical solution_ if one
      stipulated that the physical properties of the manifold
      points inside the hole in any solution to the field
      equation depend entirely on the nature of the solution;
      hence, when one creates the second solution by "dragging",
      the physical significance of the points is dragged along
      with the field.
        - It is this stipulation that constitutes the physical
          meaning of the concept of general covariance, which,
          properly speaking, has _nothing to do with coordinate
          transformation_.
    - In the case of GR, one can express this idea by saying:
      Until one introduces a metric, a manifold does not consist
      of physical events but only of mathematical points with no
      physical properties;
        - it is the _metric that imparts the physical character_
          of events to the points of a manifold.


    - Lesson 3 'summary'
        - After two years he recognized that two mathematically
          distinct solutions can represent the same physical
          reality if one assumes that the physical properties of
          manifold points inside the hole depend entirely on the
          field itself.
        - When a new solution is created by dragging, the
          physical meaning of those points is dragged with the
          field.
        - This assumption gives general covariance its physical
          significance, independent of any coordinate
          transformation. In GR, a manifold has only abstract
          points until a metric is introduced; the metric gives
          those points their physical character as events.


    - Advantages of the fibre bundle:
        - $MY_NOTES/maths/geometry/guage-fibre-fiber-bundles.txt
    - One moral of the story is that GR is better formulated
      mathematically by a fiber bundle approach in which the
      metric is represented by a cross section of the bundle.
        - On doing this, you realize immediately that in the
          bundle approach, the points of the base manifold do not
          represent physical events, which instead are
          represented by _mappings_ from a point on a cross
          section of the bundle into a point of the base
          manifold.
        - If you have no cross section (no metric), you have no
          such mapping and hence no events (see Stachel 1986b for
          more details of this approach).
        - It follows that any point of the base manifold can
          "represent" any event in an appropriate MAPPING.

    - No metric, no nothing
        - Finally, there is a issue which I call the "no metric,
          no nothing" point of view.
        - To quote Einstein: On the basis of GR, the concept of
          SPACE, as opposed to 'what fills space' ... has no
          separate existence.
        - If we imagine the gravitational field, ie, the
          functions `gik` to be removed, there does not remain
          a space of the type [of Minkowski space in special
          relativity-JS] but absolutely nothing, not even
          a 'topological space' [ie, a manifold'-JS].
        - For the functions describe not only the field but
          simultaneously the topological metrical structural
          properties of the manifold ...
    - There is no such thing as empty space, i.e., a space
      without a field. Space-time does not claim existence on its
      own, but only as a structural quality of the field
      (Einstein 1952).
    - Einstein was well aware his views on this question were far
      from universally shared:
        - It required a severe struggle to develop the concept of
          independent and absolute space, indispensable for the
          development of theory.
        - It has required no less stenuous exertions to
          subsequently overcome this concept, a process which is
          not yet completed (Einstein 1954b)

    - Global Manifold & tension with QM
        - Although the two ideas are not identical, the moral
          I want to draw from this final point may be related to
          Chris Isham's work .
        - Suppose you really take seriously the view that there
          is something _fundamentally local_ about the way GR
          approaches a prob1em.
            - Then there is another fundamental tension between
              the basic approaches of GR and of QM because in
              a deep sense QM is fundamentally global its
              approach to problems.
        - It doesn't make sense to talk about the wave function
          on one patch of space-time, or the sum over all paths
          on one patch of space-time.
            - In solving a QM problem, you have to consider the
              whole manifold from the beginning. 
            - The conventional mathematical approach to GR, which
              starts with a manifold, masks this tension.
        - If we develop a mathematical formulation of GR that
          _emphasizes the element of locality_ from the
          beginning, it would emphasize this contrast more
          sharply.
        - Such an emphasis on the tension may be a necessary
          stage in finding its ultimate resolution.
        - Indeed, this way of thinking also suggests new
          possibilities. Perhaps the existence of a global
          manifold is just a special case.
            - Maybe the ultimate theory all the patches won't
              always fit together to form a manifold, except in
              the classical limit.
        - Perhaps my ideas may connect here with Isham's. If you
          don't build a global manifold at the beginning, maybe
          you're better off, because you may have to get rid of
          it in the end anyway.
