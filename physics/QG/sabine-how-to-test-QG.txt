# How to test quantum gravity

- Today I want to talk about a topic that most physicists get
  wrong: How to test QG. Most physicists believe it's just is not
  possible. But it is possible.

- Einstein’s GR tells us that gravity is due to the curvature of
  space and time. But this theory is wrong, strictly speaking. It
  is wrong because according to GR, gravity does not have quantum
  properties. This lack of quantum behavior in gravity gives rise
  to mathematical inconsistencies that make no physical sense. 
    - To really make sense of gravity, we need a theory of QG.
      But we don't have such a theory yet. In this video, we'll
      look at the experimental possibilities that we have to find
      the missing theory.

- But before I do that, I want to tell you why so many physicists
  think it's not possible to test QG.

- The reason is gravity is a very weak force and its quantum
  effects are even weaker. Gravity does not seem weak in everyday
  life. But that's because gravity does not neutralize, unlike
  all the other fundamental forces. So, on long distances, it is
  the only remaining force and that’s why we notice it so
  prominently. But if you look at the gravitational force between
  an electron and proton for example, and the electromagnetic
  force between them, then the electromagnetic force is a factor
  10^40 stronger.

- One way to see what this means is to look at a fridge magnet.
  The magnetic force of that tiny thing is stronger than the
  gravitational pull of the whole planet.

- Now, in most approaches to QG, the gravitational force is
  mediated by a particle. This particle is called the graviton,
  and it belongs to the gravitational force the same way that the
  photon belongs to the electromagnetic force. But since gravity
  is so much weaker than the EM force, you need ridiculously high
  energies to produce a measureable amount of gravitons. With the
  currently available technology, it would take a particle
  accelerator about the size of the Milky Way to reach
  sufficiently high energies.

- And this is why most physicists think that one cannot test
  QG. It is testable in principle, all right, but
  not in practice, because one needs these ridiculously large
  accelerators or detectors.

- However, this argument is wrong. It is wrong because one does
  not need to produce a quantum of a field to demonstrate that
  the field must be quantized. Take EM as an example. We have
  evidence that it must be quantized right here. Because if it
  was not quantized, then atoms would not be stable. Somewhat
  more quantitatively, the discrete energy levels of atomic
  spectral lines demonstrate that EM is quantized. And you don't
  need to detect individual photons for that.

- With the quantization of gravity, it’s somewhat more difficult,
  but not impossible. The advantage of gravity is that it becomes
  stronger for larger systems. That's because gravity doesn't
  neutralize, unlike the other forces, and therefore adds up. So,
  we can make quantum gravitational effects stronger by just
  taking `larger masses` and bringing them into quantum states.
  For example, into a state where the masses are in two places at
  once. We should then be able to tell whether the gravitational
  field is also in two places at once. And if we can do that, we
  can demonstrate that gravity has quantum behavior.

- But the trouble is quantum effects for large objects quickly
  fade away, or “decohere” as the physicists say. 
    - So the challenge to measuring QG comes down to producing
      and maintaining quantum states of heavy objects. 
    - “Heavy” here means something like a milligram. That doesn’t
      sound heavy, but it is very heavy compared to the masses of
      elementary particles.

- The objects you need for such an experiment have to be heavy
  enough so that one can actually measure the gravitational
  field. There are a few experiments attempting to measure this.
  But presently the masses that one can bring into quantum states
  are not quite high enough. However, it's something that will
  reasonably become possible in coming decades.

- Another good chance to observe quantum gravitational effects is
  to use the universe itself as a laboratory. Quantum
  gravitational effects should be strong right after the big bang
  and inside of black holes. 
    - Evidence from the early universe could still be around
      today, e.g. the cosmic microwave background. Indeed,
      several groups are trying to discover if the cosmic
      microwave background can be analyzed to show that gravity
      must have been quantized. But at least for now the signal
      is well below measurement precision.

- With black holes, it’s more complicated, because the region
  where QG is strong is hidden behind the event horizon. But some
  `computer simulations` seem to show that stars can collapse
  `without forming a horizon`. 
    - In this case we could look right at the quantum
      gravitational effects. 
    - The challenge with this idea is to find out just how the
      observations would differ between a “normal” black hole and
      a singularity without a horizon but with quantum
      gravitational effects. Again, that’s subject of current
      research.

- And there are other options. For example, the theory of QG may
  violate symmetries that are respected by GR. Symmetry
  violations can show up in high-precision measurements at low
  energies, even if they are very small. This is something that
  one can look for with particle decays or particle interactions
  and indeed something that various experimental groups are
  looking for.

- There are several other ways to test QG, but these are more
  speculative in that they look for properties that a theory of
  QG may not have.

- For example, the way in which gravitational waves are emitted
  in a black hole merger is different if the black hole horizon
  has quantum effects. However, this may just not be the case.

- The same goes for ideas that space itself may have the
  properties of a medium give rise to `dispersion`, which means
  that light of different colors travels at different speed, or
  may have viscosity. 
    - Again, this is something that one can look for, and that
      physicists are looking for. It’s not our best shot though,
      because QG may not give rise to these effects.

- In any case, you can see it's clearly possible to test QG.
- Indeed I think it is possible that we'll see experimental
  evidence for QG in the next 20 years, most likely by the type
  of test that I talked about first, with the massive quantum
  objects.
