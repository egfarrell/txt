A bound system (like a nucleus, or like atoms forming a molecule)

To get from the higher-energy (separate nucleons) state to the

That "somewhere" is radiation, kinetic energy of emitted

So the system gives off energy as it settles into its

###  Binding vs. Unbinding Energy

* When we say **“binding energy”**, we usually mean the amount of energy released when the nucleus is formed from its separate nucleons.
* But by conservation of energy, that’s *also exactly the amount of energy you’d need to supply to break (unbind) the nucleus back into its nucleons.*

So yes — the "binding energy" can be thought of as either:

* the **release energy** (when binding happens), or
* the **required energy** (to unbind).

That’s why it sometimes feels like a slightly confusing phrase — but it’s really just one number describing how tightly the nucleus holds itself together.

### 🔹 What the book says

> *“The missing mass is converted into the energy required to hold the nucleons together – that is, it becomes the ‘binding energy’.”*

At first glance this sounds like the nucleus is somehow “paying an energy bill” to glue itself together. But that’s misleading.

---

### 🔹 What actually happens

* When nucleons come close, the **strong nuclear force** lowers the system’s potential energy.
* The system falls into a *more stable, lower-energy* state (the bound nucleus).
* The excess energy is **released** (as gamma rays, kinetic energy, etc.).

So the **mass defect** is energy that has *left* the system. The bound nucleus has *less mass-energy* than the free nucleons.

That’s why to break the nucleus apart again, you must **supply** exactly the same amount of energy.

---

### 🔹 Why the phrasing is sloppy

Saying *“energy is required to hold the nucleons together”* makes it sound like you must constantly feed in energy, like running an engine to keep it from falling apart. That’s not right — the strong nuclear force does the “holding” by itself.

The better phrasing is:

* **Binding energy is the energy released when nucleons bind together into a nucleus.**
* **Equivalently, it’s the energy you would need to supply to unbind the nucleus back into free nucleons.**

---

### 🔹 Analogy

Think of a rock in a valley:

* When it rolls down the hill into the valley, it *releases* energy (heat, sound).
* Once in the valley, you don’t need to keep adding energy to “hold it there.”
* But if you want to take it back up to the hilltop, you must put in that much energy again.

That’s exactly the binding/unbinding picture.
