    - ### Basis vectors and coordinate axes
        - $MY_NOTES/maths/linear-algebra/Abstract-vectors-vector-spaces.txt
        - A bit of terminology I was mixing up!
        - A `basis vector` corresponds to a coordinate axis, but
          the two are NOT the same mathematical object.
        - 1. E.g. consider:
            - `i` = (1, 0, 0)
            - `j` = (0, 1, 0)
            - `k` = (0, 0, 1)
            - Each is a single vector: a directed quantity with
              a particular magnitude and direction.
        - 2. The coordinate axes
            - Each basis vector DEFINES a corresponding axis:
            - `i` defines `x-axis`.
            - `j` defines `y-axis`.
            - `k` defines `z-axis`.
            - An axis, however, is an _entire line_ extending
              infinitely in both directions. The vector `i` is
              just one particular vector along that line.
        - 3. Example:
            - The `x-axis` contains all vectors of the form
              `(a,0,0)`, where `a` can be any real number.
            - The basis vector `i` is just the particular case
              `(1,0,0)`.

    - ### Hilbert Space nitpick
        - $MY_NOTES/physics/QM/Hilbert-Space-Precise.txt
        - The Hilbert space `H`:
            - Mathematical space containing state vectors `|ψ⟩`.
            - Strictly, a physical pure state corresponds to
              a `ray` in Hilbert space, rather than to one unique
              state vector.
            - `State vector`: 
                - one particular vector, with a specific length
                  and direction.
            - `Ray`: 
                - all vectors pointing in that same direction,
                  with any non-zero length.
            - Phase:
                - multiplying a state vector by a NON-ZERO
                  complex number doesn't change the ray. 
                - For normalised state vectors, this means `|ψ⟩`
                  and `e^(iφ)|ψ⟩` represent the same physical
                  pure state, regardless of global phase `φ`.
                - Aside:
                    - Strictly, scalar-multiples apply to
                      NON-ZERO complex scalars; the `zero vector`
                      is not a state vector representing
                      a PHYSICAL STATE.

    - ### What is a tensor?
        - $MY_NOTES/maths/Vector-Calculus/Tensors-contravariant-covariant.txt
        - Good! The key is "in terms of ... basis vectors"!
        - The distinguishing characteristic of a tensor is
          a special property called `multilinearity`, which means
          that it must be linear in each of its `r` arguments.
          Recall that linearity for a function with a single
          argument just means:
            - T(v + cw) = T(v) + cT(w)
            - for all vectors `v` and `w` and numbers `c`).
        - This multilinearity enables us to express the value of
          the function on an arbitrary set of `r` vectors in
          terms of the values of the function on `r basis
          vectors` like `x̂`, `ŷ`, and `ẑ`.
        - These values of the function on basis vectors are
          nothing but the familiar components of the tensor,
          which in older treatments are usually introduced first
          as part of the definition of the tensor.

    ### Bell & Counterfactual definiteness
        - $MY_NOTES/physics/QM/Peres-unperformed-Paper.txt
        - Bell's theorem identifies an impossible trio:
            - You CANNOT simultaneously have
                - 1. locality (local outcome/measurement
                  functions depend on the local setting [e.g.
                  `a⃗`] and also `λ`, but not the distant
                  setting),
                - 2. counterfactual definiteness (unperformed
                  measurements have pre-existing values),
                - 3. and agreement with quantum predictions.
                - Experiment confirms QM, so one of the first two
                  must go.
        - Me:
            - But where does Bell assume 'counterfactual
              definiteness'?? I thought he only assumed causual
              locality? ie that was his ONLY assumption
        - Great answer:
            - $MY_NOTES/physics/QM/Peres-CFD-Gemini.txt
            - Counterfactual Definiteness (CFD) enters right at
              the beginning of his 1964 paper in his definition
              of the measurement functions
                - Outcome of Alice's measurement along AXIS `a⃗`
                    - `A(a⃗, λ) = ±1`
                    - (ie assign a definite number +1 or −1 to
                      every possible choice of `a⃗`)
                - Bob's along AXIS `b⃗` as:
                    - `B(b⃗, λ) = ±1`
            - When Bell later computes the difference between two
              correlation experiments:
                - `P(a⃗, b⃗) − P(a⃗, c⃗) = ∫ ρ(λ) [A(a⃗, λ) B(b⃗, λ)
                  − A(a⃗, λ) B(c⃗, λ)] dλ`
                - he writes `B(b⃗, λ)` AND `B(c⃗, λ)` inside the
                  SAME integral, evaluated on SAME state `λ`.
                - In the real world, you can measure `B` along
                  `b⃗` OR along `c⃗`, but never both on the SAME
                  PARTICLE.
                - Writing both values as simultaneously existing
                  for a single system `λ` is the exact definition
                  of _Counterfactual Definiteness_.
            - Another LLM
                - He uses the EPR argument to motivate
                  predetermined outcomes, then formulates a local
                  hidden-variable `MODEL` in which outcomes are
                  functions of the local setting and `λ`.
                - The mathematical derivation uses those
                  functions for ALTERNATIVE settings as well as
                  the settings actually chosen.
                - Peres's paper draws attention to the
                  interpretive significance of THAT STEP.
                - It is therefore more accurate to say that
                  Bell's original argument combines locality with
                  _EPR-motivated_ predetermination, rather than
                  presenting locality and counterfactual
                  definiteness as two explicitly independent
                  postulates.

    - ### More Peres (summary)
        - $MY_NOTES/physics/QM/Peres-unperformed-GPT2.txt
        - (A) Local randomness
            - Each side individually looks like pure noise:
            - No pattern
            - No bias
            - No information about the other side
        - (B) Strong non-classical correlation
            - Together, they produce:
            - Highly structured correlations
            - Specifically: `−α·β`
            - This violates Bell inequalities
        - Classically, you'd expect:
            - If outcomes are random locally → correlations should be weak
            - If correlations are strong → there must be underlying predetermined values
            - But QM says:
                - Outcomes are not predetermined
                - Yet correlations are stronger than any classical model allows
                - This is the contradiction Bell exposed.
        - 7) Aside: Final clarification of the “contradiction”
            - Peres is not resolving a contradiction inside QM.
            - He is resolving this:
            - The contradiction between
                - (a) classical intuitions (pre-existing values + locality)
                - and
                - (b) quantum correlations (−α · β)
            - And his resolution is:
                - Drop pre-existing values.
            - Your resolution is:
                - Drop locality.
                - Both are valid. The choice is interpretational, not forced by experiment alone.

    - ### The actual Peres paper
        - $MY_NOTES/physics/QM/Peres-unperformed-Paper.txt
        - Bell’s core result:
            - You cannot simultaneously maintain
                - (i)
                    - locality,
                - (ii)
                    - predefined outcomes for all possible
                      measurements (counterfactual definiteness)
                - and
                - (iii)
                    - agreement with quantum correlations
                    - `E(α, β) = −α·β`.
                - One of these must be abandoned.
        - Where the contradiction actually lies:
            - It is not within QM itself, but in trying to
              explain quantum predictions using a CLASSICAL
              picture that assumes both locality and pre-existing
              values. This leads to Bell inequalities, which are
              experimentally violated.
        - Hidden assumption identified by Peres:
            - The step that generates the contradiction is
              assigning SIMULTANEOUS values to measurements that
              were never performed. This is the assumption of
              counterfactual definiteness.
        - Peres’ key claim:
            - Reject that assumption — “unperformed experiments
              have no results.” Once you stop treating
              hypothetical measurements as having real outcomes,
              the contradiction disappears without needing to
              abandon locality (at least at the level of no
              signalling).
        - What makes quantum correlations striking:
            - Individual outcomes are locally random (no
              signalling), yet jointly they exhibit strong,
              highly structured correlations (−α·β).
            - This combination cannot be reproduced by any
              classical local model with predefined values.
        - The real interpretational fork:
            - Bell’s theorem forces a choice. Either:
                - → drop counterfactual definiteness
                  (Peres-style, no predefined values),
                - or
                - → drop locality (e.g. Bohmian mechanics,
                  explicit nonlocality).
          - The disagreement is philosophical: what kind of
            “reality” one is willing to give up.

    - ### Ah! Peres is about Contextuality
        - $MY_NOTES/physics/QM/Peres-Contextuality.txt
        - Peres reframes Bell, not rederives it.
            - Bell proved QM violates a hidden-variable
              inequality. Peres diagnoses *why*:
                - the violation comes from illegitimately
                  assuming unperformed measurements have definite
                  outcomes — which QM rejects.
        - The real target is counterfactual definiteness.
            - This ties Bell's theorem to Kochen-Specker
              contextuality: quantum observables have no
              pre-existing values independent of measurement
              context. Peres unifies both under one slogan.
        - Legacy is philosophical more than technical.
            - The title became canonical Copenhagen shorthand for
              unmeasured quantities, and seeded lasting debate
              about what Bell actually proves — nonlocality, or
              the failure of classical counterfactual reasoning.

    - ### How to ESCAPE Bell?
        - $MY_NOTES/physics/QM/Peres-unperformed-GPT2.txt
        - The paper is basically a _sharpened restatement of
          Bell’s theorem_, but with a very specific philosophical
          target:
            - the idea that unperformed measurements HAVE
              definite results.
            - Peres’ slogan “unperformed experiments have no
              results” is not a throwaway line; it is the logical
              ESCAPE HATCH from Bell’s contradiction.
            - Individually:
                - Each local result looks completely random.
                - Each local average is zero.
                - No dependence on distant choices is visible
                  locally.
            - Jointly:
                - When you compare the results from `A` and `B`,
                  they show a highly structured, ANGLE-DEPENDENT
                  CORRELATION.
                - This structure is too strong to be explained by
                  any assignment of PRE-EXISTING local values.
                - Yet it does not allow signaling and does not
                  show up in any single-detector statistics.

    - ### Not maximally non-local
        - $MY_NOTES/physics/QM/Peres-unperformed-Gemini.txt
            - Popescu-Rohrlich (PR) Boxes
                - The Implication:
                - This proves that the "No-Signaling" principle
                  alone is not sufficient to derive QM.
                - Nature is non-local, but NOT *maximally*
                  non-local.
                - Why? That is one of the biggest open questions
                  in foundations today.
            - "un-performed"
                - Peres mentions "unperformed experiments have no
                  results." This refers to _Contextuality_.
                - The Insight:
                    - Bell's theorem is really a special case of
                      the broader phenomenon of `Contextuality`.
                    - The Kochen-Specker theorem proves (using
                      pure geometry in 3D Hilbert space) that you
                      cannot assign values to observables
                      independent of the *context* (i.e., which
                      other commuting observables you measure
                      them with).

    - ### Structural Realism reading group
        - $MY_NOTES/physics/metaphysics/structural-realism.txt
            - Structural realism argues we don’t have direct
              knowledge of objects as INDEPENDENT units
              ("monads").
            - Instead, our knowledge is basically about
              relationships between objects, captured in
              `equations` and `interactions`, rather object
              attributes in isolation.
            - ie: Monadic propositions ("electron has charge
              `-e`") would describe properties of particles as if
              they exist independently of other entities.
            - But Structural Realism says we only know particles
              (like electrons, quarks) through the network of
              relationships and interactions they participate in,
              rather than as standalone, monadic objects.
        - Reading Group:
            - We start with an overview of SR in the philosophy
              of science, and then dive into David Wallace's
              popular `math-first` approach that aims to
              articulate a clear notion of structure suited to
              modern physics.
            - After considering the strengths and limitations of
              the math-first approach, we move via a case study
              on phlogiston into the `real patterns` approach,
              inspired by Daniel Dennett and advocated by James
              Ladyman. We end with a connection between SR and
              recent `effective` realisms.

    - ### Two meanings of 'locality'
        - $MY_NOTES/physics/QM/Sabine-Entanglement-analysis-GPT.txt
        - ### (1) Operational locality
            - No faster-than-light signalling
            - QM satisfies this
        - ### (2) Bell locality (local causality)
            - No superluminal causal dependence
            - Requires factorisation:
                - `P(a,b | A,B,λ) = P(a|A,λ) P(b|B,λ)`
                - This factorisation expresses Bell locality
                  (local causality):
                - Once `λ` is specified, Alice’s outcome depends
                  only on her setting `A`, and Bob’s outcome
                  depends only on his setting `B`.
                - QM violates this.
                - Note:
                    - `A`, `B`
                        - measurement settings
                        - (e.g. which spin direction Alice and
                          Bob choose)
                    - `a`, `b`
                        - measurement outcomes
                        - (e.g. spin up/down → `±1`)
                    - `λ`
                        - “hidden variables”
                        - (a complete specification of the
                          system’s physical state, beyond QM if
                          needed)
                    - `P(a, b | A, B, λ)`
                        - joint probability of getting outcomes
                          a and b given:
                        - both settings `(A, B)` and the
                          underlying state `λ`
        - Also:
            - QM is:
                - Local (no signalling)
                - Nonlocal (violates Bell locality)
            - So:
                - Hossenfelder: emphasising no signalling
                - Maudlin: emphasising failure of local causality
            - Both correct, but different notions of “locality”

    - ### The Gradient is a 'Jacobian'!
        - $MY_NOTES/maths/Vector-Calculus/Jacobian.md
        - Slogan: gradient = Jacobian-for-scalar-functions.
        - A function is:
            - scalar-valued → one output number
            - vector-valued → many output numbers arranged in
              a vector
        - Is the `gradient` basically the `Jacobian` for
          scalar-valued functions?
            - Yes. More precisely:
            - If `f: Rⁿ → R` is scalar-valued, its Jacobian
              should be a 1×n matrix:
                - `J_f = [ ∂f/∂x₁ ∂f/∂x₂ … ∂f/∂xₙ ].`
            - But this `1×n` matrix is exactly the gradient
              vector `∇f`, up to the convention of writing it as
              a row or column.
            - So the gradient is the Jacobian of a scalar-valued
              function, just with `m = 1`.

    - ### Standard v Infinitesimal Calculus
        - See more: $MY_NOTES/maths/analysis/infinitesimals.txt
        - Standard calculus:
            - The fundamental concept isn’t the differential of
              a variable but the `derivative` of a function; the
              derivative is defined as a limit.
            - The differential is then defined in terms of the
              derivative. The notion of functions and limits
              involves dependent and independent variables.
        - Infinitesimal approach:
            - the fundamental concept is the `differential` of
              any variable, regardless of dependence or
              independence, and the derivative is defined as
              a quotient of differentials.

    - ### A Variation: Why symbol `δ`?
        - $MY_NOTES/maths/analysis/Variations-Extremal-Paths.txt
        - No deep mathematical necessity — it's mostly historical
          convention, originating with Euler and Lagrange.
        - They wanted:
            - A symbol distinct from `d`, to avoid confusion
            - Something suggestive of "difference" or "deviation"
            - something easy to write alongside integrals
        - `δ` was a natural choice because `Δ` (capital delta)
          already meant a finite difference, and `δ` intuitively
          evokes an infinitesimal difference but of a DIFFERENT
          KIND from `dx`.
        - So the hierarchy became:
            - `Δx` = finite difference
            - `δx` = infinitesimal variation of a FUNCTION
            - `dx` = infinitesimal differential of a variable

    - ### Strong/Weak objectivity
        - $MY_NOTES/physics/metaphysics/strong-weak-objectivity.txt
        - STRONG:
            - when a theory or statement can be read as
              describing how the world is *in itself*,
              independently of observers, measuring devices, or
              information-gathering conditions.
            - Strong objectivity: statements have the form
              "System X has property `P`," where `P` is intended
              as a MIND-INDEPENDENT feature of reality.
        - WEAK:
            - When a theory or statement makes claims that are
              objectively *testable* and publicly *agreeable*,
            - BUT
            - whose meaning essentially depends on observational
              or preparational conditions.
            - The statements aren't straightforwardly about "how
              things are," but about what any observer would
              *obtain* in specific experimental contexts.
            - Standard textbook QM and some statistical mechanics
              fit this category:
                - their basic laws refer to measurement outcomes,
                  ensembles, coarse-graining procedures, or
                  preparation protocols.

    - ### Maxwell's Constraints
        - $MY_NOTES/physics/EM/Maxwells-Equations.txt
        - Constraints + dynamical
        - ### Gauss’s law for electricity.
        - `∇·E = ρ/ε₀`
        - Tells us charge is the `source` of the electric field.
          But subtlety: it’s not an evolution equation, it's
          a CONSTRAINT. It doesn’t say how `E` changes with
          _time_; only how it must look given `ρ` at each
          instant.
        - It acts as a consistency condition that must hold at
          all times, and will stay true automatically if we start
          with initial data satisfying it and then use the other
          equations to evolve forward.
        - It's one reason Maxwell’s equations aren’t "four
          equations for four fields"—instead two equations are
          dynamical, two are constraints.

    - ### Weak Gravity / Hierarchy prob
        - Take two protons separated by a distance `r`.
        - The ratio (independent of `r`) is
            - `F_g / F_e = (G·m_p²) / (k·e²)`
        - Plugging in the constants gives:
            - `F_g / F_e ≈ 10⁻³⁶ to 10⁻³⁸`
        - So gravitational attraction between two protons is
          about `10³⁶` times weaker than the EM force between
          them.
        - That enormous disparity is sometimes called the
          hierarchy problem: why is gravity so feeble compared to
          the other fundamental interactions?
        - More numerology: The ratio of the radius of the
          observable universe (`~10²⁶` m) to an atom (`~10⁻¹⁰` m)
          is about `10³⁶` — eerily close to the same ratio.
        - This contrast hints at why a "quantum spacetime" might
          be necessary. If spacetime itself becomes grainy or
          fluctuating at the Planck scale (~10⁻³⁵ m), then
          gravity’s extreme weakness at ordinary scales could be
          a mere shadow of its true quantum structure. The
          mystery hides in the enormous gulf of scales.

    - ### Hierarchy problem 2
        - $MY_NOTES/physics/spacetime/Hierarchy-problem.txt
        - key: "forces go like energy^2"
        - compare the two key energy scales in our universe:
            - Electroweak scale (set by the Higgs field):
                - v ≈ 246 GeV
            - Planck scale (gravity becomes strong):
                - M_Pl ≈ 10¹⁹ GeV
            - Their ratio is v / M_Pl ≈ 10⁻¹⁷.

            - It’s the same "tiny number" as in the force ratio!
            - It’s just expressed differently: instead of saying
              "gravity is `10⁻³⁶` times weaker," we’re saying
              "the electroweak scale is `10⁻¹⁷` times smaller
              than the Planck scale."

            - Why the different exponent?
            - Because forces go like `(energy scale)²` — roughly
              speaking, the strength of gravity between particles
              of mass `m` goes as `(m / M_Pl)²`.
            - So `(1 GeV / 10¹⁹ GeV)² ≈ 10⁻³⁸`, which matches
              your intuitive proton-force ratio.

        - `Reductionist` explanations:
            - focus on breaking complex phenomena down to their
              simplest components and understanding the system by
              analyzing these parts.
            - Eg, explaining the behavior of a gas by studying
              the motion of individual molecules is reductionist.
        - `Functionalist` explanations:
            - focus on the role or _purpose_ of a phenomenon
              within a larger system. Instead of looking only at
              the parts, functionalism asks
                - "what does this do?" or
                - "what role does it play?"
            - Eg, in biology, explaining the heart by its
              function of pumping blood, rather than by the
              mechanics of individual cells, is functionalist.

              observable violations of the second law.
            - In particular, if a particle is large enough to be

        - GPT discussion
            - The second law states that entropy (disorder) in
              a closed system always increases or remains
              constant.
            - However, at the microscopic level, random thermal
              motion can momentarily cause small, localized
              _decreases_ in entropy.
            - Normally, these fluctuations are too small to
              notice, but Einstein realized that if a suspended
              particle is large enough to be seen, its motion
              (due to collisions with surrounding liquid
              molecules) would provide visible evidence of these
              microscopic fluctuations.
            - This is exactly what happens in Brownian motion,
              where large particles in a liquid move in
              a jittery, random way due to the impacts of
              countless tiny molecules.
            - Even though the second law holds on average, these
              fluctuations briefly create small, temporary
              reversals of entropy—violations that are visible
              under a microscope!
        - What is a 2nd law 'reversal'?
            - A temporary violation of the second law of
              thermodynamics means that, for a brief moment, the
              system becomes more ordered instead of more
              disordered. This can happen due to random
              fluctuations, and here’s what it might look like at
              different scales:
            1. Atomic Level
                - At the atomic scale, the liquid molecules
                  surrounding a suspended particle are constantly
                  moving due to thermal energy.
                - Normally, these molecules push the particle in
                  _random directions_, leading to Brownian
                  motion. However, due to statistical chance,
                  there can be moments when:
                    - More molecules happen to push the particle
                      in one direction instead of randomly. This
                      would momentarily give the particle
                      a small, seemingly "non-random" motion.
                    - The surrounding molecules could briefly
                      cluster together in an unusually
                      low-entropy way, forming a temporary
                      "density fluctuation" where some region
                      becomes more ordered.

            2. Visible Microscopic Level
                - If you're looking through a microscope at
                  a large suspended particle, a second-law
                  violation might look like:
                - The particle suddenly _moving in one direction_
                  for an unusually long time, as if it were
                  "self-organizing" rather than moving randomly.
                - Several Brownian particles briefly clustering
                  together instead of spreading out, which would
                  be a momentary local decrease in entropy.
                - A spontaneous local flow of heat from a colder
                  region to a hotter region (extremely rare, but
                  possible due to fluctuations).

            - These fluctuations don’t last long and are quickly
              "corrected" by the usual increase in entropy.
              That’s why the second law is statistically valid
              over time, even though temporary reversals can
              happen.
            - So yes—one way to picture it's particles
              momentarily coming together or organizing in an
              unexpected way before randomness takes over again!
