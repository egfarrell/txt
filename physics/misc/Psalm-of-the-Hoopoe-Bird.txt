# LEVEL XII

### Psalm of the Hoopoe Bird

[]

Horned altar cut from one piece of basalt rock using iron tools.
Makor, 1116 B.C.E. Bull’s head carved in low relief. Aperture for
blood of animal sacrifices. Religious significance of the four
corners known as “horns” not clear, but on consecrating a new
altar the blood of animal victims was rubbed on each of the
horns, according to the directions given by Yahweh to Moses in
Exodus 29:12. “And thou shalt take of the blood of the bullock,
and put it upon the horns of the altar with thy finger, and pour
all the blood beside the bottom of the altar.” Fugitives seeking
sanctuary, even from the king, were secure so long as they
grasped the horns of the altar, as explained in I Kings 1:50:
“And Adonijah feared because of Solomon, and arose, and went, and
caught hold on the horns of the altar” Deposited at Makor in late
spring, 963 B.C.E.

[]

- Morning in Makor: birds chattered, children played, and the
  governor’s door opened as a pudgy, freckled man left wearing
  a frown.

- Children chased him chanting “Hoopoe,” and his scowl melted
  into a half-moon grin as he tossed sweets to them; the town
  adored him.

- The nickname came from a comical local bird — short, crested,
  and habitually poking into holes — a bird various peoples found
  amusing or sacred.

- The engineer called Hoopoe (real name Jabaal) resembled his
  namesake: short, dumpy, always inspecting holes; he’d won
  affection by being cheerful, generous and harmless.

- He was thirty-nine, a former fighter for King David, now
  charged with rebuilding Makor’s new inner wall; his wife Kerith
  was the priest’s daughter who had seen David in Jerusalem.

- Makor worshiped both old Canaanite gods (Baal) and the newer
  Yahweh; families mixed beliefs pragmatically, and Jabaal’s name
  — “Yahweh is Baal” — helped him bridge communities.

- Hoopoe worked relentlessly, treated slaves well, and converted
  many practical men to Yahweh; word of his competence reached
  General Amram in Jerusalem.

- The new wall was necessary because the old outer rampart had
  been buried by rubble; though the town had shrunk, Makor
  prospered under David’s rule.

- Kerith was beautiful, devoted and pious; she and Hoopoe loved
  one another despite a spiritual difference: she leaned fully to
  Yahweh, he respected Baal’s claim on the soil.

- Despite solid new ramparts, Hoopoe feared the town’s real
  weakness: the exposed waterwall and well, an obvious target for
  Phoenician siege engines.

- He conceived a radical fix: dig a deep central shaft, descend
  through rubble and rock, then tunnel nearly two hundred cubits
  under the town to the well, burying it so besiegers couldn’t
  reach the water.

- The governor laughed, dismissed the idea as waste and mockingly
  told Hoopoe to “dig worms,” suspecting only a fear of losing
  slaves.

- Hoopoe sketched plans at home, kept his crew busy on other
  works to avoid losing them, and refined the scheme: about 40
  feet through mound, 90 feet of rock, ~280 feet of sloping
  tunnel — roughly 410 feet of bore.

- Frustrated by disbelief, Hoopoe slipped that night into the
  slave pens and woke Meshab, the stoic Moabite foreman: “Meshab,
  the time has come to build the water system.”

- Hoopoe and Meshab agree a shaft-and-tunnel to the hidden well
  can be done—if they solve the orientation problem.

- Meshab says they can cut rock with Phoenician iron, but from
  the shaft’s bottom they won’t know which direction to tunnel.

- Hoopoe remembers a sailor’s trick: align distant flags (a
  “range”) to steer a ship into harbor.

- He realizes the same trick could guide their digging: plant
  flags on the mountainside and rooflines so a straight line will
  point to the well.

- By lamp-light they mark positions: flags halfway up the slope,
  several more toward the governor’s roof—the sixth flag
  completing the range.

- Guards try to stop them, but Hoopoe vouches for Meshab and they
  slip out at night to place cloth markers on the well and climb
  Baal’s mountain to test the sightlines.

- In moonlight they confirm a clear visual line from the town’s
  water-wall to the governor’s roof—exactly the unfailing range
  they need.

- Meshab worries how diggers at the shaft’s bottom could see the
  range; Hoopoe hints he has a solution.

- Descending, they encounter torch-bearing pilgrims and follow
  them up to the ancient Baal monolith.

- Meshab kneels, praying to Baal; he insists he will “live and
  die with Baal,” refusing to become a Hebrew.

- Hoopoe, once Canaanite like Meshab, explains how his people
  gradually accepted Yahweh; their conversation sketches the
  larger religious shifts but ends with mutual tolerance.

- Two women arrive to pray; Hoopoe hears their simple, domestic
  petitions and then privately prays to Baal for a different
  favor: that his wife Kerith be allowed to go to Jerusalem.

- Hoopoe asks Baal to help him build something that will bring
  him before King David in Jerusalem—an awkward, hopeful prayer
  that blends loyalties.

- For two weeks Hoopoe stalls on the water project and scrambles
  to keep his work crew employed; the governor still will not
  grasp the tunnel’s promise.

- Kerith, longing for Jerusalem, hears that General Amram—the
  kingdom’s fortifications inspector—is coming north and might
  visit Makor.

- Excited, she goes to tell Hoopoe at the slave camp; the stench,
  filth, and broken men shock her, but she finds her husband
  planning with Meshab.

- The news galvanizes Hoopoe: if Amram understands the design, he
  could approve the project—and possibly carry Hoopoe’s skill to
  Jerusalem.

- Kerith urges Hoopoe to ask Amram directly for a post in
  Jerusalem; Hoopoe instead insists he must first present his
  water system.

- Kerith reluctantly agrees to wait the three years Hoopoe
  estimates the tunnel would take, calls him a “fool” for
  hesitating to seize a direct chance, and the couple reveal
  their deeper divide: Hoopoe practical, rooted at Makor; Kerith
  yearning for Yahweh and the holy city.

- Back at the camp Hoopoe finishes detailed plans on leather;
  Meshab notices he aligns the tunnel’s diagonal to the six-flag
  range.

- Hoopoe replies simply: that diagonal—tied to the range—is what
  will make the underground tunnel possible.

- Meshab baked forty-five clay tablets of sectional drawings
  while Hoopoe prepared a leather roll—one set for Jerusalem
  explanations, the tablets to guide work in Makor.

- On a bright spring morning General Amram rode in from Megiddo;
  children cheered, the governor welcomed him, and Kerith,
  Hoopoe’s wife, served at the gates.

- Amram—a hard, experienced military leader—noticed Kerith’s
  beauty and Hoopoe’s nervous pride; he listened politely as the
  governor praised the fortifications.

- Impatient to inspect, Amram brusquely left the reception with
  Hoopoe and Kerith at his side, testing the walls and asking
  pointed questions about defenses.

- He mocked the governor’s vanity but diplomatically praised
  Hoopoe’s workmanship to flatter him before removing him from
  the field of view.

- Hoopoe warned that Makor’s waterwall was vulnerable; he
  proposed a bold fix: dig a central shaft and tunnel to the
  well, then bury and disguise it so the town’s water supply
  could never be seized.

- His vision of an impregnable Makor inspired Amram; seeing the
  strategic value, the general pressed Hoopoe for
  requirements—slaves and detailed plans.

- Hoopoe fetched Meshab the Moabite to show the clay plans, but
  Amram froze when he recognized Meshab as the man who had slain
  his brother in past Moabite conflicts.

- Angry and unforgiving, Amram ordered Meshab away; Kerith curtly
  commanded the slave to set down the tablets and return to camp,
  defusing the moment.

- At the feasts Kerith cultivated Amram’s favor—serving him and
  drawing his attention—hoping his influence could bring her and
  Hoopoe to Jerusalem, the spiritual center she longed for.

- Amram, shrewd and worldly, used Hoopoe: he kept the engineer
  busy on the hill and in the forest, ostensibly to test flag
  alignments, while he spent private hours with Kerith.

- Kerith confessed her longing to live where “the worship of
  Yahweh is pure”; Amram listened, flirted, then shocked her by
  declaring personal allegiance to Dagon, revealing his
  pragmatic, soldierly faith.

- Humiliated yet determined, Kerith resolved to use Hoopoe’s
  tunnel as her route to Jerusalem—if the project succeeded, it
  would bring the couple to the king’s attention.

- Amram, half mocking, declared the tunnel “the marvel of the
  north” in a public toast, then left; Hoopoe, buoyed by official
  authorization and fifty new slaves, began building in earnest.

- Hoopoe marked the shaft site with a primary red flag, set
  a second higher to form a range through the well and waterwall,
  and placed roof-flaggers across town for alignment.

- Meshab, assigned to the governor’s roof, insisted on keeping
  a crucial flag despite the governor’s outrage; Amram intervened
  and resolved the dispute by placing the flag on the wall and
  instructing Hoopoe to fetch a tall sapling.

- Over the next three years Hoopoe sank the large square main
  shaft, uncovering layered relics of past ages; he collected
  curiosities for Kerith and reflected on ancient fires and
  vanished towns.

- The local limestone proved workable when wet; Meshab proved
  indispensable—he devised the proper excavation sequence, rigged
  ropes and inclines, and organized women’s stairways to the
  well.

- Hoopoe promoted Meshab from slave foreman to house resident so
  the man could oversee the work night and day; Kerith
  reluctantly consented despite Meshab’s bloody past.

- The plan: Hoopoe would tunnel down from the well, Meshab would
  tunnel up from the shaft, and they would meet underground—both
  men worried how to keep the tunnels aligned in darkness.

- To solve the alignment problem Hoopoe quietly led Meshab away
  from town to the hills and tall trees—hinting he had a secret
  method to guide the two men through rock and shadow.

- Hoopoe and Meshab judge a tall tree suitable for aligning
  a tunnel; Hoopoe humbly prays to Baal before work begins.

- The tree is felled and placed diagonally across the square
  shaft to mark the range set by six flags so any tunnel on that
  line must hit the well.

- Hoopoe reveals his clever device: two weighted cords lowered
  from either end of the tree so the line between them reproduces
  the tree’s direction at the bottom of the shaft.

- The two taut strings, set as far apart as possible, give
  a precise heading; Meshab vows to follow them and suggests
  lamps for night work.

- Meshab begins chiseling downward through limestone while Hoopoe
  organizes the upward approach from the well; both pray—Meshab
  to Baal, Kerith to Yahweh—for success.

- Hoopoe discovers the old spring has become a deep well; he
  clears the waterwall, walls up an ancient cave, and sinks
  a narrow shaft to water level, repeating the weighted-line
  trick there.

- The well’s smaller opening forces closer strings and increases
  the risk of directional error; Hoopoe checks the range
  obsessively and consults clay tablets for the correct upward
  pitch.

- To avoid missing each other in full-size tunnels, they agree to
  dig narrow pilot shafts—just large enough to work and hear
  through the rock—and later widen and correct them.

- For months they dig day and night in cramped conditions; each
  evening they exchange ritual signals: a flag on the wall and
  nine measured hammer blows from each end.

- As the tunnels lengthen, hope grows but sound remains muffled;
  they meticulously recheck range, poles, and cord alignments,
  especially the vulnerable well end.

- Hoopoe, burdened by doubt, takes responsibility for any error,
  prays on the high place to Baal and then to Yahweh, and returns
  resolved to make the alignment perfect.

- At dusk, amid their ritual signals, Meshab and Hoopoe
  unexpectedly hear each other’s sledge through the rock; they
  strike together and meet precisely where planned.

- The town erupts in cheers; flags wave; the two men greet each
  other in the open and confirm the alignment has succeeded.

- They set about correcting headings and preparing to join the
  pilot holes, but their iron tools are worn out; new chisels and
  wedges must be bought in the Phoenician port of Aecho.

- Hoopoe travels alone to Aecho to purchase tools, passing guards
  and customs, and marvels at the cosmopolitan harbor and exotic
  goods.

- In the ironmonger’s he procures permitted tools—but they are
  greased with pork fat to prevent rust, embarrassing him as
  a Hebrew; he accepts them anyway.

- He lingers in the city, buys a braided glass gift for his wife,
  and despite warnings about drinking, samples Phoenician beer;
  by midnight he is merry, sings, and becomes intoxicated.

- The groat merchants find the unsteady Hoopoe and steer him
  home; he collapses along the waterfront, still insisting Meshab
  should have accompanied him.

- While Hoopoe was away at Aecho, Meshab pressed on alone,
  listening at his rock face, then at the well and into Hoopoe’s
  tunnel, using echoes to pinpoint and nudge the heading so the
  two tunnels would meet.

- Meshab suspected Hoopoe’s work had been off course; alone, he
  corrected the small error quietly. The men marveled at how
  accurately the Hebrew had oriented his shaft.

- Each evening Meshab checked the tree and guide strings,
  inspected the waterwall, washed at Hoopoe’s house, donned
  a salvaged Moabite robe and sat in heavy sandals dreaming of
  freedom after the tunnel was finished.

- He still ate with the slaves at first, reluctant to be seen
  dining at Hoopoe’s table, until Kerith, Hoopoe’s wife, invited
  him after Hoopoe left for Aecho.

- Over shared meals Meshab and Kerith spoke frankly: he described
  the tunnel’s likely completion that month and how they could
  adjust the alignment; she confessed her longing for Jerusalem
  and a life beyond Makor.

- Meshab revealed his past: a once-important Moabite farmer whose
  wife and children were killed in a Hebrew raid; later captured
  by Amram, spared by David, enslaved by Amram, he kept faith
  with Baal rather than convert to Yahweh.

- Kerith, pragmatic and ambitious, admitted she had once tried to
  curry favor with General Amram to reach Jerusalem; Meshab
  responded with wry caution—Jerusalem seen under a yoke is no
  prize.

- Their talks deepened: Kerith recognized Hoopoe’s honesty but
  doubted he was the kind of man summoned to the capital; Meshab
  defended Hoopoe’s character and quietly admired Kerith’s
  intelligence.

- Despite her restlessness, Kerith was moved by Meshab’s
  willingness to endure seven years in the slave camp rather than
  renounce his god.

- One evening Hoopoe returned from Aecho drunk but triumphant,
  bearing extra iron tools, a Cypriot sword and gifts. He
  discovered Meshab’s subtle correction and embraced him for
  averting a public error.

- Hoopoe celebrated, promising Meshab freedom when the final rock
  was pierced, and immediately issued the new tools to the men
  for the last cuts.

- At the end of Ethanim, with early rains and the town’s future
  at stake, excitement mounted: both crews had tunneled nearly
  140 feet through rock and were within a few days of meeting,
  likely within a two-foot tolerance.

- On the last day Hoopoe let a reliable slave make the first
  puncture; when the breakthrough came, the tunnel erupted with
  shouts. Hoopoe crawled through, saw Meshab, called him brother
  and declared him free—Meshab stayed to finish the tunnel beside
  him.

- At that instant a slim, exhausted man named Gershom, carrying
  a small kinnor, clambered toward the town seeking sanctuary and
  made for the temple.

- (New scene) Back at the Tell, khamsin winds stalled excavation;
  only some Moroccans kept working in the trenches as heat and
  dust made digging almost impossible.

- John Cullinane, missing Vered Bar-El, half-occupied himself
  with a progress report; Eliav and Tabari accused him of leaning
  too much on parallels from Megiddo and Gezer and urged him to
  trust Makor’s own evidence.

- To prove their point they toured modern sites: a new Akko
  house, a modest immigrant home, an old Arab dwelling, and
  a cave used for millennia—showing how wildly different levels
  can exist side by side and warning against simplistic
  homogeneity in reporting.

- The three men revealed different mental maps of the
  land—west-to-east, north-to-south, and timeless local
  rootedness—then returned to Makor with fresh perspective.

- Gershom, a hill singer who had killed a man in the uplands and
  fled, arrived exhausted at Makor pleading sanctuary. The
  governor directed him to the plain, unadorned temple—small,
  reddish field stone, olive-wood doors with stone hinges—while
  the governor also escorted the pursuers to the same sanctuary.

- The town temple was dimly lit by a few oil lamps. At its raised
  center stood a black basalt altar carved with a bull’s head and
  four worn “horns” — now rounded corners of rock but still
  a place of asylum.

- A man named Gershom knelt on the top platform, clutching two of
  those horns; his sheepskin was tossed aside and his kin-nor lay
  nearby.

- The slain man’s three brothers stood outside; the governor
  reminded them of King David’s law: sanctuary must be respected
  while the fugitive touches the altar, and they must wait fifty
  cubits away.

- The brothers withdrew; the governor asked Gershom what had
  happened. Gershom, showing a fresh scar across his neck, said
  he had killed a man in a quarrel — “angry words … over
  nothing.”

- Doubting the brothers’ restraint, the governor posted four
  guards to preserve Gershom’s life so long as he held a horn
  — a local adaptation of the ancient cities-of-refuge custom.

- Civic life was suddenly energized by another event: shouts
  echoed that the town’s slave-dug tunnels had met. Meshab the
  Moabite emerged exultant; Hoopoe the engineer was credited for
  guiding the match.

- The governor celebrated the tunnel’s success, promising Hoopoe
  a salary and offering Meshab full civic acceptance if he would
  accept circumcision; Meshab declined, declaring his devotion to
  Baal of Moab.

- With the governor reassured that blood-feud rituals would
  likely fade after the required waiting period, townspeople
  turned their curiosity to the unusual refugee at the altar.

- Children brought Gershom food; the Levites provided water and
  privy accommodations; villagers lingered to hear him tune his
  lyre and sing both old mountain songs and new hymns to Yahweh.

- Though Gershom sang passionately, he always kept a hand ready
  on the horns, alert to the brothers’ periodic checks at the
  temple door.

- On the third day Kerith, Hoopoe’s wife, carried their household
  offering to the temple and heard Gershom sing for the first
  time: tender, personal hymns that spoke of Yahweh’s care for
  the lonely and suffering.

- When she quietly told him the brothers had left, Gershom
  rejoiced; his music became a small public event in Makor,
  drawing children and adults alike.

- Meanwhile Hoopoe and Meshab supervised the merging and
  enlargement of the slave tunnels into a finished water conduit:
  an accurately planned, ten-foot-by-six-foot tunnel that would
  supply the well below the town.

- Meshab, now a freedman, celebrated with Hoopoe and Kerith;
  Hoopoe planned to reward Tunnel workers and hoped the tunnel
  would bring recognition from Jerusalem.

- Gershom’s reputation spread. Some villagers spread darker
  stories about his past — that he’d arrived without genealogy,
  stolen sheep, and ambushed the murdered man — but many accepted
  him for his songs and presence.

- Kerith was captivated not by physical attraction but by the
  meaning in Gershom’s music: he portrayed Yahweh as both cosmic
  sovereign and intimate comforter, matching the spiritual
  longings she had nurtured for years.

- Her visits to the wine shop grew frequent under the pretense of
  buying oil; she lingered to hear the seven-stringed lyre and
  the hymns that made Yahweh approachable and personal.

- Meshab, alarmed by gossip that Kerith loved Gershom, warned
  Hoopoe. Hoopoe bristled but insisted on Kerith’s purity; he
  clung to hopes of taking the tunnel’s success — and Kerith — to
  Jerusalem for a better future.

- Kerith finally spoke to Gershom in the street, asked about his
  songs and his scar, and invited him to sing in her home while
  Hoopoe was away. Gershom accepted; he sang often at Hoopoe’s,
  delivering his simple testimony of Yahweh.

- Hoopoe, Meshab and others listened on three levels: Meshab as
  an outsider accepted Yahweh like any foreign deity; Hoopoe
  admired the engineer’s probable fame; Kerith received the songs
  as spiritual revelation and prepared inwardly for a single,
  unifying faith.

- Gershom lived modestly: a small room behind the wool
  merchant’s, irregular work, scraps of food, favors to guards so
  he’d be warned if the brothers returned. As his presence grew
  familiar, he became a beloved singer to children, artisans, and
  the lonely.

- In spring, Hoopoe and Meshab selected six huge squared stones
  from the quarry to conceal the well. They built two
  crisscrossing grids of these monoliths over the shaft, then
  filled and covered them with rubble and earth.

- With the well effectively hidden and the tunnel cleared, Hoopoe
  ordered the old Canaanite waterwall torn down — a final step in
  completing the tunnel and securing the town’s new water system.

- Stones from the old waterwall were taken to town for new
  houses; Hoopoe and Meshab climbed the hills to check whether
  the well’s secret was still hidden.

- Hoopoe worried the old mortar line would betray the tunnel;
  Meshab proposed building a small tower at the postern gate to
  conceal the telltale mark.

- Meshab at first wanted to leave, but Kerith begged him to stay;
  moved, he agreed to build the tower.

- News arrived: King David was coming to inspect and name the
  waterworks the David Tunnel. Kerith prayed that Yahweh would
  lead them to Jerusalem.

- The king’s procession entered Makor with trumpets; Abishag
  emerged from the palanquin to lead the frail, aged David before
  the people.

- David, roused by the acclamation, briefly recalled his vigor,
  then retreated into near-oblivion; Kerith pleaded with him and
  saw flashes of the old king.

- In the governor’s house David asked for the builder of the
  tunnel; Hoopoe was presented and invited the king to see it.

- At the tunnel mouth David refused to descend and could not find
  the hidden well; Meshab had disappeared and would not present
  himself.

- Embarrassed, Hoopoe mumbled; the king turned instead to music,
  asking for the town’s lyre-player.

- Kerith led David to Gershom, the young singer, who rose from
  a squalid room, tuned his lyre and sang songs that moved the
  king deeply.

- Over days David and Gershom sang together; David decided to
  take Gershom to Jerusalem as his musician.

- Hearing that, Kerith quietly told Gershom she would go with him
  to Jerusalem; Hoopoe pleaded, but she packed only a few things
  and left.

- Grief-stricken, Hoopoe sought Meshab for counsel at the
  postern, but Meshab, discovered by a captain, fled to the
  temple and seized sanctuary at the altar.

- David, furious over Meshab’s past deeds, denied sanctuary;
  a violent struggle erupted when Hoopoe defended Meshab.

- Guards dragged Meshab from the altar and killed him; the altar
  broke and the temple was stained with blood—an act that
  immediately filled David with remorse.

- Tormented, David asked for Gershom to play; Kerith, who had
  returned with Gershom, spoke in Meshab’s praise, calling him
  a just and valiant man who’d helped build the tunnel.

- Her words pierced David; he wept, confessed his sin, and
  listened as Gershom sang psalms and lamentations that echoed
  the king’s guilt and longing.

- Moved by the music and Kerith’s testimony, David comforted them
  and resolved to take Gershom to Jerusalem; Gershom later became
  the king’s chief musician and a compiler of songs.

- Kerith left Makor with Gershom; Hoopoe never saw her again. The
  governor shunned Hoopoe for his role in the temple fight, and
  his slaves went to Jerusalem with the king.

- Hoopoe stayed in the town he had fortified but was reduced to
  a figure of ridicule—remembered mostly for his smallness and
  the scandal of his wife’s departure.

- Meshab’s death, Gershom’s rise, and David’s remorse closed
  a painful chapter: Makor kept its hidden water and walls, but
  its people, and Hoopoe above all, were changed forever.

- When Solomon became king and Jerusalem boomed with building,
  Hoopoe imagined the king would summon him to the capital; his
  name was unknown and he was never called.

- In old age he vanished; his children assumed he was dead, but
  Hoopoe had entered the tunnel he alone had designed, bringing
  hammer, chisel and a small scaffold.

- He worked for days on the ceiling while young women passing
  below brought him scraps of food and speculated about the roof.

- He kept silent, shielding them with a blanket on the scaffold,
  and finally finished his flawless construction.

- Unaware, he walked the tunnel for the last time: the well’s
  rock tiers would protect the roof for millennia, sealing the
  ancient caves; the cold sweet well fed the people; the tunnel
  rose to a shaft with two twisting stairways into sunlight.

- Emerging from the shaft, he went to the cemetery and sat on the
  grave of Meshab the Moabite, his friend and fellow worker whom
  he had buried years before.

- He thought of old days and intended to climb the mountain of
  Baal to be with his old god, but dizziness seized him; sensing
  death, he prayed, “Almighty Yahweh, accept me,” and died.

- Gershom the Psalmist left words that echoed through time;
  Hoopoe the Builder left a tunnel that was filled and forgotten.
  The poet embraced one God; the builder had vacillated between
  Baal in the earth and the unseen Yahweh, and in that wavering
  he felt himself eroded and useless at the end.

- Centuries later, in Bul of 1964, a descendant of Makor’s family
  stumbled on the tunnel; it was excavated and photographed,
  hailed by engineers as a surveying masterpiece.

- A French philosopher called the tunnel itself a “psalm,”
  praising the builder’s tangible devotion to work over words.

- Then American archaeologist John Cullinane found an actual
  inscription on the damp roof—hidden carvings revealed with
  ladders, infrared photography, powder and brushes.

- The dedication became vital: one of the earliest Hebrew
  writings, a chronological anchor, and the voice of a real man
  wrestling with divine and earthly loyalties.

- The inscription, later called “The Psalm of the Tunnel
  Builder,” read :

- Jabaal of Makor built this David Tunnel. Using six flags he
  found the secret. Using white cords he probed the earth. Using
  iron from Aecho he cut the rock. But without Meshab the Moabite
  nothing. Jabaal worked from the well and wandered. Meshab from
  the shaft and true. For Meshab was his brother and is now dead,
  slain by King David. From the heavens Yahweh directed. From the
  earth Baal. Praise to the gods who sustain us.
