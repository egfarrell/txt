
### Trying to understand the wavevector 'k'
    - A `wavevector` typically has units of: 
        - ----------------
        - CYCLES per METRE
        - ----------------
    - It has a magnitude and direction. 
    - Its magnitude is the `wavenumber` of the wave (inversely
      proportional to the wavelength), and its direction is
      perpendicular to the wavefront. 
    - In isotropic media, this is also the direction of wave
      propagation. 

### Ah wavevector `k` useful for Fourier
    - The advantage of using `k` is that it can be NEGATIVE as
      well as positive: whereas `wavelength` is always positive. 
    - The negative k’s take care of negative exponentials: 
        - `exp −2πix/λ = exp ikx`

### GPT
    - To understand `k` physically, it helps to think how it
      relates to the wave’s properties and how it provides
      additional information compared to the wavelength `λ`.
      Here’s a way to think about `k` intuitively:

1. Spatial Frequency:
   - `k` represents the `spatial frequency` of the wave, meaning
     how many wave cycles fit into a unit of distance. 
        - (rather than the temporal frequency, the period? which
          is how many wave cycles fit into a unit of time)
   - The larger the magnitude of `k`, the more wave cycles occur
     in a given length, indicating a shorter wavelength.

2. Direction and Magnitude:
   - While `λ` only tells you about the length of one complete
     wave cycle, `k` tells you both how frequently the wave
     oscillates (magnitude) and in what direction the wave is
     traveling (vector direction).
   - Think of `k` as pointing in the direction the wave is
     moving. Its magnitude is how tightly the wave is packed in
     that direction.

3. Wave Propagation:
   - In a 3D space, a wave can travel in any direction. The wave
     vector `k` points in the direction of wave propagation. For
     instance, if the wave is moving diagonally in a room, `k`
     points along that diagonal.
   - The physical distance a wave travels before repeating itself
     is still described by `λ`, but `k` adds the layer of
     directional information, which is crucial for waves in more
     than one dimension.

4. Analogy:
   - Imagine you are standing on a hill with a series of parallel
     ridges (like waves). If you walk straight across the ridges,
     the distance between each ridge is the wavelength `λ`.
     However, if you walk at an angle to the ridges, you
     encounter ridges more frequently. The direction and
     frequency with which you encounter these ridges as you move
     can be represented by `k`.

5. Formula Connection:
   - The relationship `k = 2π / λ` links the two concepts
     directly. `k` encapsulates the idea of how often the wave
     repeats (2π per wavelength) and in what direction this
     repetition occurs.

6. Practical Visualization:
   - Consider a plane wave moving in space. At any point, the
     wave vector `k` tells you the direction in which the
     wavefronts are traveling. Its magnitude tells you how
     closely spaced these wavefronts are.
   - If you could see the wave as a series of parallel planes
     moving through space, `k` would point perpendicular to these
     planes and indicate how tightly they are packed.

In summary, `k` is a compact way to describe both the periodicity
and directionality of a wave. While `λ` gives you the distance
between repeating features of the wave, `k` tells you how these
features are oriented and spaced in space, making it a more
comprehensive descriptor, especially in multiple dimensions.
