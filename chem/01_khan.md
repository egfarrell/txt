### elements
    - smallest unit of pure substance that still has the property
      of that substance.
    - smallest unit: atom? (water is not an element)

### another definition
    - element - a basic substance that can't be simplified
      (hydrogen, oxygen, gold, etc...)
    - atom - the smallest amount of an element

### cross section of air
    width of hair - 1 million carbon atoms

### number of PROTONS
    - (atomic number)
    - Thats what actually _defines_ the element.
    - The # neutrons or electrons can change, but if you change
      the number of protons then you change the element.


### orbital
    probablity _distribution_ of location of electron

### potential
    - the further away an electron is from the nucleus the MORE
      potential energy it has.

### mass
    - 1 proton = 1836 electrons
    - so really the protons and the neutrons (each 1amu) are what
      define the mass of an atom

### swipe
    - another atom can swipe away an electron from carbon, so
      carbon will be left with a net positive charge, (6 protons
      and 5 electrons)


### atom
    - a mental _abstraction_ that allows us to describe a lot
      of observations?

### light
    - wavelength of light much larger than the size of atom.
      (most things we see via reflected light)

### notation
            mass number          # protons + neutrons
                    `C`                    `C`
            atomic number        # protons

### amu
    1 proton = 1 amu

### mass
    - mass of the neucleus is really what matters for mass of
      atom

### isotope
    - an element with a different number of _neutrons_ (but
      same number of protons)

### atomic weight of carbon
    - 12.0107
    -     C
    - 6
    - why the funny fraction? 0.0107?
    - well theres two types of carbon :
        - `12 C_6`
        - `14 C_6`
    - and the atomic weight is just the weighted _average_
    of both types (more skewed towards the more common
    carbon 12)

### electrons
    - hard to even describe them as particles.
    - Hard to say where and how fast they are travelling.
    - (probability cloud)

### volume
    - Nucleus is 1/10,000 the volume of the atom!
    - Most of the space in an atom is free vacuum space, you
      could go straight through it.
    - They look solid because of the way light reflects from
      them.

### size
    - an atom is about 1 Angstrom (100,000 femto metres)
    - the Nucleus is 1 fm!


### electron
    - best described as a _smear_ around the nucleus
    - (remember heisenbergs uncertainty principle)

### order
    - electron orbitals (energy levels) are filled from _low
      energy_ to high energy.
    - Think: tetris bricks filled from ground up with more bricks
      on top.
    - So each orbital is an energy shell.

### 1 S orbital
    - a sphere around the nucleus where the electron will be
      found 90% of the time.
    - note: every-so-often an electron can show up totally
      outside of the orbital sphere.

### more energy
    - an electron in a wider/bigger orbital has more energy

### intuition
    - you've got 2 electrons in the lowest energy orbital,
      closest to the nucleus. You want to add a 3rd electron.
    - Why doesn't it go beside the 2 electrons in the S orbital?
    - Well its attracted by the nucleus but repelled by the
      2 electrons already there.

### high energy states
    - are further away from the nucleus, so the Coulomb force is
      weaker and they're easier to pluck off.

### orbitals
    - have two aspects:

    1. shell n=1,2,3... corresponds to (period) row in the
       periodic table

### period 1 - electron cofiguration
    Hydrogen: `1s^1` (in the 1st period, 1 electron)
    Helium:   `1s^2` (in the 1st period, 2 electrons)

### period 2 - electron cofiguration
    (this uses the _aufbau_ principle)
    Lithium:  `1s^2 2s^1` (3 electrons altogether,
                           2 in 1st orbital shell,
                           1 in 2nd orbital shell)

### "p" orbitals
    - like 3 dumb-bells, (because of standing waves)
    - 1. one in x direction
    - 2. one in y direction
    - 3. one in z direction

### p orbital example
    Carbon: `1s^2    2s^2 2p^2` (6 electrons altogether)

### silicon example
    14 electrons
    Silicon: `1s^2   2s^2 2p^6   3s^2 3p^2`


### idea
    - the `s` subshells always spherical, but the `d`, `f` shells
      etc. have mad shapes like dumbbells...

### subshells
    - s, p, d and f subshells
    - Khan says that the further out from the nucleus, the more
      space is available to start playing around with these
      bizarre shaped orbitals

### columns
    - each column in the periodic table is a "group" or "block".
    - say columns 13 - 18 are the _p-orbital_ group

### D-block "back fill"
    - When you write out the electron configuration, for the
    _D BLOCK_ you take the energy shell _minus one_.
    e.g. outermost shells for Iron Fe: `4s^2 3d^6`
                                             |
                                             |
                                             |
                                             ---------> notice the 3

    Wrong: Iron Fe: `1s^2  2s^2 2p^6  3s^2 3p^6  4s^2 4d^6`
    Right: Iron Fe: `1s^2  2s^2 2p^6  3s^2 3p^6  4s^2 3d^6`

### valence
    - Iron has actually 2 valence electrons (in its outermost
      shell)
    - because although the 6 "D" electrons come after the 4s^2
      electonrs,
    - the "D" electrons backfill down into the 3rd shell...

### F block
    when backfilling the _F block_, you _subtract 2_ from
    the shell number.

### backfilling
    - so even though a certain group is backfilled into the
      previous period, they still represent electrons in
      a _higher energy_ state.
    - (because they have to be shoved in closer to the positive
      nucleus?)
    - but the actual valence electrons which are physically
      furthest from the nucleus are at a lower energy state(?)

### Big Rule
    - All atoms want to have _8 electrons_ in their outer shell
      (why? most stable energy state?)

    - e.g. postassium has 1 valence electron
           chlorine   has 7 valence electrons
    - guess what happens when you MIX them?!

### group 1
    - alkaline metals, all have 1 outer (valence) electrons
    - that they really want to give away, so they are all highly
      reactive, especially when you put them near group
      7 (halogens)

### Big Rule Exception
    - everything wants 8 on the outside except hydrogen and
      helium. (So we dont really say hydrogen is one of the
      alkaline metals even though its in group 1)

### metallic
    - when chemists talk about "metallic" nature, they're really
      talking about how bad certain elements want to _give away_
      electrons.

### alkaline metals (group 1)
    - very rarely found in their elemental state, because they
      always want to give away their single outer electron, so
      they're very reactive

### more groups
    - alkaline earth metals
        - (group 2)
        - has 2 valence electrons

    - transition metals
        - (groups 3-12)
        - includes iron, silver!
        - has 2 valence electrons

    - metals
        - (group 13)
        - has 3 valence electrons

    - nobel gases
        - righmost groups
        - has 8 outermost electrons
        - has 0 valence electrons


# trends

### ion
    - any atom or molecule (group of atoms)
    - where #protons not equal #electrons

    _cation_:
        positive ion: (# protons) > (# electrons)

    _anion_ :
        negative ion: (# protons) < (# electrons)

### ionisation energy (remove!)
    - energy required to _REMOVE_ an electron from the neutral
      state of that atom.
    - should really be called the "cation-isation energy!"

### low ionisation energy
    - the group 1 alkaline metals require very little energy to
      remove an electron
    - (cos they want to get rid of the electron anyway)
    - ⇒ _low_ ionisation energy

### high ionisation energy
    - the nobel gases don't want to change their electronic state
      at all
    - ⇒ _high_ ionisation energy

### moving DOWN the table
    - The ionisation energy _decreases_ because
    - the number of electrons increase
    - ⇒ the size of the electron clouds get bigger
    - ⇒ further away from the nucleus
    - ⇒ weaker Coulomb force
    - ⇒ easier to pull away an electron

### SECOND ionisation energy
    - energy required to remove the very next electron after
    - the first electron has been removed.
    - Now think about this:
    - some elements with the
    - * lowest ionisation energy,
    - * will have the highest 2nd ionisation energy.

    - e.g. Lithium (Li) -> remove 1 electron, then stable, its
      electron configuration looks like Helium! Now to remove
      a 2nd electron will now be very difficult, so lithium has
      a very high 2nd ionisation energy.


### covalent bonds
    sharing electrons
                                H           H
         .        .            ..           |
        :O:   + 2 H     =    : O :     or : O :
         .                     ..           |
                                H           H


### Electronegativity 1:
    - Linus Pauling
    - when you form a covalent bond,
    - one atom is going to hog the electrons more.
    - For the _O_ and the 2 _H_
    - the electrons stay around the O more, so the O has high
      electronegativity.

    - But the group 1 atoms (alkaline metals) dont really want
      their electron. So in covalent bonds, their electron doesnt
      spend much time around these atoms so they have low
      electronegativity.

### Electronegativity 2:
    - which atoms are more electro negative?
    - The ones over on the right hand side, that only need one or
      two electrons to reach the nobel gas configuration.

### nobel gases
    - dont form covalent bonds! (because they have a full
      complement of electrons already)

### Metallic Nature
    - For metals, I think of:
        - conduct electricity, shiny, can bend it, malleable
    - But in chemistry they mean:
        - willingness to give away electrons

    - so increasing metallic nature is down towards the bottom
      left of the periodic table. (only one valence electron and
      weaker Coulomb force)

### Atomic radius
    - as you go _down_ any one group, the size of the atom
      increases. (adding on more and more energy levels, more
      shells)

    - as you go _across_ any period (row), you keep adding more
      and more electrons to the same shell, but increase the
      number of protons, so the electrons actually get pulled
      inwards. so as you move right, size decreases...
