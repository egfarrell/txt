- Today we explore training and certification options in infosec.
- Exams are essential to establish a baseline of knowledge and
  skills. While not perfect, major boards (like SANS, CREST,
  OffSec) each have strengths and weaknesses. I've done CPSA,
  CRT, CCT-Web App, OSCP, and CISSP—so I admit my views are
  biased.
- Exams should be a minimum standard, not the measure of ability.
  They're vital early in one’s career but less so over time. I'll
  update this post as I find new providers.

### Exams vs Experience

- A LinkedIn post caught my attention—someone argued experience
  should outweigh certifications. They’d built similar labs,
  bypassed AVs, taught themselves mobile testing, and wanted
  a chance to prove themselves.

- I see two problems with this:
    1. Time: Exams like OSCP or CRT can be completed in a few
    days or over a weekend. CHECK status (via CREST) can help win
    contracts—well worth the effort.
    2. Recreating labs: If someone hasn't taken OSCP or CRT, how
    are they replicating the labs? Either they’ve seen leaked
    content (NDA violation) or are guessing, which undermines the
    credibility of the exams.

- This isn’t a critique of the individual's skills—they clearly
  have strong technical ability. But selling yourself well
  matters too.

### CREST

- https://www.crest-approved.org/
- CREST offers 15 exams across infosec roles, from beginner to
  advanced. Exams include a multiple-choice portion (closed-book)
  and a hands-on practical (open-book, using your own setup).
  Certifications are valid for \~3 years; renewal involves
  progressing to the next level.
- CREST offers no official training—just reading lists and
  syllabi. Students can access some training via Immersive Labs.
  CRT grants CHECK team member status; CCT qualifies you for team
  leader.

    - **Pros**
    * Strong question coverage and practical challenges.
    * No tooling restrictions—you can use your own setup.
    * Retaking every 3 years helps ensure testers stay up to
      date.

    **Cons**
    * Allegations of leaked exam content damage credibility.
    * The closed exam network can be limiting—missing tools may
      block progress. I resolved this with a custom Kali ISO, but
      that doesn’t cover everything.
    * CREST doesn’t provide comprehensive learning materials.
    * They could contribute to OWASP-style guidance or open-source testing content.

### OSCP (Offensive Security)

- OffSec certifications bundle labs and videos with practical
  exams. They’re the creators of Kali Linux and great for
  newcomers.

- Progression typically goes OSCP → OSCE/OSWP → OSEE/OSWE.
- I’ve done the OSCP, so won’t compare higher levels.

- However, OffSec certs aren’t NCSC-accredited and don’t qualify
  for CHECK status—this limits their value in the UK government
  sector.

* OSCP guides candidates through instructional videos and reading
  materials that cover common attack methodologies. Afterward,
  they move on to hands-on lab work using a provided toolkit. The
  process includes stages such as vulnerability scanning, basic
  web application testing, infrastructure exploitation, stack
  buffer overflows, and foundational report writing.

* The labs contain around 50 machines to attack, and the exam
  consists of 5 machines with varying difficulty. Each box
  includes two tokens and requires privilege escalation to root
  or equivalent. The lab network is segmented, requiring pivoting
  and understanding interdependencies between hosts.

* Later exams in the series build on this, adding advanced
  overflows, anti-malware evasion, and bypassing security
  controls.

### *The Good*

* Compared to CREST, OSCP is a clear winner for students. It
  includes videos, follow-along labs, and forums/IRC for support.
  It’s hands-on, structured around a satisfying loop of exploit
  → privilege escalation → repeat.

* With one year of CREST equivalency now available in the UK,
  OSCP becomes even more attractive.

* The exam challenges you with numerous boxes, many without
  walkthroughs. Hints are minimal, requiring creative thinking.

* Good mix of Windows and Linux machines. Anyone who’s worked
  through the labs and passed the exam should be reasonably
  capable in infrastructure testing.

* Bonus tip: The exam is relatively cheap to retake. It's worth
  attempting at the end of your lab time, even if you're unsure.
  It’s valuable experience and you might just pass on the first
  try.

### *The Bad*

* Content needs updating—some vulnerabilities are over-emphasized
  while others are underrepresented. Some boxes still reference
  outdated network elements.

* Example: the provided Kali image was still based on Kali 1,
  despite being long out of date during the Kali rolling release
  cycle.

* The flag-based system and box resets encourage brute-force,
  "spray and pray" tactics, which aren’t good habits for
  real-world engagements.

* There's limited emphasis on risk assessment—only the bugs that
  lead to code execution get points, discouraging comprehensive
  vulnerability discovery.

* Better would be scoring for identifying *all* exploitable
  issues on a host, not just those tied to flags.

### TIGER

- https://www.tigerscheme.org/

* Tiger Scheme is a legacy exam board similar to CREST, with
  exams featuring multiple choice, long-form, and practical
  components. Now defunct.

### SANS

* https://www.sans.org/

* SANS courses are intensive, week-long, instructor-led trainings
  followed by multiple choice exams. Content comes straight from
  the course guides.

* While some courses are good, the high cost (\~\$8,000) often
  outweighs the value. That money might be better spent on OSCP,
  books, or attending conferences like DEF CON.

* Generally not recommended for students until you're ready for
  the 600 or 700 series. Even if someone else pays, think about
  opportunity cost.

### ISC2 CISSP

* https://www.isc2.org/

* ISC2 offers certifications like CISSP, often described as "a
  mile wide, an inch deep." These aren't ideal for entry-level
  roles.

* The CISSP covers 8 domains: risk, asset security, identity,
  security testing, architecture, network, operations, and
  software security.

* Training options include classroom instruction or self-study
  using books like *Shon Harris’ All-in-One* and *11th Hour*
  guides.

* Mainly useful for clearing HR filters later in your career—not
  an essential early certification.


### CEH (Certified Ethical Hacker)

* https://www.eccouncil.org/programs/certified-ethical-hacker-ceh/

* CEH is widely criticized and can be seen as a negative on some
  job applications.

* It’s largely about memorizing a marketing-heavy tool list, then
  dumping that into a short multiple-choice exam.

* It’s cheap and might offer a basic intro for total beginners,
  but that’s about it.

* Some UK universities now include it in undergrad modules, which
  reflects its shallow depth.

### CompTIA

* https://www.comptia.org/certifications

* CompTIA certifications (Security+, Network+, etc.) are
  entry-level, multiple-choice exams.

* Like CEH, they’re useful for newcomers but offer limited
  hands-on value. Best seen as stepping stones.


### Immersive Labs

* https://www.immersivelabs.com/

* A browser-based platform delivering RDP access to cloud-hosted
  boxes. Unique in that each student gets their own instance,
  preventing interference.

* Covers hundreds of labs across topics like OSINT, risk, and
  vulnerabilities. Free for .ac.uk emails.

* Labs include an info page, a list of questions, and a VM or
  file bundle. Gamified with points and leaderboards.

* Feels like university labs without the lectures. Great idea,
  but rough execution:

  * No persistence between labs (settings/history lost)
  * Awful web client (poor keyboard shortcut and clipboard support)
  * Question formatting issues can break progress
  * No hint system if you're stuck

* Still, as a free student resource, it’s worth trying.


### Project Ares

* https://circadence.com/project-ares/

* Similar to Immersive Labs, with browser access and gamified
  challenges. Uses Flash (which is a concern).

* Short trial use suggested less content and more fluff (e.g.,
  flashcards over technical depth).

### PentesterLab

* https://pentesterlab.com/

* Focuses on web app testing. Offers ISO-based labs and videos.
  Free and paid tiers (paid: \$20/month, with student discounts).

* The free tier is limited but useful. Heard good things about
  the paid version if you progress quickly.


### VulnHub / Hack The Box

* [HackTheBox](https://www.hackthebox.eu/)
* [VulnHub](https://www.vulnhub.com/)

* Both are CTF-style platforms. VulnHub offers downloadable
  community-made vulnerable VMs, while HTB provides a VPN-based
  lab environment.

* HTB closely mirrors OSCP-style labs. Both are excellent for
  long-term skills development once you're comfortable with basic
  exploitation.
