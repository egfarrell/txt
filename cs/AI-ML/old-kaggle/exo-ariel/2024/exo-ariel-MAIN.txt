### Сhanging of "white curve" over time outside the transit zone
    - 'gain noise'
    - https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/ariel-data-challenge-2024/discussion/533743#2988832
    - The trends observed in the light curve can be attributed to
      gain noise, a multiplicative effect on the signal. This
      noise may exhibit both temporal and chromatic correlations
      and can have various origins. Since this is a relative
      measurement, essentially anything that alters the measured
      flux can manifest as a multiplicative effect. Similar
      phenomena have been observed in various space missions,
      with amplitudes and patterns ranging from almost random to
      more structured. The JWST also experiences these effects,
      though generally with lower amplitudes compared to the
      simulations presented here.
    - This type of noise can include both a random component,
      such as electronic fluctuations, and a part that can be
      modelled using instrumental models. A notable example is
      the HST's scanning mode, where such effects are identified
      and corrected through dedicated models.

    - Another question:
        - Thank you for your question! It seems there might be
          some confusion between the concepts of "trend" and
          "jitter."
        - The drift of the source across the focal plane can
          indeed cause a trend similar to what is observed in the
          light curve. This type of drift typically results from
          a gradual pointing shift in the telescope over several
          hours, leading to a slow change in the measured signal.
          When the star moves across the detector, it passes over
          different pixels (or parts of them), altering the
          quantum efficiency that converts light into electrons.
          This introduces a multiplicative effect, which indeed
          falls under the definition of "gain noise" or "gain
          drift."
        - However, jitter is a different phenomenon. It is
          a high-frequency effect caused by vibrations in the
          telescope and the corrective actions of the pointing
          system as it tries to recenter the object. Jitter
          manifests as rapid, small-scale variations, unlike the
          slower "trend" caused by drift, as highlighted in these
          examples.
            - Followup question:
            - 1) differences in quantum efficiency: wont these be
              corrected by the flat field corrections applied?
            - 2) jitter vibrations: do these show up on the
              spectrogram in such a way that same wavelength
              lights are being resolved as different wavelengths?
              (perhaps due to the entering light rays not being
              normal to the prism?)
            - Thank you for your questions...
                - 1 ) Regarding the differences in quantum
                  efficiency:
                    - Yes, flat field corrections are applied to
                      account for variations in quantum
                      efficiency across the detector. However,
                      these corrections are never perfect—in
                      real-world scenarios, we can only achieve
                      the best possible estimate. Additionally,
                      the structure of each pixel plays a role.
                      Specifically, the quantum response is not
                      uniformly distributed across the pixel's
                      surface; it typically decreases towards the
                      edges. As a result, when the signal drifts
                      by fractions of a pixel, the measurements
                      vary depending on which areas of the pixel
                      are more or less illuminated. It's
                      important to note that this internal pixel
                      effect is not corrected by the flat field;
                      the flat field only accounts for relative
                      variations in quantum efficiency between
                      different pixels.
                - 2) Regarding jitter vibrations:
                    - You're absolutely right. The "blurring"
                      effect introduced by the PSF (Point Spread
                      Function) due to these vibrations means
                      that the collected light during an
                      integration is spread over more pixels (or
                      fractions of pixels) than if the source
                      were stationary. In a spectrometer, while
                      in the spatial direction this results in
                      a broader PSF, in the spectral direction it
                      means that when we extract the spectrum
                      based on the wavelength solution, there’s
                      contamination from neighbouring wavelengths
                      in each spectral bin. The extent of this
                      contamination depends on the amplitude of
                      the jitter. Part of the challenge is
                      decorrelating this effect, which is
                      difficult to trace because the jitter isn’t
                      necessarily the same in both directions.

### Jitter noise
    - https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/ariel-data-challenge-2024/discussion/532639
    - Question: I wondered if you could share any assumption
      about the jitter noise you model in these observations, in
      particular the expected rms or equivalent, is it the same
      accross all the observed transits etc. I would like to test
      some denoising models that require a bit of a priori
      knowledge on the noise sources in the data, and having more
      details on this would be great!
    - Answer: Pointing jitter on a space telescope arises from
      a variety of sources. To put it simply, it’s the result of
      a noninear interplay between solar wind, vibrations from
      the instruments and the efforts of the pointing system to
      stay locked onto the target. Therefore the noise’s power
      sprectrum is not flat but significantly non-Gaussian.
      Simulating this is quite challenging, as it largely depends
      on how the payload is constructed. If you're interested in
      exploring this topic from an academic perspective, there is
      some literature we can point you to. However, you’ll find
      that the impact and spectral characteristics of jitter
      varies significantly between space missions due to the
      unique design and components of each mission.
        - For this exercise, we used realistic jitter models for
          Ariel space mission provided by the manufacturer for
          the specific observed targets in question.
    - Question: 
        - thanks for your detailed answer- can you please clarify
          if the (pointing) jitter is of the same origin in the
          2 instruments in our data? From what I saw they seem to
          be independent- is this an effect of the used simulator
          or is this the expected behavior?
    - The pointing jitter originates from the same source for
      both instruments, as they are observing the same target
      simultaneously. However, the apparent independence you've
      noticed could be due to differences between the two
      detectors. While the physical pointing jitter is identical,
      each detector has its own characteristics, such as PSF
      shape, pixel quantum efficiency, and integration time,
      which influence how the jitter manifests in the final data.
        - So, the fact that the jitter seems different across the
          two instruments is not an effect of the simulator, but
          rather an expected behaviour due to the distinct
          properties of the detectors. The movement of the point
          source during integration is convolved with the
          different characteristics for each detector, leading to
          results that may appear independent.

### What is sigma
    - https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/ariel-data-challenge-2024/discussion/533608
    - If you calculate the partial derivative of the loss
      function by `sigma(planet, wavelength)` you'll find that it
      zeros for `sigma = y - mu`. 
    - So, the best sigma is simply the difference between the
      target and the prediction. From here we can see the
      meaning: 
        - sigma is the prediction, of how far our prediction for
          mu is from the target.

### Box Least Squares (BLS)
    - https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/ariel-data-challenge-2024/discussion/529533
    - How to identify the transit
    - The Box Least Squares (`BLS`) method is a statistical tool
      astronomers often use to detect transiting exoplanets. 
    - It looks for those periodic dips in a star's brightness
      that might indicate a planet passing by. Instead of getting
      too complex, `BLS` keeps it simple by fitting
      a straightforward box-shaped model to the light curve data.
    - The "box" here is just a way to represent the time period
      when the star’s light dims because the planet is crossing
      in front of it. It’s not perfect, but it’s been quite
      useful in helping us find small signals that might
      otherwise go unnoticed.


### Key Challenge
    - Observing these atmospheres and deriving their properties
      is a formidable challenge. These atmospheric signals only
      account for a minute fraction of the starlight received
      from the planetary systems, and are _regularly corrupted_
      by instrument noise.
    - Ariel 2024 focuses on overcoming these noise sources, like
      "jitter noise" caused by spacecraft _vibrations_. 
    - This noise, along with other disturbances, complicates the
      analysis of spectroscopic data used to study exoplanet
      atmospheres.
    - Scientists involved in the Ariel mission now seek novel
      methods to push the boundaries of current data analysis
      approaches: innovative solutions that can effectively
      SUPPRESS THESE NOISE SOURCES and extract vital signals from
      exoplanet atmospheres.

### Question About Target
    - https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/ariel-data-challenge-2024/discussion/528683
    - Is the target `(Rp/Rs)^2`, or `∆F/F`? 
    - Equivalently, is there an assumed model of _limb darkening_
      implied by the simulated train and test data?
    - Answer:
        - That’s an excellent question!
        - The TARGET in our simulations is `ΔF/F`, or the
          relative flux change during a transit, which
          corresponds to the transit depth. 
            - This is directly related to `(Rp/Rs)^2`, the square
              of the planet-to-star radius ratio. 
        - However, this relationship is perturbed by the
          contribution from the planet’s atmosphere.
        - For a simple reference, let’s start with the model
          presented in Sing (2018), eq. 15:
            - $MY_IMAGES/astro/exo/transit-depth-perturbed-atmosphere.jpg
        - This highlights how the relationship between ΔF/F and
          Rp/Rs is perturbed by the planet’s atmosphere. 
        - This perturbation changes with wavelength due to the
          physical and chemical properties of the exoplanet’s
          atmosphere. I won't go into further details here, as
          there are already suggested references that explain the
          science behind this more thoroughly.
        - For this particular exercise, we’ve chosen `not to
          include a limb darkening` model to avoid adding further
          complexity to the problem.

                  corresponds to the 356 different wavelengths at
                  which the AIRS-CH0 instrument measures light.
                - Position in the Data Cube: 
                    - In the data cube, AIRS-CH0-axis2-um maps to
                      the spectral dimension (X-axis), specifying
                      the wavelength (in micrometers) that each
                      of the 356 columns corresponds to.
        - 3. `AIRS-CH0-integration_time` 
            - `Temporal` Dimension
                - Represents the time dimension in the data cube.
                - 11,250 Time Steps: The first 11,250 rows in
                  AIRS-CH0_signal.parquet correspond to different
                  time steps during the observation.
                - Position in the Data Cube:
                    - `AIRS-CH0-integration_time` indicates the
                      duration for which each time step was
                      recorded. 
                    - Each time step in the data cube corresponds
                      to a different row in the
                      AIRS-CH0_signal.parquet file, with the
                      integration time INFLUENCING the signal
                      captured in each row.

    - Summary:
        - `AIRS-CH0-axis0-h`: Represents the spatial dimension (32
          pixels), indicating different positions along the
          spatial axis in the data cube.
        - `AIRS-CH0-axis2-um`: Represents the spectral dimension
          (356 wavelengths), indicating different wavelengths
          along the spectral axis in the data cube.
        - `AIRS-CH0-integration_time`: Represents the time
          dimension (11,250 time steps), indicating the

### Spectral Domain: more info
    - In this plot, the curve shows how the `planet-to-star`
      radius ratio (`Rp/Rs`) varies with wavelength. 
    - This variation is crucial because it provides insights into
      properties of the planet's atmosphere.

    - Rises (Bumps) in the Curve:
        - Physical Meaning: A rise or bump in the curve indicates
          the planet appears larger at a specific wavelength.
            - ie a bump means MORE light blocked.
        - Atmospheric Interpretation: 
            - `Absorption` Features: This typically occurs when
              specific gases or molecules in the planet's
              atmosphere strongly absorb light at that
              wavelength. 
                - Question: could be absorbed OR SCATTERED?
            - When light from the star passes through the
              planet's atmosphere during transit, certain
              wavelengths are absorbed more effectively, making
              the planet appear larger at those wavelengths.
            - Example: A bump could be due to the presence of
              water vapor (H2O), methane (CH4), or carbon dioxide
              (CO2) in the atmosphere. These molecules have
              specific wavelengths at which they absorb light,
              causing the planet-to-star radius ratio to increase
              at those wavelengths.

    - Dips in the Curve:
        - Physical Meaning: A dip in the curve indicates that the
          planet appears smaller at a specific wavelength.
            - i.e. a dip means LESS light blocked.
        - Atmospheric Interpretation: 
        - Less Absorption or `Scattering`: The star light is not
          absorbed or scattered by the planet atmosphere. The
          starlight streams through, making a shallower transit
          dip in the time domain and making the planet appear
          SMALLER.
        - Transparent Wavelengths: At these wavelengths, the
          atmosphere may be more transparent, allowing more
          starlight to pass through without being absorbed. This
          makes the planet's atmosphere less opaque, reducing the
          apparent size of the planet at those wavelengths.
        - Example: A dip might occur in regions of the spectrum
          where there is no significant absorption by any
          atmospheric molecules, or where the atmosphere is
          relatively clear, such as in the infrared for certain
          gases.

    - Overall Significance:
        - Atmospheric Composition: The bumps and dips across
          different wavelengths help scientists identify the
          specific gases and particles present in the atmosphere.
        - Each gas has a unique "fingerprint" in the form of
          absorption features at specific wavelengths.
        - Temperature and Pressure: The shape of the curve can
          also give clues about the temperature and pressure of
          the atmosphere, as these factors influence the
          absorption characteristics.
        - Clouds and Hazes: The presence of clouds or hazes in
          the atmosphere can smooth out or obscure some features,
          but they might also create their own distinct
          absorption or scattering effects.

    - Conclusion:
        - Bumps: Show strong absorption by specific atmospheric
          constituents at certain wavelengths, suggesting the
          presence of certain gases.
        - Dips: Indicate wavelengths where the atmosphere is more
          transparent, revealing an absence of strong absorbers
          at those wavelengths or regions where the atmosphere is
          clearer.

    - By analyzing these variations in the `spectral domain`,
      astronomers can infer the chemical makeup, temperature, and
      other properties of the exoplanet's atmosphere.

### Instrument Summary
    - The different number of rows in the `AIRS-CH0_signal` and
      `FGS1_signal` files is due to the differing _observation
      cadences_ and purposes of the two instruments:

    - FGS1 (Fine Guidance System):
        - Purpose: 
            - Mainly used for guiding, centering, and focusing
              the satellite, but also provides high-precision
              _photometry_ in the visible spectrum.
        - Higher Temporal Resolution: 
            - The FGS1 instrument is designed to capture rapid
              changes in light intensity, such as those occurring
              during a planetary transit. 
            - As a result, it takes measurements at much shorter
              time intervals, leading to a high number of
              observations. 
            - That's why there are 135,000 rows in
              `FGS1_signal.parquet`, each row representing
              a snapshot in time.

    - AIRS-CH0 (Ariel InfraRed Spectrometer - Channel 0):
        - Purpose: 
            - Captures the infrared spectrum of the exoplanet's
              atmosphere; provides info about the chemical
              composition and other atmospheric characteristics.
        - Lower Temporal Resolution: 
            - `AIRS-CH0` doesn't require as high a temporal
              resolution as the FGS1, so it captures fewer
              snapshots over the same observation period. This
              results in 11,250 rows in `AIRS-CH0_signal.parquet`


### AIRS-CH0_signal.parquet
    - `11,250 rows`: Each row corresponds to a single time step
      in the observation sequence. This means that the data
      captures 11,250 distinct moments during the observation of
      the exoplanet transit.
    - `32x356 image`: Each row originally represents an image
      that is 32 pixels high and 356 pixels wide. This image is
      a snapshot of the signal detected by the AIRS-CH0
      instrument at a specific time step.
    - `11,392 columns`: The original 32x356 image (`32 * 356
      = 11,392` pixels in total) has been "flattened" into
      a single row with 11,392 columns. Each column in this row
      represents the intensity or flux measured by one pixel in
      the original 32x356 image.

### FGS1_signal.parquet
    - `135,000 rows`: Each row corresponds to a single time step in
      the observation sequence. This means that the data captures
      135,000 distinct moments during the observation of the
      exoplanet transit.
    - `32x32 image`: Each row originally represents an image that
      is 32 pixels high and 32 pixels wide. This image is
      a snapshot of the signal detected by the FGS1 instrument at
      a specific time step.
    - `1,024 columns`: The original 32x32 image (which has 32 * 32
      = 1,024 pixels in total) has been "flattened" into a single
      row with 1,024 columns. Each column in this row represents
      the intensity or flux measured by one pixel in the original
      32x32 image.
    - `Reshaping`: The data can be reshaped back into its original
      32x32 image format using numpy.reshape(135000, 32, 32).
      This means you can convert each row of 1,024 columns back
      into a 32x32 image, and you would have 135,000 such images.



### 283 columns
    - Each of the 283 columns represents a specific wavelength in
      the spectrum of the exoplanet's atmosphere. When you
      predict the spectra, you're essentially predicting the
      intensity of light (or the flux) at each of these 283
      different wavelengths. These wavelengths correspond to
      different parts of the light spectrum that pass through the
      exoplanet's atmosphere, providing information about the
      atmospheric composition and conditions.

### Scoring, uncertainty, GLL
    - Indeed, if the participants are able to pinpoint exactly
      the transit depths (intensities) in the test set, the best
      strategy is to submit their network with 10ppm as
      uncertainties. This conclusion builds on the assumption
      that the model can predict the transit depths with a very
      high degree of accuracy. Given our understanding of the
      noise within the dataset, there will be information loss
      (due to the noise), which may prevent the model from
      predicting the transit depths perfectly (at least in the
      test set).
    - Also, note that 10ppm is a very narrow value for
      uncertainty, and their model will be heavily penalized if
      the predictions are _outside the uncertainty range_ (a
      characteristic of the GLL function), resulting in a worse
      score.
