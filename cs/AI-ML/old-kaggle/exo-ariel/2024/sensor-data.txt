### Understanding Calibration Data in Our Simulations
    - Important info about Readout noise, Dark frames, ADUs etc

    - Dark Frames vs Dark Current Calibration Products
        - In typical astronomical data processing, dark frames
          are images taken with the shutter closed, capturing
          only the inherent electronic noise of the detector,
          known as dark current. These dark frames are usually
          subtracted from the actual observation data to remove
          this unwanted noise.
        - However, in our case, the "dark frames" we provide are
          not traditional dark frames collected by a telescope.
          Instead, they are estimates of the dark current.
          Because of this, these dark current products need to be
          scaled according to the integration time of each frame
          before being subtracted from the observational data.
          This ensures that the dark current is accurately
          accounted for in relation to the exposure duration.

    - Flat Fields in Our Simulations
        - Similarly, flat fields in typical observations are used
          to correct for variations in the pixel-to-pixel
          sensitivity of the detector. Observationally, these are
          obtained by imaging a uniformly illuminated field. The
          data we provide, however, are not observational flat
          fields. Instead, they are estimates of the relative
          efficiency of each pixel in detecting light, which is
          why these "flats" have a mean value of one. Their
          purpose is to correct for variations in quantum
          efficiency across the detector, but because they are
          simulated, they might differ statistically from
          observational flats.

    - Uniformity of Calibration Products
        - In our data, the calibration products (darks, flats,
          etc.) are identical across frames. This is because
          we’ve simulated observations using the same detector
          consistently, which reflects real-life scenarios where
          different observations would be made with the same
          telescope. As a result, the same calibration products
          would be available throughout all observations.

    - Should I calibrate?
        - Calibration is crucial in the study of planetary
          transits to ensure precise measurements of the
          brightness variations. Since transit signals are very
          weak, with a required accuracy of about `100 ppm`, even
          small instrumental errors can introduce significant
          distortions.
        - E.g., if there is a constant additive signal `C`
          in the data, the transit depth, calculated as the
          difference of the out of transit signal and the in
          transit signal divided by the signal out of transit
          will be distorted.

        - Answer:
            - The data we provide are raw and in units of `ADU`
              (Analog-to-Digital Units).
            - ADU refers to the digital values generated by the
              detector in response to the light it collects. When
              light hits the detector, it creates a signal that
              is converted from an analogue signal (the voltage
              produced by photons hitting the detector) to
              a digital signal through an analogue-to-digital
              converter. 
                - These values are stored as integers because the
                  analogue-to-digital conversion process rounds
                  the signal to whole numbers for efficiency and
                  simplicity in storage. 
            - The ADU value represents the strength of this
              signal but has not yet been calibrated into
              physical units like flux (e.g., `mJy`).

### Meaning of dimensions in AIRS-CH0 images
    - I'm trying to understand what the `AIRS0-CH0` images
      represent. 
    - We are told they are `32 x 356` images.
    - According to this diagram the `356` sized dimension
      corresponds to different spectra. 
        - It also says that the slice `[39,321]` corrosponds to
          the spectra in `wavelengths.csv`. 
        - Is this correct? 
        - If so what is contained in the `[0, 39]` and `[321,
          356]` slices (spectra outside 1.95um-3.90um)?
    - Further what is the meaning of the length `32` dimension? 
        - IS IT SPATIAL INFORMATION OF SOME SORT?
        - YES: 32 pixels along the detectors spatial direction.
    - Answer: 
        - The `356-sized dimension` corresponds to different
          spectral channels as the light is dispersed
          horizontally, while the `32-sized dimension` represents
          the SPATIAL DIRECTION OF THE DETECTOR. 
        - Before the light is dispersed onto the detector, it is
          _filtered_ to ensure that the relevant wavelengths
          within the 1.95 µm to 3.90 µm range reach the detector.
          The slice [39, 321] specifically contains the spectra
          within this wavelength range, and therefore listed in
          wavelengths.csv.
        - The slices `[0, 39]` and `[321, 356]` represent regions
          of the detector that fall outside the 1.95 µm to 3.90
          µm band. These regions are considered "out-of-band"
          because the signal in these areas is attenuated by the
          filters.

    - In the context of a spectrometer, particularly for an
      instrument like the Ariel InfraRed Spectrometer (AIRS), the
      "spatial direction" of the detector refers to the way the
      instrument images the light it captures before it disperses
      it into a spectrum. Let's break this down:

    1. Spectrometer Basics:
        - A spectrometer disperses incoming light into its
          component wavelengths (creating a spectrum) which is
          then detected by a sensor.
        - The sensor records both the _spatial and spectral_
          information. The spectral information is usually
          represented along one axis (e.g., the 356 pixels
          corresponding to different wavelengths), while the
          spatial information is captured along the other axis.

    2. Spatial Dimension (32 Pixels):
        - The 32 pixels along the "spatial direction" likely
          correspond to a physical direction on the sky that the
          instrument is imaging.
        - Imagine the instrument is looking at a strip of the sky
          or the surface of a planet; it captures a small slice
          (or strip) of that image along one axis (this is your
          32 pixels).
        - The spectrometer then disperses the light from each of
          those 32 spatial pixels into a spectrum, resulting in
          the 356 different wavelengths (the spectral dimension).
        - Each row in the 32 x 356 image corresponds to the
          spectrum of light from one of those 32 spatial
          positions.

    3. Physical Interpretation:
        - The 32 pixels do not directly correspond to a specific
          width on the sky. Instead, they represent how the
          instrument spatially samples the incoming light.
        - It might be that the instrument is focused on
          a specific target, and these 32 pixels are recording
          different points across that target. For example, if
          AIRS is observing a planet or a star, the 32 pixels
          could correspond to different points across the
          planet's disk or along a strip of the star.

    4. Detector Layout:
        - The detector used in a spectrometer typically has a 2D
          array of pixels. One dimension of this array is aligned
          with the spatial direction (here, 32 pixels), while the
          other dimension is aligned with the spectral direction
          (here, 356 pixels).
        - After light from a specific region of the sky is
          dispersed into its spectrum, the spectrum from each
          spatial pixel is recorded along the spectral axis,
          giving you the full 32 x 356 image for each timestamp.

    5. In Summary:
        - The "32 pixels along the detectors spatial direction"
          means that the instrument is recording data from 32
          different spatial points (like a strip) on the sky (or
          target). Each of these points has a corresponding
          spectrum, which is recorded as 356 different wavelength
          measurements. The detector captures these 32 spatial
          positions simultaneously, and each of these spatial
          positions is dispersed into a spectrum. The result is
          a 32 x 356 pixel image representing both spatial and
          spectral information.

        - So, to answer your specific question: the "spatial
          direction" of the detector is the dimension that
          captures different points (or "pixels") across the
          object or field of view that the spectrometer is
          observing. This dimension of 32 pixels is not a literal
          strip on the sky but represents how the instrument
          spatially samples the incoming light before dispersing
          it into a spectrum.
