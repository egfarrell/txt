### Frequency range
    - note 1:
        - So, `Tsft` refers to the _duration_ over which
          a Fourier transform was taken (i.e. each timestamp
          contains 1800s of data on which a Fourier transform was
          taken). 
        - The 360 is the number of bins in the frequency axis
          (i.e. how many frequencies does your Fourier transform
          contain). 
        - Since _frequency resolution_ is 1/1800, then your data
          spans 360 * (1/1800) = 0.2 Hz, as you can see in the
          plots.

    note 2:
        - All provided SFTs have 360 frequency bins, and
          frequency range is 0.2Hz. So, `delta_f` is constant and
          it is equal to 1/1800 Hz.
        - There are gaps on the time axis of SFTs, because the
          detectors are not always online.

### SNR, depth advice
    - The amplitude distribution of injected signals is not
      provided, but you already have a hint of the range: 
        - one or two magnitudes lower
    - I interpret it as a general description since those are the
      expected ranges in real CW searches (I think). So I'd
      assume unknown SNR / depth.
    - If you generate data yourself and keep track of the depth
      (`sqrtSX / h0`) used in each positive sample, you'll see
      that image classifiers have trouble with deeper signals
      (obviously), and it's a matter of trying to go as deep as
      possible. 
    - Furthermore, since the train data contains 400 positive
      samples and _none have real non-stationary noise_ or any
      other glitches, you can look at your CV AUC score with
      generated data versus your AUC obtained from the kaggle
      train set, and infer a bit how deep the signals are there. 
    - By the way, the test set seems to be even harder in terms
      of depth, even for the samples with generated noise.

### Labels (SNR definition)
    - `0/1` labels refer to whether we included a simulated
      signal to that sample (1) or if it consists only on noise.
    - In terms of the quantities of the kernel your refer to,
        - a label of `1` would correspond to a signal with an
          amplitude `h0 > 0` (i. e. there's a signal), 
        - while a label of 0 would correspond to not having
          a signal (i.e. amplitude `h0 = 0`).
    - The same definition can be made in terms of _SNR_: 
        - Label of 1 corresponds to SNR > 0, 
        - label of 0 corresponds to SNR = 0 
            - (i.e. no signal added at all).

### Timestamps and Frequencies Insights
    - The data provided consists of short-time Fourier Transforms
      (SFTs), but there seems to be 2 differences with
      conventional SFT's.

    - 1) The _time delta_ between each timestamp is
      _inconsistent_, meaning the time between measurement in the
      SFT's can vary greatly.
        - Besides the unknown unit of the timestamps, the time
          delta in several samples examined varies between 33.0
          and 48648 seconds, with ~91% of time deltas being 1800
          (30 minutes).
        - How could we handle these inconsistent time deltas?
    - 2) The frequency range is consistently ~0.2Hz, however the
      min/max frequency ranges from around ~50to ~498Hz.
        - As the frequency range is consistent between SFT's
          I expect the varying min/max frequency can be ignored,
          how do you feel about this?
    - 3) The timestamps start at:
            - 2009-03-27/28 and end on
            - 2009-07-25/26/27.
        - The recordings are thus gathered during 4 months with
          often 30 minutes between the recordings. 
        - We should thus not interpret the spectrograms as
          continuous sounds, which is conventionally the case.
    - Answer:
        - Timestamps are GPS time, which started ~10 years after
          UNIX time (i.e, data starts at 2019 rather than at
          2009).

### PyFstat Signal Parameters
    - https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/g2net-detecting-continuous-gravitational-waves/discussion/361562
    - As explained in the tutorials, you can use a helper
      function to inject some parameters based on priors:

    - {pyfstat.injection_parameters.isotropic_amplitude_priors} {
        - 'cosi': {'uniform': {'low': -1.0,                'high': 1.0}},
        - 'psi':  {'uniform': {'low': -0.7853981633974483, 'high': 0.7853981633974483}},
        - 'phi':  {'uniform': {'low': 0,                   'high': 6.283185307179586}}}

    - These are the valid ranges for `cosi`, `psi` and `phi`,
      which are expected to be _isotropically distributed_
      (uniform distribution, no preference for any particular
      value).

    - `cosi`
        - Cosine of the angle between the source and us.
        - Range: [-1, 1]
    - `psi` and `phi`
        - polarization angle and phase.
        - Ranges: [-pi/4*, pi/4*] and [0, 2*pi]
    - `Alpha`
        - Right ascension of the source's position on the sky.
        - Range: [0, 2*pi]
    - `Delta`
        - Declination of the source's position on the sky.
        - Range: [-pi/2, pi/2]
    - `Band`
        - This is just the width of the frequency "slice" to be
          taken. It's _0.2 hz_ for all files in the train and
          test datasets, which translates to `360` "frequency
          lines".
    - `F0`
        - This may be tricky. The generated noise will be
          centered around this frequency.
        - If a signal is created, it will be also a signal with
          this frequency (at the source; when it arrives to the
          detector, doppler and other effects will shift the
          frequency), so it will appear more or less on the
          center of the data.
        - Range used on the datasets: [50, 500] Hz.
    - `F1`
        - This is technically the first derivative of the
          frequency (how it changs over time).
        - Physically, the sources (neutron stars) lose energy
          over time because they emit the GWs, so this should be
          a negative number.
        - A reasonable range could be [-1e-9, 0] Hz but that's up
          to us.
    - `F2`
        - This is guess it's the second derivative, which maybe
          is `0` for the purposes of this competition.
    - `h0`
        - The amplitude of the signal.
        - In the tutorials, the approach is to define it as
          a ratio of the `sqrtSX`.
        - What makes the competition (and the real-world problem
          of finding continuous GWs) is that expected `h0` will
          be between 1 and 2 orders of magnitude smaller than the
          amplitude of the noise.
        - In my opinion, part of the challenge is to create your
          own signals with a _distribution of h0_ similar to the
          test set.

    - Regarding other parameters you mentioned like `yp`,
      `asini`, `period`, I think they are used as alternative
      ways of specifying the pyhsical parameters of the source
      and you can ignore them:
        - Depending on the emission mechanism, `h0` can be
          further described using further physical quantities
          such as the source's frequency, ellipticity, or
          distance to the detector.

    - Rodrigo Tenorio Competition Host
    - If I could just add the following:
        - `psi` is actually defined within [-pi /4 , pi / 4] (and
          it's periodic, so anything beyond that folds back onto
          this range).
        - `F2` is another term describing the intrinsic evolution
          of a gravitational wave. As @darkbitur correctly
          guesses, it's set to `0` in this competition as we only
          focus on frequency and spindown.
        - `tp`, `asini`, `period` are parameters used to describe
          neutron stars in _binary systems_, and they affect the
          frequency evolution of the signal.
            - You can safely ignore those in this competition
              (but do feel free to play around with them if you
              wish to!).

        - Depending on the emission mechanism, `h0` can be
          further described using physical quantities such as the
          source's frequency, ellipticity, or distance to the
          detector.
            - As for this, we normally don't worry about the
              physics at search time: We can safely encapsulate
              all that information on `h0`, as that would be what
              our detectors would basically measure
                - (gory details: the signal is actually amplitude
                  modulated depending on `psi` and `Alpha`,
                  `Delta`, and then `cosi` acts as an overall
                  scaling on top).
                - Once the search is done and we get an upper
                  bound of `h0` we work out the physical
                  consequences of that
                    - (e.g. how far away could we have seen
                      a star with a given ellipticity, how much
                      ellipticity can nearby NS sustain…).

        - In order to achieve a model that is agnostic to the

          convolutional architecture. This means there are no
          fully connected (or dense) layers.

          the size of their input data.

    - To avoid large computational cost for the training, we want

      trained over the full frequency band compares to “special-

    - On the other hand our architecture searches did not yet

        - $MY_BOOKS/Astro/DSP/Scientist-Engineers-Guide-to-DSP-2e.pdf
        - Chapters 19 and 20 show how to design recursive filters

          custom?
            - The answer is to design a recursive filter just as
              you would a neural network: 
                - start with a generic set of recursion
                  coefficients, and use iteration to slowly mold
                  them into what you want.
            - This technique is important for two reasons. 
                - First, it allows custom recursive filters to be
                  designed without having to hassle with the
                  mathematics of the z-transform.
                - Second, it shows that the ideas from
                  conventional DSP and neural networks can be
                  combined to form _superb algorithms_.

    - Fourier Gaps?:
        - Fourier transforms are taken over _continuous
          segments_. For every timestamp, you can assume there's
          a continuous span of time on which we applied the
          Fourier transform. 
        - Whenever we hit a gap, we stop and wait until we have
          again enough data to compute another Fourier transform.

    - Yes, each timestamp labels a period of time of _1800s_ 30
      mins (you can check that by noting the frequency resolution
      of 1/1800 Hz)
        - But timestamps need not to be consecutive
        - (i.e. there may be times at which no data is
          collected).

    - Parameters
        - like `F0`, `F1`, `Alpha`, `Delta` affect the
          _frequency_ evolution of your signal
        - whereas parameters like `cosi`, `phi`, `psi` affect the
          _amplitude_ of the signal.
        - Nice visualisation: https://www.glowscript.org/#/user/grahamwoan/folder/Public/program/gwgw

    - _Loud instrumental features_ in the data tend to saturate
      detection statistics due to their strong resemblance to CW
      signals along short periods of time.
        - This problem spurred the development of methods capable
          of suppressing narrow-band features in the data, such
          as the application of the Hough transform to the search
          for CW signals.

    - When we talk about SNR, we refer to the SNR of a specific
