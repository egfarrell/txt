### Learning v knowledge
    - "Richard Sutton – Father of RL thinks LLMs are a dead end"
    - https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=21EYKqUsPfg
    - I think the distinction here is between `knowledge` and
      `learning`. Learning is distinct from knowledge.
        - You can use learning to gain knowledge, but you can't
          knowledge your way to learning (or at least we don't
          really have great examples of that, maybe it's
          possible).
    - In other words knowledge is a thin veneer that sits on top
      of a way more complex behavior called "learning". Learning
      is both what produces knowledge and what consumes it.
      Learning is something all organisms do and it guides
      behavior. The hack humans have found is to transfer that
      learning through knowledge. We can encode knowledge and
      transfer it from generation to generation, and that has
      created an exponential, an accumulation of knowledge to
      bootstrap learning. That is "schooling" or by any other
      means of transmitting information. However that is only an
      accumulation of knowledge, that uses the tools of learning
      to learn. But is not learning itself.
    - In other words knowledge is not the means/tool through
      which we learn, it is the thing we consume. The tool we're
      using to learn is something different entirely.
    - LLMs are then the component that is the accumulator of
      knowledge. It's able to parse knowledge, retrieve
      knowledge, and even modify knowledge, but it is unable to
      actually learn. That component is still missing in the
      intelligent machines.
    - This is similar to how language works for humans as well.
      We're able to use language to structure sensory
      information/information produced by learning.
    - So basically the real question here is what are you using
      to learn? What instruments are you using to learn? And this
      distinction is subtle to what learning is using to learn.
      That is we're talking about the activity of learning itself
      in contrast to the things learning uses to learn.
    - A windmill uses the wind to turn and produce some amount of
      work. The question here is not about the wind, but about
      the thing that is able to capture the wind and change its
      behavior.

### Why LLMs hallucinate
    - https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45147385
    - They hallucinate because it's an ill-defined problem with
      two conflicting usecases:
        - 1. If I tell it the first two lines of a story, I want
          the LLM to complete the story. This requires
          hallucination, because it has to make up things. The
          story has to be original.
        - 2. If I ask it a question, I want it to reply with
          facts. It should not make up stuff.
    - LMs were originally designed for (1) because researchers
      thought that (2) was out of reach. But it turned out that,
      without any fundamental changes, LMs could do a little bit
      of (2) and since that discovery things have improved but
      not to the point that hallucination disappeared or was
      under control.

### Difference: ML / DL
    1. Machine Learning (ML) is a broad field involving training
       computers to find patterns in data and make predictions or
       decisions without being explicitly programmed.
        - Traditional ML has algorithms like:
        - Linear Regression
        - Decision Trees
        - Random Forest
        - Support Vector Machines (SVM)
        - k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN)

    2. Deep Learning (DL) is a *subset* of ML specifically using
       neural networks with multiple layers (hence "deep").
       Unlike traditional ML, DL models automatically learn
       feature representations from raw data, which makes them
       particularly powerful for complex tasks like:
       - Image recognition (e.g., CNNs)
       - Natural language processing (e.g., Transformers, RNNs:
         Recurrent Neural Networks)
       - Autonomous driving and reinforcement learning

    - ### Are Neural Networks ML or DL?
        - Neural networks in general are part of `ML`.
        - Deep neural networks (DNNs) (which have multiple hidden
          layers) fall under `DL`.
        - A simple neural network (e.g. only one hidden layer)
          could still be considered ML, but when it becomes deep
          (multiple layers), it moves into the DL category.

    - So, all DL is ML, but not all ML is DL—DL is just a more
      advanced and data-hungry subset of ML!

### It's all just Curve fitting?
    - https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=41732853
    - "Interesting work on reviving RNNs.
      https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01201 -- the fact that many
      recent architectures coming from different directions
      roughly match `Transformers` is proof that architectures
      aren't fundamentally important in the curve-fitting
      paradigm (aka deep learning)
    - Curve-fitting is about embedding a dataset on a curve. The
      critical factor is the dataset, not the specific hard-coded
      bells and whistles that constrain the curve's shape. As
      long as your curve is sufficiently expressive all
      architectures will converge to the same performance in the
      large-data regime."
    - Reply:
        > The critical factor is the dataset, not the specific
        > hard-coded bells and whistles that constrain the
        > curve's shape
        - I have almost the opposite take. We've had a lot of
          datasets for ages, but all the progress in the last
          decade has come from advances how curves are
          architected and fit to the dataset (including applying
          more computing power).
        - Maybe there's some theoretical sense in which older
          models could have solved newer problems just as well if
          only we applied 1000000x the computing power, so the
          new models are 'just' an optimisation, but that's like
          dismissing the importance of complexity analysis in
          algorithm design, and thus insisting that bogosort and
          quicksort are equivalent.
        - When you start layering in normalisation techniques to
          minimise overfitting, and especially once you start
          thinking about more agentic architectures (eg. Deep
          Q Learning, some of the search space design going into
          OpenAI's o1), then I don't think the
          just-an-optimisation perspective can hold much water at
          all - more computing power simply couldn't solve those
          problems with older architectures.
    - Reply
        > One big thing that bells and whistles do is limit the
        > training space.
        - For example, when CNNs took over computer vision it
          wasn't because they were doing something that dense
          networks couldn't do. It was because they removed a lot
          of edges that didn't really matter, allowing us to
          spend our training budget on DEEPER NETWORKS.
        - Similarly transformers are great because they allow us
          to train gigantic networks somewhat efficiently. And
          this paper finds that if we make RNNs a lot faster to
          train they are actually pretty good. Training speed and
          efficiency remains the big bottleneck, not the actual
          expressiveness of the architecture

### RAG "Retrieval-Augmented Generation"
    - It's a model architecture used in NLP tasks, combining the
      capabilities of two major types of models:
        - a retriever model
        - and a generator model.
    - Retriever Model: This component is responsible for fetching
      relevant context or information from a large database or
      corpus of documents. It retrieves documents that are likely
      to contain useful information for generating a response or
      output.
    - Generator Model: After retrieval, the generator model,
      often a large language model like GPT or BERT, uses the
      retrieved documents along with the original query to
      generate a coherent and contextually relevant response or
      output.
    - RAG models are very effective in tasks where additional
      context or specific knowledge not contained within the
      model itself is needed to generate accurate and
      contextually rich responses. Examples include open-domain
      question answering and fact-based content creation.
    - The combination of retrieval and generation allows these
      models to leverage vast amounts of information beyond their
      training data, enabling more accurate and informed
      responses in complex tasks.

### "embedding"
    - "looking up" something in an array
    - An embedding matrix in deep learning is a powerful concept
      used to transform discrete data, like words in text
      processing, into continuous vectors. Think of it as
      a translation tool: it converts categorical, often
      symbolic, data into a format that neural networks can
      understand and process more effectively.
    - Here's the kicker: each unique item (like a word) gets
      mapped to a high-dimensional vector. These vectors capture
      more than just the identity of the items; they encode
      relationships and semantic meanings. For example, in word
      embeddings, words with similar meanings tend to have
      vectors that are close to each other in the embedding
      space.
    - The beauty of embedding matrices lies in their ability to
      learn these representations during the training of a model,
      making them context-aware. This isn't just about turning
      words into numbers; it's about capturing the essence of
      those words in a mathematical space. This is crucial for
      tasks like natural language processing, where understanding
      context and meaning is key.

### gradient boosting
    - use a tiny tree (not many splits)
    - residue: then calculate the difference between prediction and
      actual (the residiue)
    - then train another tiny tree to predict that residue (error)
    - then calculate the residue again
    - train another tree to predict that, keep going etc
    - at the end, take the `SUM` of all the trees (not the mean of
      all the trees like in 'bagging')
    - because one predicts the difference between the ACTUAL and
      all the previous trees...
    - so 'boosting' vwesus 'bagging' as a meta-ensemble technqiue
    - bagging trees: Random Forest
    - boosting trees: Gradient Boosting Machine
    - Generally gradient bossting is more accurate than random
      forests, but you can over-fit

### "OneR" (One Rule)
    - a decision tree with a single binary split
    - in the 1990s was the best performing model(!)
    - e.g. titanic dataset:
        - split on `female (0)` or `male (1)` (i.e. a binary
          split) to predict survival
    - The OneR method involves selecting the single rule that
      performs the best across a dataset for creating a binary
      split. It's a basic yet surprisingly effective technique,
      especially useful for understanding the fundamental
      behavior of a dataset. The algorithm scans through each
      attribute in the data, evaluating how well it can classify
      the data based on that single attribute. It then selects
      the attribute with the best classification accuracy.
    - OneR is favored for its simplicity and interpretability,
      which is a contrast to more complex models like random
      forests or neural networks. However, its simplicity can be
      a limitation, as it might not capture complex patterns in
      data as effectively as more sophisticated algorithms.

### Etymology: Regularization
    - "Regularization" for ML is rooted in mathematics,
      specifically the field of _ill-posed problems_ and
      _inverse_ problems.
        - In many mathematical problems, one often wants to
          "invert" a process. i.e., given some `output`, one
          tries to determine the possible `input`.
        - But not all problems are "well-posed" (introduced by
          Jacques Hadamard). A well-posed problem is where
          a solution exists, is unique, and changes continuously
          with the initial data. Problems that don't meet these
          criteria are "ill-posed."
        - Inverse problems are typically ill-posed. E.g. consider
          deducing an image (like a medical MRI) from a set of
          measurements. Small errors in the measurements might
          lead to wildly different images, making the problem
          ill-posed.
        - One way to address ill-posed problems: introduce
          additional info or constraints to make them well-posed.
          This is often done by seeking solutions that are not
          just consistent with the data but also have some
          "smoothness" or simplicity properties.
        - This process of adding constraints or additional
          criteria to _stabilize the solution_ of an ill-posed
          problem is called "regularization."
    - For ML, the idea is analogous. Complex models esp. DL can
      overfit the training data (learn noise and fluctuations).
      This is similar to an ill-posed problem where many
      solutions (models) can fit the data.
    - Regularization techniques introduce constraints to prefer
      simpler or _smoother models_, analogous to mathematical
      regularization which seeks smoother or simpler solutions.
    - ML "regularization" essentially borrows this concept from
      Maths, emphasizing the adding of constraints or _penalties_
      to "regularize" or stabilize the learning process and
      prevent overfitting.

### regularization
    - "Regularization" are techniques to prevent OVERFITTING.
    - Overfitting occurs when a model performs super on training
      data but poorly on unseen or test data.
    - This is because the model may have learned the training
      data's _noise_ or _fluctuations_ instead of the underlying
      distribution.
    - Regularization adds a penalty on the complexity of the
      model, encouraging it to be simpler, which can lead to
      better generalization to new data.
    - 'Standford' slides:
        - $MY_IMAGES/cs/AI-ML/computer-vision/svm-regularisation.jpg
        - $MY_IMAGES/cs/AI-ML/computer-vision/types-of-regularisation.jpg

    - Common regularization techniques:

    1. _L1 Regularization (Lasso Regression)_:
        - Adds a penalty equal to the absolute value of the
          coefficients magnitudes.
        - Can lead to some feature coefficients becoming exactly
          zero, effectively leading to FEATURE SELECTION.

    2. _L2 Regularization (Ridge Regression)_:
        - Adds a penalty equal to the square of the coefficient
          magntitudes.
        - Keeps all features but can reduce the magnitude of
          coefficients for features that are not informative.

    3. _Elastic Net_:
        - A combination of L1 and L2 regularization.
        - Useful if many _correlated_ features in the dataset.

    4. _Dropout (specifically for NNs)_:
        - During training, randomly selected neurons are ignored
          (i.e., their outputs are set to zero).
        - This means that during each iteration of training,
          a slightly different model is used, which can prevent
          overfitting and co-adaptation of neurons.

    5. _Early Stopping_:
        - Training is stopped as soon as the performance on
          a validation set starts to degrade, even if the
          training set error is still decreasing.
        - This prevents the model from learning the noise in the
          training set.

    6. _Batch Normalization_:
        - Normalize the activations of the neurons in a layer for
          each mini-batch.
        - This can have a slight regularization effect, as the
          _distribution_ of inputs to layers inside the neural
          network can shift slightly for each mini-batch.

    7. _Weight Decay_:
        - A form of L2 regularization during the update of the
          weights in NNs, where a small proportion of the weight
          values is subtracted, pushing the weights towards zero.

    8. _Noise Injection_:
        - Noise is added to the inputs or in the network itself,
          which can prevent over-reliance on any particular input
          and improve generalization.

    9. _Data Augmentation (common in DL for images)_:
        - Training data is artificially increased by applying
          various transformations (rotations, translations,
          zooming) on the original images.
        - Provides more varied examples for the model to learn
          from, effectively regularizing the model.

    - The choice of regularization method and its strength (often
      controlled by a hyperparameter, like `lambda` in L1 and L2
      regularization) depends on the specific problem, dataset,
      and the nature of overfitting observed.


### L1 and L2 Norms in Regularization
    - "L1" and "L2" in the context of regularization refer to the
      mathematical `norms` used to compute PENALTY TERMS.
    - Both norms are ways to measure the "size" or "magnitude" of
      vectors. In regularization, the vectors are typically the
      WEIGHT VECTORS of a model.

    #### L1 Norm
    - Also known as the _Manhattan norm_ or _taxicab norm_.
    - It is the sum of the absolute values of the components of
      a vector.
    - For a vector `v = [v_1, v_2, ..., v_n]`, the L1 norm is
      calculated as: ``` ||v||_1 = |v_1| + |v_2| + ... + |v_n|
      ```
    - L1 regularization adds the L1 norm of the weights as
      a PENALTY to the loss function.

    #### L2 Norm
    - Also known as the _Euclidean norm_.
    - It is the square root of the sum of the squares of the
      components of a vector.
    - For a vector `v = [v_1, v_2, ..., v_n]`, the L2 norm is
      calculated as:
    - ```
    - ||v||_2 = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2 + ... + v_n^2}
    - ```

    - `L2` regularization adds the squared `L2` norm of the
      weights as a penalty to the loss function.

    - The naming convention comes from the general _p-norms_ (or
      Lp norms) in mathematics, where:
    - For `p=1`, we have the L1 norm, and for  `p=2`, the L2
      norm. Other values of `p` define other norms, but in
      machine learning regularization, L1 and L2 are the most
      commonly used.

### Normalization
    - Normalization Layers:
        - Imagine you're preparing a cake recipe. You need to mix
          ingredients: flour, sugar, eggs, and butter. However,
          each ingredient might have different properties. E.g.,
          flour might have different brands or types, some with
          finer grains, others coarser.
        - Normalization in the context of NNs is like ensuring
          all your cake ingredients are the right consistency and
          in the right proportion. It's a way to ensure
          everything works together.
    - Batch Normalization:
        - Take 'normalization' a step further. Think of training
          a NN like teaching a group of students a new skill,
          like playing an instrument. Each student learns at
          a different pace, and they might have different levels
          of talent. Some become skilled quickly, while others
          need more time.
        - Batch normalization is like ensuring all students
          progress at a _similar rate_ during their learning.
          Imagine you're teaching them to play a musical piece,
          and you want them all to reach the same level of
          proficiency. You might:
            1. Regularly assess their progress (after every
            practice session) to determine how each student is
            doing.
            2. If one student is progressing faster than the
            others, you might ask them to slow down a bit so that
            everyone can catch up.
            3. If one student is struggling and progressing
            slowly, you might provide extra guidance or practice
            to help them improve faster.
    - In essence, batch normalization does something similar for
      NNs during training. It keeps the activations (the outputs
      of neurons) in each layer of the network at a similar
      scale. It does this by examining how the activations change
      during each batch of training data and adjusting them as
      needed.
    - This helps the network train more smoothly and converge
      faster, similar to ensuring all your students learn to play
      music at a similar pace. It's like fine-tuning the learning
      process to make it more efficient and effective.
    - In summary, normalization layers ensure the "ingredients"
      in your NN work together smoothly, and batch normalization
      is a technique to ensure the "students" (neurons) in your
      network progress at a similar rate during training, helping
      the network learn better and faster.

### Embeddings
    - Think of embeddings in DL networks as a way to translate
      something complex or abstract, like words, into a format
      the computer can manipulate more easily.
    - Imagine you're a chef and you have a recipe book written in
      a foreign language you don't understand. You want to cook
      a dish from that book, but you need to figure out what each
      ingredient is and how much of it to use. So, you decide to
      create a special page in your recipe book where you write
      down a list of ingredients and next to each one, you put
      a picture of that ingredient. This way, even if you don't
      understand the language, you can simply look at the
      pictures to know what you need.
    - In this analogy, the foreign language is like the words or
      concepts in a neural network, and the pictures of
      ingredients are like the embeddings. The embeddings take
      something abstract and turn it into a more concrete and
      numerical representation that the neural network can
      process. So, instead of dealing with raw words, the network
      works with these embeddings to understand and learn
      patterns in the data.
    - Embeddings make it easier for neural networks to work with
      complex information, just like the pictures of ingredients
      make it easier for you to cook from a foreign-language
      recipe book. They help the network grasp the meaning and
      relationships between words, which is crucial for tasks
      like natural language processing and understanding.

### Ah STOCHASTIC gradient descent!!!
    - $MY_NOTES/cs/AI-ML/3blue1brown-neural-nets.txt
    - To calculate the _exact proper_ gradient descent, we'd have
      to use all the training data each time we calculate the
      gradient.. but that's COMPUTATIONALLY EXPENSIVE...
    - So we _RANDOMLY SPLIT_ (stochastic) the data into
      `mini-batches` and calculate the negative gradient for each
      smaller batch...
        - much better computationally, but because we're only
          operating on batches of data each time, we don't
          actually compute the most "efficient" descent...
    - So we're like a 'drunk man' coming down the hill in wobbly
      steps, rather than the most precise, efficent, direct route
      down the hill (if we were using ALL the data for each
      negative gradient calculation)

        - (1) Build a model using training data.
        - (2) Predict labels for an unseen test dataset.

### Learning step size
    - $MY_BOOKS/CS/AI-ML/2016-Make-Your-Own-Neural-Network.pdf
        - $MY_IMAGES/cs/AI-ML/learning-step-size.jpg
    - We should moderate how much we nudge the value of `c`:
        - If the outputs are getting close to the correct answer,
          (the error is getting smaller), _then don’t nudge the
          changeable bit so much_.
        - That way we avoid overshooting the right value, like we
          did earlier.
    - Without getting distracted by the exact way of calculating
      `c`, and to remain focussed on the idea of successively
      refining it, we could suggest that the _correction is
      a fraction of the error_.
    - That’s intuitively right:
        - a big error means a bigger correction is needed, and
        - a tiny error requires the teeniest of nudges to `c`.

        - So we're looking at a lot of _spurious interactions_
          and this clouds our thinking.
        - In fact the `network topology` is what's driving
          network function here, but we're not doing a good job
          exposing that intuitively.
        - Computational research on `Gene Regulatory Networks`,
          also modeled with the same NN math, have shown how
          network topology is the key driver of function.
    - I published a paper on this (195 citations).
    - It shows that under an evolutionary process, Artificial
      Gene Regulatory Networks select out spurious network
      interactions, leaving you with what's functionally
      necessary (Also see the supplementary).
    - I believe this could be important for building a foundation
      for NNs. (I came from AI before writing this paper)
        - https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2538912/

### Difference perceptrons, sigmoid neurons
    - The problem is: this isn't what happens when our network
      contains perceptrons.
        - In fact, a small change in the weights or bias of any
          single perceptron in the network can sometimes cause
          the output of that perceptron to completely flip, say
          from `0` to `1`
    - That flip may then cause the behaviour of the rest of the
      network to completely change in some complicated way.
    - So while your "9" might now be classified correctly, the
      network behaviour on all the other images is likely to have
      changed in some hard-to-control way.
    - That makes it difficult to see how to gradually modify the
      weights and biases so the network gets closer to the
      desired behaviour.
        - Perhaps there's some clever way of getting around this
          problem. But it's not immediately obvious how we can
          get a network of perceptrons to learn.
    - We can overcome this problem by introducing a new type of
      artificial neuron called a `sigmoid neuron`.
        - Sigmoid neurons are similar to perceptrons, but
          modified so that _small changes_ in their weights and
          bias cause only a small change in their _output_.
        - That's the crucial fact which will allow a network of
          sigmoid neurons to learn.

            - $MY_IMAGES/cs/AI-ML/history-observables-RL.jpg
    - A "UU" Example for an ML system is simply proof it has
      failed to acquire the relevant classification criteria, and
      on most problems, ML systems cannot in principle do so.
        - eg. consider that 3D data is often needed and only 2D
          given.
    - I'd prefer to call these "breaking examples" or
      "beyond-scope examples".
    - What's happening is that the domain of inference (`X`)
      fails to contain data about the target (`Y`), though `Y`
      belongs to the `Problem Domain PD` of which `X` stands as
      an incomplete sample. Eg:
        - `X` = images of roads;
        - `Y` = pedestrian in street;
        - `PD` = X + 3D data + behavioural data + pedestraian
          theory of mind + weather_data + ..
    - Actually deciding `Y` requires the `PD`; we have `X`.
    - So there are many "breaking examples" which fall within
      `PD` but outside of `X`.
    - In general, we cannot measure the full `PD` using
      contemporary machine systems.
    - Humans are able to capture much more of the `PD` in complex
      ways which would take a while to spell out.
    - Much of modern ML, as advertised, is a bit of delusion and
      a bit of charlatanism:
        - we are offered a means of obtaining `Y` via `X`, but
          never consider `PD`.
    - We just keep "evaluating" the systems on examples
      consistent with `X`.
    - That's the point of this article.
    - However, it is much more radical than it's making out. This
      practice is really a way of disguising that ML systems do
      not work.

### Optimizers (e.g. Gradient Descent)
    - During the training process, we tweak and change the model
      parameters (weights) to minimize the loss function, and
      make our predictions as correct and optimized as possible.
    - But how exactly do you do that?
        - How do you change the parameters of your model, by how
          much, and when?
    - This is where optimizers come in.
        - They tie together the loss function and model
          parameters by updating the model in response to the
          output of the loss function.
    - In simpler terms, optimizers shape and mold your model into
      its most accurate possible form by futzing the _weights_.
    - The loss function is the guide to the terrain, telling the
      optimizer when it’s moving in the right or wrong direction.
    - The grandaddy of optimizers: Gradient Descent

### GD: Gradient Descent
    - I got it with the gradient slope! `∇`
        - why we take the negative gradient `-∇`

    - `CHANGE forward (along x-axis, parameter-axis) = -∇`

    - if `∇` (slope) is NEGATIVE,
        - it means as we go forward on the x-axis, we will go
          down. That is good so the change should be forward
          (positive) by the amount of the slope. But to make it
          "forward/positive" we have to multiply the slope by
          a negative.

    - if ∇ (slope) is POSITIVE,
        - it means as we go forward on the x-axis we'll actually
          start to "climb" up the hill, and so further away from
          our desired target with is down the hill in the
          minimum.
        - therefore the negative `-` minus drags us back down, as
          we move forward along the x-axis

### Basic approach DL
    - Initialize::
        - We initialize the parameters to random values. This may
          sound surprising. There are certainly other choices we
          could make, such as initializing them to the percentage
          of times that pixel is activated for that category, but
          since we already know that we have a routine to improve
          these weights, it turns out that just starting with
          random weights works perfectly well.

    - Loss::
        - Samuel calls this "testing the effectiveness of any
          current WEIGHT ASSIGNMENT in terms of actual
          performance".
        - We need a function that will return a number that is
          small if the model performance is good (the standard
          approach is to treat a small loss as good, and a large
          loss as bad, though this is just convention).

    - Step::
        - A simple way to figure if a weight should be increased
          a bit, or decreased a bit, would be just to try it:
            - increase the weight by a small bit, and _see if the
              loss goes up or down_.
        - Once you find the correct direction, you could then
          change that amount by a bit more, and a bit less, till
          you find an amount that works well.
        - However, this is slow!
            - As we will see, the magic of calculus allows us to
              directly figure out which direction, and by roughly
              how much, to change each weight, without having to
              try all these small changes.
            - The way to do this is by calculating _gradients_.
              This is just a performance optimization, we would
              get exactly the same results by using the slower
              manual process as well.

    - Stop::
        - Once we've decided how many epochs to train the model
          for (a few suggestions for this were given in the
          earlier list), we apply that decision. This is where
          that decision is applied.
        - For our digit classifier, we would keep training until
          the accuracy of the model started getting worse, or we
          ran out of time.

### gradient / slope:
    - whatever we get for the slope, just step in the NEGATIVE
      direction (of that slope) to ensure we're going downhill...
    - to make sure we are minimising the cost
        - (making it as small as possible)

### Memristors
    - ./memristors.txt
    - https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=25933723
    - If memristors act like neurons, put them in NNs
    - Memristors are the technology that seemed poised to ignite
      a new era of computing. The promise of being able to redraw
      the current computer architectural hierarchies is
      tantalizing.
    - If ML applications are what finally get memristors out into
      the world, I wish them godspeed.
    - What do you think of memristors vs FPGAs?
        - They have many similarities -- they both redrew current
          computer architectures, they integrate memory and
          computing, they have randomness built-in, and they both
          take less power than mainstream GPUs.
    - What does memristor provide that specially designed "neural
      FPGA" can not?
        - They aren't really directly comparable like you imply.
        - A memristor is basically a resistor that changes
          resistance when you run current through it. An FPGA is
          a clever layout of many thousands of logic gates that
          can be programmed to form arbitrarily complex digital
          logic circuits. It's like comparing apples to
          skyscrapers because they're both associated with New
          York.
    - Presumably, a memristor based neural network would have the
      advantage over an FPGA of requiring significantly less
      silicon area to achieve the same function.
    - I imagine an FPGA based neural network would approximate
      analog signals digitally, perhaps using floating point
      "half's" or something.
    - Memristors would directly operate on analog signals,
      encoding information as amplitudes or pulses of currents
      and voltages.
    - Notably, FPGAs and GPUs can't really be directly compared
      to each other in terms of power consumption unless you
      specify specific use cases. You can't build the equivalent
      of a mainstream GPU out of FPGAs without severely limiting
      the clock speed (because of how physically large it would
      be), and if you did anyway, it would use many orders of
      magnitude more power to function.
    - So, a GPU is way more power efficient than an FPGA for
      rendering graphics. There are certainly problems that a GPU
      isn't good at solving, and so there's a good chance that an
      FPGA solution would be more power efficient.
    - Correct me if I'm wrong, but isn't it a good metaphor to
      say that FPGAs are just ASIC emulators? I.e. much less
      efficient than an equivalent ASIC, but good for prototyping
      and something to use in production if you can't afford to
      manufacture your own chip (which is most low-volume use
      cases).
        - Under this metaphor, memristor-based ASICs will
          obviously be more efficient than FPGAs emulating them,
          but that's predicated on there being a high-volume use
          case justifying the creation of a memristor chip in the
          first place.

### Essence of gradient descent
    - Short: `w     -= gradient(w) * lr`
    - Long:  `weight = weight - gradient(weight) * learning_rate`
    - This is known as stepping your parameters, using an
      optimizer step.
    - Picking a good `lr` is very important.
        - Too small and it takes ages. Too big and you overshoot
          or bounce around.

    - This suggests we could make massive progress if we just
      looked at stars with identical or near-identical spectra.

### ML for language - Context
    - Two experiences taught me that humans don't understand
      language well WITHOUT CONTEXT:
    - Raising a child.
        - My father is surprised when my daughter knows how to
          use a word in just the right context.
        - I'm not, because its a natural product of mimicry: if
          you copy what others say, you'll use the words in the
          correct context usually.
        - As with computer-generated text, the exceptions are
          often hilarious.
    - Song lyrics.
        - I had a very clear experience where I just couldn't
          understand the refrain of 'Gold Guns Girls'.
        - It sounded unintelligible until I read the lyrics.
          After that, it was crystal clear. Reading the lyrics
          gives CONTEXT.
