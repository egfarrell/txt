### Summary
    - BIRADS stands for
        - _Breast IMAGING-Reporting and Data System_
    - Developed by American College of Radiology (ACR) for
      radiologists to categorize mammography findings (but
      expanded now to include `ultrasound` and `MRI` of the
      breast).

    - BIRADS provides clear, consistent communication between the
      radiologist and referring physician and standardizes the
      terminology used in breast imaging reports.

### BIRADS assessment categories:

    1. BIRADS 0:
        - Incomplete - Need Additional Imaging Evaluation
        - This means the mammogram or ultrasound is not complete,
          and more imaging is needed. This can be due to
          overlapping tissues, a potential abnormality, or the
          comparison with prior studies.

    2. BIRADS 1:
        - Negative
        - This is a clear or "negative" mammogram or ultrasound
          with no significant abnormalities.

    3. BIRADS 2:
        - Benign
        - This means there is a benign (non-cancerous) finding on
          the mammogram or ultrasound, and there's no cancer
          suspicion. Examples might include benign cysts or
          fibroadenomas.

    4. BIRADS 3:
        - Probably Benign
        - The findings are not expected to be malignant but are
          not definitively benign either.
        - The recommendation is usually a short-term follow-up
          (e.g. 6 months) to ensure the ROI remains stable and
          doesn't change.

    5. BIRADS 4:
        - Suspicious (4A, 4B, 4C)
        - The finding is suspicious for malignancy, and a biopsy
          should be considered. This category can be further
          subdivided (4A, 4B, 4C) based on suspicion level.

    6. BIRADS 5:
        - Highly Suspicious of Malignancy
        - There's a high chance (greater than 95%) that cancer is
          present. A biopsy is recommended.

    7. BIRADS 6:
        - Known Biopsy-Proven Malignancy
        - Used when imaging is performed for a known cancer,
          either before treatment or to evaluate the results of
          treatment.

    - BIRADS also includes `descriptors` for mammographic,
      sonographic, and MRI findings to further characterize
      lesions and provide standardized terminology.

    - While BIRADS provides a standardized way to describe
      findings, the final diagnosis (benign vs. malignant) often
      requires a biopsy tissue diagnosis, especially for lesions
      at BIRADS `4` or `5`.


### BIRADS descriptors
    - Descriptors help radiologists characterize lesions.
    - These descriptors serve as a "checklist" of sorts, helping
      radiologists categorize lesions consistently.

    1. Shape:
        - Oval
        - Round
        - Irregular

    2. Orientation
        - (compared to skin line):
        - Parallel
        - Not parallel

    3. Margin:
        - Circumscribed (well-defined)
        - Indistinct
        - Angular
        - Microlobulated
        - Spiculated

    4. Echogenicity
        - brightness compared to surrounding tissue:
        - Anechoic
            - fluid-filled, such as a simple cyst
        - Hypoechoic
            - lesion darker than surrounding tissue
        - Isoechoic
            - lesion has same brightness as surrounding tissue
        - Hyperechoic
            - lesion brighter than surrounding tissue
        - Complex
            - combination of anechoic and echogenic areas, like
              a cyst with a solid component

    5. Posterior Features
        - seen behind the lesion:
        - No posterior features
        - Enhancement (`brighter` area behind the lesion)
        - Shadowing (`darker` area behind the lesion)
        - Combined pattern

        - Other descriptors might relate to
            - lesion size,
            - elasticity (with elastography techniques),
            - vascularity (with Doppler techniques)

    - Note: BIRADS 3 (Probably Benign)
        - often includes findings that are not typical for cancer
          but are not definitively benign.
        - Lesions in this category might include fibroadenomas,
          non-simple cysts, or focal asymmetries that haven't
          changed over time.
        - Characteristics like oval shape, circumscribed margins,
          and parallel orientation might be consistent with
          BIRADS 3.
        - But the key is there's a low suspicion of malignancy,
          and short-term follow-up is typically recommended.

    - While descriptors guide radiologists, the final BIRADS
      categorization also takes into account
        - the radiologist's experience,
        - clinical information, and
        - comparison with prior imaging.

    - It's not purely a "check-the-box" process. The descriptors
      help standardize reporting, but there's still an element of
      professional judgment involved.

### "anechoic" and "echolucent" (black)
    - For ultrasound imaging, the terms "anechoic" and
      "echolucent" are often used interchangeably. Both describe
      a region within an ultrasound where the echoes are absent
      or much weaker than the surrounding areas.

    - Anechoic: Literally means "without echo." Anechoic
      structures appear BLACK on an ultrasound image.
      Fluid-filled structures, like simple cysts, are classic
      anechoic lesions in breast ultrasound.

    - Echolucent: This term emphasizes the relative "lucency"
      or "lack of echoes" compared to surrounding tissues. It
      also appears BLACK or VERY DARK on ultrasound images.

    - both terms describe similar sonographic appearances, but
      "anechoic" is more commonly used in breast imaging.
      However, in some contexts or regions, one term might be
      preferred over the other.

### Posterior Acoustic Shadowing
    - 'Posterior acoustic shadowing' in breast US, especially
      behind potentially malignant lesions, is rooted in the
      principles of ultrasound physics and tissue
      characteristics.

    - simplified explanation:

    1. Sound Attenuation: As ultrasound waves travel through
       tissue, they lose energy due to scattering, reflection,
       and absorption. This process is termed attenuation.
        - If a lesion attenuates the sound waves more than the
          surrounding tissue, fewer waves will pass through the
          lesion and continue deeper into the tissue.
        - As a result, the area behind the lesion will receive
          less ultrasound energy and appear DARKER on the image,
          creating a shadow.

    2. Malignant Characteristics: Malignant lesions often have
       characteristics leading to increased attenuation:
        - Higher Cell Density: Malignant tumors often have
          a higher cell density compared to benign tumors or
          normal tissue. More cells mean more interfaces for the
          ultrasound waves to scatter and reflect.
        - Desmoplasia: This refers to the growth of fibrous or
          connective tissue that often surrounds malignant
          tumors. This dense tissue can strongly attenuate
          ultrasound waves.
        - Calcifications: Malignant lesions can contain
          microcalcifications, which are tiny calcium deposits.
          These calcifications reflect ultrasound waves
          effectively, contributing to shadowing.

    3. Refraction and Reflection: When ultrasound waves
       encounter a boundary between two tissues with different
       acoustic impedances (like a solid tumor and surrounding
       fat), some waves are reflected back to the transducer, and
       others are refracted or bent.
        - This can further reduce the amount of ultrasound energy
          that continues past the lesion.

    4. Comparatively, Benign Findings: Simple cysts, which are
       fluid-filled and benign, are anechoic and do not typically
       cause shadowing because the sound waves pass easily
       through the fluid.
        - On the other hand, solid benign lesions like
          `fibroadenomas` may cause some shadowing, but it's
          often less pronounced than in malignant lesions.

    - While posterior acoustic shadowing can be suggestive of
      malignancy, it's essential to consider it in the context of
      other lesion characteristics and clinical factors.
    - Not all malignant lesions will display shadowing, and not
      all lesions with shadowing are malignant.
    - Always, the combined assessment of various ultrasound
      features will provide the most accurate characterization of
      a lesion.
