### What Makes You a Good Bioinformatician?
Posted on October 20, 2013 by !nfoplatter

Does knowing contemporary data analysis tools and keeping
a set of persuasive Unix commands at your fingertips make
you a good bioinformatician? Or, is it that your stunning
programming skills that transform you into a big tool
production house make you a good bioinformatician?

I recently performed an extensive literature mining and
considered the opinions of pioneering researchers and
scientists in the field of bioinformatics on this topic.

I've come across raging discussions about what bioinformaticians
are meant to do. Some are adamant that experts from various
fields should collaborate instead of one person doing all things
poorly!

It is crucial to understand that a bioinformatician is
different from mathematician/statistician and also different
from a biologist regarding their ability to establish an
interface for research and also channel the requirements and
findings from both the directions.

One thing I observed is that a _definition_ of bioinformatics is
not universally agreed upon. Generally , we define it as the
development and application of IT for understanding the problems
in biology, most commonly _molecular biology_ (but increasingly
other areas of biology as well). It deals with methods for the
systematic storage, retrieval and analysis of _biological data_
including:
    * nucleic acid and protein sequences,
    * their structures,
    * functional roles,
    * regulatory pathways
    * biophysical interactions.

Some researchers interpret bioinformatics more narrowly and
include only issues dealing with high throughput _sequencing
data_ along with the integration and the analysis of _OMICs_.

Others construe bioinformatics more broadly and include all
areas of computational biology, including _population
modelling_ and _molecular simulations_. Others think
bioinformatics is the development of the necessary tools and
databases to analyse biological data and draw meaningful
conclusions.

In spite of the diversity of bioinformatics domains, the
foundations can be summed up in three educational pillars:

    1. Biology
    2. Mathematics and Statistics
    3. Computer Science

It is ideal (but ambitious) to be expert in all the three
pillars of bioinformatics. A good bioinformatician would
possess a thorough knowledge of any _two pillars_ but at the
same time would also be aware of the fundamentals of the
third.

If you have trouble deciding, then let your _interest_ pick
two pillars, based on which you will develop a bioinformatics
flavor, anything from sequence analysis to molecular dynamic
simulations.

These pillars are themselves very diverse. For example, when
we talk about _biology_, it comprises of:

* molecular biology,
* biochemistry,
* evolution,
* ecology,
* behaviour etc.

Similarly, in mathematics and statistics, you have options
from probability theory to _analytical combinatorics_. As
soft skills, you have options from programming languages to
learning systems.

One becomes a bioinformatics domain expert based on what slice is
taken from the stack. You may chose molecular biology,
differentials and integrals, and Matlab and be a good
bioinformatician in the domain of _molecular dynamics_ and
simulations.

Some might argue that this is a very naive and one would require
much more than that. Well naturally there is no upper limit. If
you keep your knowledge stagnant, you'll soon realise that you
have stopped growing.

Due to the pace advancements are happening in biology and
technology, it has become vital to upgrade our knowledge, skills
and techniques but at the same time absolutely essential to stick
to your stream, because success comes from experiences and
experiences comes from dedicated efforts.

Your integrity is like a tip of an iceberg. The same tip,
from outside may appear smaller than a frozen lake but when
it comes to summer, the tip is all that remains since it has
a strong foundation which no ordinary summer can melt.
