### Intro
    - Folding is more than tidiness in biology: after protein
      synthesis creates a polypeptide chain, additional
      changes—especially folding—are often required for a protein
      to become functional.

### Reminder of Protein Roles
    - Proteins perform many roles: form channels, contribute to
      structure, act as enzymes, participate in cell signaling
      (receptors), and help protect the body.

### Modifications of Proteins
    - Proteins often undergo chemical modifications before
      becoming functional (e.g., phosphorylation) in addition to
      folding.

### Importance of Shape for Proteins
    - Shape and function are tightly linked: receptors and their
      signals or enzymes and their substrates must fit specific
      three-dimensional shapes to work.

### Levels of Protein Structure
    - Protein folding is described at four structural levels
      (primary, secondary, tertiary, quaternary), each involving
      different kinds of interactions.

### Primary Structure
    - The primary structure is the linear sequence of amino acids
      (monomers) in a polypeptide, held by peptide bonds.
    - Genes (DNA) determine the order and number of amino acids;
      even a single amino acid change can alter function (e.g.,
      sickle cell).
    - Each amino acid has a carboxyl group, an amino group, and
      an R group (side chain).

### Secondary Structure
    - Secondary structure is local folding of the backbone into
      patterns like the `alpha helix` and `beta pleated sheet`.
    - These shapes arise largely from hydrogen bonds between
      backbone atoms and depend on the amino acid sequence.

### Tertiary Structure
    - Tertiary structure is the overall three-dimensional fold of
      a single polypeptide driven mainly by interactions among
      R groups.
    - R groups can be hydrophilic or hydrophobic (hydrophobic
      residues tend to be buried; hydrophilic ones tend to be
      exposed).
    - Other forces contributing to tertiary structure include
      ionic bonds, van der Waals interactions, disulfide bonds,
      and hydrogen bonds involving side chains.

### Quaternary Structure [not in all proteins]
    - Quaternary structure applies when a functional protein
      consists of more than one polypeptide chain (subunits).
    - Subunits are held together by interactions such as hydrogen
      bonds and disulfide bonds.

### Proteins often have help in folding [introduces chaperonins]
    - Folding can involve intermediate steps and is complex; many
      proteins require assistance.
    - Chaperonins are protein complexes (barrel-shaped) that
      provide an environment to help other proteins fold
      correctly.

### Denaturing Proteins
    - Each protein has an ideal environment (temperature, pH).
      Exposure to extremes (e.g., high heat, abnormal pH) can
      disrupt the interactions at various structural levels and
      denature the protein, altering its shape and preventing
      function.
    - Denaturation may be reversible in some cases but is
      irreversible in many others; misfolding and denaturation
      are implicated in various diseases.
