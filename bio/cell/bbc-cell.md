
### Dutchman Van Levenhook
    - 17th Century linen merchant. Obsessed with lenses. His
      microscope: piece of brass, one tiny hole a millimetre wide
      containing a spherical lens. He could curve the lens more
      than anyone else, allowed him to magnify objects up to 500
      times. 

    - He looked at the water near Delft. It changed colour with
      the seasons. He thought there might be something in the
      water he could discover. Tiny bugs "scooting around",
      probably protozoa/amoeba. He thought he saw miniaturised
      versions of everyday animals (animalcules).

    - The Royal Society were sceptical of Van Levenhook and asked
      Hooke to investigate. Earlier Hooke had looked at cork with
      a "Magnifying glass" and saw cells. He looked at water from
      the Thames but saw nothing. He improved his microscope and
      replicated Van Levenhooks findings.

    - Van Levenhook then examined tooth plaque bacteria, red
      blood cells and sperm cells. 

### Spontaneous generation
    - 17th century belief. Mice were generated from sweat and
      wheat. Since Hooke, botanists had discovered that many
      plants had these "cells". In 1830 Robert Brown studied
      orchids for many years (which have larger cells than other
      plants). Brown noticed a distinctive shape in every cell
      and called it the nucleus. This gave rise to the idea that
      cells might be universal. (He also came up with "Browian
      motion". He's contributed to atomic physics and biology!)

### Colour Blur
    - Lister designed a new microscope that didn't suffer from
      colour blur. And in Berlin Theodore Schwann was about to
      unite the two strands of biology - animal and vegetable.

    - Anatomy was in chaos. People thought that animals and
      humans were made from granules, fibres, tubes, globules and
      bladders. Animals biology were lagging behind botany
      because animal cells were much harder to see.
