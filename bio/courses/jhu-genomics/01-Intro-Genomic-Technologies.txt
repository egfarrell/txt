### Genomics:
    - Branch of molecular biology concerning the structure,
      FUNCTION, evolution and mapping of genomes
    - mapping: (sequencing) where the genes are and other
      interesting bits
    - gene: has changed a lot over the past 50/100 years
    - gene: old: an inheritable unit
    - GENE: small section of the genome that encodes a protein
      that in turn has some function.

    - Biology

### genomic life cycle:
    - start with some subjects (e.g. humans)
    - collect samples (eg skin cells)
    - prepare samples and send for sequencing
    - sequencer generates enormous amounts data
    - "reads": short fragments of dna sequences
    - align reads to reference human genome (1 reference genome
      for now)
    - tells us how they differ from reference genome
    - for every genome in a person, you've got 2 copies (one from
      each parent)
    - question: how does reference differ from the 2 copies you
      have?
    - do analysis: deposit  data in public database (CBI), then
      apply more analysis to these datasets

### stage 1: experimental design
    - I've a question, how much data do I need, how many subjects
      do I need, what kind of data
    - often "sequence data", but modifications
        - "ChIP-seq"
        - metaSeq
    - alignment & assembly: how much of a particular gene is
      present, find the genes that were expressed in tissue you
      are studying
    - preprocess and normalise data
    - sequence machines have errors:
        - random
        - systematic
    - question: for a sample: what genes were turned on and at
      what level were they present in?

    - For PCR you need:

        - lots of A, G, C, T molecules

    - PCR Process:
        - Melt at 94º C
        - Cool to 54º C
        - Warm up to 72º C
        - Repeat

    - NextGen
        - errors: $MY_IMAGES/bio/intro-genomics/next-gen-errors.jpg
        - sequencer computes quality: look at how "pure" each spot color
          was

    - NextGen Applications

        - if you want to know the proteins, you need to know the
          protein coding exons (the introns get thrown away)
        - exons only comprise 1.5% of the genome
        - use a bead to rip out only the exonic bits

        - RNA sequencing... at the end of transcription the RNA
          has a long sequence of A's at the end: "poly(A) tail"
            - CCUAGAAAAAAAA

        - ChIP-seq:
            - cross-link protein to DNA
            - find protein binding sites

        - Methyl-seq
            - methylation seq: find methylation marks (groups)
            - where on the genome the DNA has been _methylated_
            - important epigenetic modification
            - methyl groups only ever attached to 'C'
            - affects which proteins are being expressed in the
              cell
            - Methyl marks can be passed on from one cell cycle
              to another as cells divide
            - method: split DNA into 2 identical samples
              (aliquots)
            - Bisulfite conversion on one sample: convert all
              `C`s that are NOT Methaylated into `U`
            - sequence again and comapre strands
            - Need special aligners because the converted DNA (C
              -> U conversion) doesn't match the ref genome we
              usually align to.

    - Internal storage of DNA. (2 bits)
        - Reduced dataset: {A, C, G, T}  
        - To get 4-fold compression:
            - A: 00 
            - C: 01 
            - G: 10 
            - T: 11 

    - Worked out a probability distribution for 10 bases around introns:
        - "donor" intron :   typically has "GT"
        - "acceptor" intron: typically has "AG"
        - $MY_IMAGES/bio/intro-genomics/Intron-Probability-distribution.jpg

    - Efficiency: accessing memory is a bit like a "travelling
      salesman" problem. Want to minimise round-trips between
      memory and hard-disk 

    - interesting titbit: "RNA editing"
        - sometimes some nucleotides in the RNA can be edited by
          the cell. You have a piece of RNA that doesn't quite
          match the DNA. Therefore the protein might be
          different.
        - Only affects 1 or 2 nucleotides. Not any nucleotide can
          be changed into another. 

    - RNA editing debacle. 
        - Some researchers take a huge dataset and tried to find
          examples of it.
        - Approach: sequence DNA and RNA from the same person and
          align both sequences to each other.  
        - done recently, but the scientists didnt know the
          software well. Thought they had discovered hundreds of
          new examples of RNA editing.
        - well the software normally has 1 mis-alignment per
          1 million.  
        - but in a "big data" dataset, this can yield 100s or
          errors and seeming mis-matches between the DNA seq and
          RNA seq.
        - Moral: Check the software! Trust it but verify.  
        - ### Evolution of translational omics
            - Following a recent case involving premature use of
              omics-based tests in cancer clinical trials at Duke
              University, the NCI asked  IOM establish
              a committee to recommend ways to strengthen
              omics-based test development and evaluation.


    - Tuexdo pipeline:
        - turns reads into genes
        - each gene has several "splice isoforms"
        - each isoform can be expressed at a different level  
        - NEW tuxedo suite of tools: Bowtie2, Hisat, StringTie,
          Ballgown  (ie cufflinks is old...)
        - Alignment is the most basic computational prob. But
          different software systems give different answers (eg
          BWA and Bowtie2). Problem isn't totally discrete and
          well defined.  
        - $MY_IMAGES/bio/intro-genomics/align-software-different-answers.jpg

    - new technology to produce longer and longer reads. But isnt

    - NOTE: if underlying SEQUENCING TECHNOLOGY has changed, the
