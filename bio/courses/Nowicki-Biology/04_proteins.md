
### take home message
    - the shape of a protein, and hence its function, all boils
      down to the order of amino acids in a chain

### etymology
    - from Greek proteios - first place

### functions of proteins
    - only slight exageration to say proteins do nearly
      everything in a cell. They are the molecular work-horses.

### function: form structural elements
    - e.g. support the outer walls of cells by acting as little
      girders on the inside of cells to give them their shape.
      Form cytoskeleton.

### function: molecule support
    - hold together larger molecules. 
    - e.g. DNA is surrounded by proteins which serve as
      a scaffolding to hold it together.
    - Chromosomes are actually made of much more protein than DNA
      even though DNA is whats interesting about the chromsomes.

### function: mechanical work
    - some proteins act as little motors. 
    - they cause cells to change shape by pulling on little
      elements inside. This is how muscles work. 
    - Or they move things around in the cell acting like little
      conveyer belts.

### function: transporters
    - act as the vehicle for transporting other materials around
      the cell.
    - e.g. the protein hemogloblin which transports the oxygen
      molecule around the body in blood. (the oxygen binds with
      the hemogloblin)

### function: signal receivers
    - they act as receptors to detect molecular signals from
      inside the cell or from other cells. They often act as the
      signals themselves.

### function: regulate cell function
    - they act as little molecular switches, turning on and off
      processes that a cell runs.

### function: catlysts
    - help make a chemical reaction run. Proteins acting as
      catlysts have a special name: enzymes. Enzymes are very
      important for bio-chemistry: chemical reactions that occur
      in the concentrations and at the temperatures found in
      living cells.

### Question 
    - how can proteins do so many different things?
    - Answer
        - there are many many different types of proteins. e.g.
          in a bacterial cell like E. Coli there might be 10,000
          proteins! In a human cell, there might be 50,000
          proteins each with a different job.

### Question
    - what makes a protein good at doing whatever it does? 
    - How do proteins become specialised for these different
      functions?

    - ### answer: shape 1
    - the function of a protein is mainly determined by the SHAPE
      of the protein. They are generally large molecules. They
      are polymers made up of a relatively small number of amino
      acid building blocks. But they are found in a complex 3D
      shape.

    - ### answer: shape 2
    - The relationship between shape and function is kind of
      intuitive for many proteins. e.g. 

    - a support structure:
        - a molecular girder, must be long and stiff. 

    - enzymes:
        - to speed up a molecular reaction. Shape is critical
          here too. The shape will have special nooks and
          crannies which will fit the molecule it has to react
          with. 

    - However its not a simple as that, the _chemical properties_
      of the protein matter too, although the properties are
      often determined by the shape too.

### shape: carbon atoms / amino acids
    - how does a string of amino acids form a 3D shape? 
    - Need to look at organic chemistry. 
    - At the centre of an amino acid is a carbon atom. For all
      amino acids the carbon atom is surrounded on 3 sides by the
      same 3 things and on the 4th side by something different
      depending on which amino acid it is.

    - amino acid side chain

### how do amino acids bind?
    * Carboxyl group of one amino acid 
    bonds with 
    * the amino group of another amino acid 

    - Carbon and Nitrogen are left in a covalent bond (atoms
      share electrons). Specifically: a covalent bond between
      amino and Carboxyl groups is called a _peptide_ bond.

### backbone chain amino acids
    - result of peptide covalent bonds leaves a chain of
      Nitrogens and Carbons
    - ```
    - ... - N - C - C - N - C - C - N - C - C -  ...
    - ```

### polarity to string
    - The backbone chain of amino acids has an inherent polarity.
    - (amino acid)N - C - C - N - C - C - N - C - C(carboxyl group) 
    - this gives a direction that the DNA code must be run later
      when constructing the protein.

### length of protein   
    - some short  : 15 amino acids
    - some long   : 5,000 amino acids
    - average     : several 100 long


### key question
    - how does the unique sequence of amino acids determine the
      structure of the protein? 
    - Well the amino chains don't stay stretched out. They fold
      up, bunch back on each other in more of a globular form.
      They way they fold is not random, but depends on the
      sequence of amino acids. So the 3D structure comes from the
      linear sequence of acids.

### side chains 
    - Key to folding, is the way the side chains interact with
    each other both physically and chemically and also
    interact with the (watery) environment in the cell.

### four levels of protein structure 
    1. primary
    2. secondary
    3. tertiary
    4. quaternary

### primary structure
    - the sequence of amino acids that make up that protein.
    - i.e. just the ordered list of acids. 
    - Early work concentrated on this level. Insulin (a hormone,
      molecular messenger) was the first protein to have its
      amino acids sequenced by Sanger at Cambridge. 
    - He found out that each protein has its own unique sequence.
      (taken for granted for today!)

### secondary structure
    - first level of bending and folding that occurs. 
    - The first level of 3D shape that emerges.
    * alpha helix
    * beta pleated sheet these kinds of structures are common to
      parts of many different proteins

### how secondary structure formed
    These are formed by weak chemical interactions along the
    backbone of the amino acid sequence. The regular pattern
    of Carbons and Nitrogens along the back bone form weak
    Hydrogen bonds with each other.
    Although Hydrogen bonds are weak, the repeated pattern
    of C and N give enough common opportunity to form enough
    Hydrogen bonds to build the _regular_ secondary structure.

### tertiary strucuture
    most interested in this level. Because at this level we
    begin to see how structure begins to form function. This
    level also the result of weak interactions, but among
    the side chains rather than the back bone. Because the
    side chains can occur in an irregular intervals the
    pattern of twisting and folding can be much less
    predictable. So not regular like the pleated sheets etc.

### tertiary strucuture - water molecule bonding
    some side chains are more or less willing to be near
    water molecules.
    some side chains - hydrophobic
    some side chains - hydrophilic

    those hydrophobic amino acids tend to be repelled by
    the watery environment in the cell and tend to move
    into the centre of what will emerge as a globular
    protein.

    The hydrophilic acids will do just the opposite.
    They move and twist and turn until they find 
    themselves on the outside of the protein.

    So depending on the unique sequence of amino
    acids you're going to get different forces
    on that string to bend and twist in different
    ways to get some acids on the inside, some
    on the outside.

    So this is a major determinate of what causes
    a protein linear structure to bend and fold in
    a particular pattern to maximise or minimise
    contact with water on the outside.

### tertiary strucuture - other bonding
    The side chains also bond using Hydrogen bonds and other
    weak chemical reactions (disulfide), again different to
    the secondary level because the pattern is irregular and
    harder to predict.

### tertiary strucuture - kids class outing
    some kids like the water, some don't so you can predict
    who will be where. In addition some kids like each other
    more than others, so friends will be a bit closer to 
    each other.

### covalent bonds (which level of structure?)
    some amino acids will form covalent bonds which
    greatly strengthens the 3D structure and pulls
    the molecule together.

### quaternary structure
    Some proteins are made from several polypeptide chains.
    Hemogloblin has 4 sub-chains that all fold together
