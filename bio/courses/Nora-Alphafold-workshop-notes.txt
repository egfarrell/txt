### Questions
    - How easy/accurate is to predict the active site of
      a protein utilizing modeling from the protein sequence?
      When utilizing machine learning, what is the lower training
      data set one can start with to generate valuable
      results?/Cynthia
        - In many cases, the most important and conserved parts
          of the protein, including the active site, will be
          modelled well by AlphaFold and RoseTTAFold. Not always,
          so it’s still going to be a good idea to obtain an
          experimental structure. For training data, I don’t
          think the deep-learning models are tailored to
          particular proteins, but the prediction step does
          benefit a lot from prior knowledge of related proteins.

    - This is more a comment than a question. One point I always
      found amazing: how scientists focus  on the mutations
      mostly in viruses. But do we have any information on the
      conservation (I mean 100% conservation) of sequences and
      amino acids in the human proteome? In other words: if we
      sequence the full genome of people, would we find any
      mutations that could also argue for the higher affinity of
      the spike for some people (perhaps even tribes?)

    - in the template based modeling, how far evolutionarily can
      the proteins be , to generate accurate models?
        - With sequence identity between target and template at
          60% and higher, the model of the target will be very
          accurate, usually. At 25% and higher you can also
          sometimes get quite good models. It depends on the
          number of insertions/deletions comparing target and
          template. And the length of the sequences. Few indels
          good, long segments good.


- Do you think we are far from predicting geometries of protein
  with several stable conformations? The most clear case would be
  Prions
    - I think a general approach is a still a matter of current
      research: i think Frank Noé gives a good account of current
      progress here:
      https://www.annualreviews.org/doi/abs/10.1146/annurev-physchem-042018-052331.

### Gradient Descent
- In the slide that illustrates the AlphaFold1 internal workings,
  it would seem that from the initial guess of the CNN, the
  protein-specific gradient descent basically folds the whole
  protein. I am surprised by this, is it known what
  optimization/relaxation routine did they use? Great talk!
    - A simple gradient descent algorithm based on a learned cost
      function.

- And related to my question: I am not sure I understood how
  AlphaFold v2 differs from this, i.e. does v2 still use this
  gradient descent method?
    - Simply put: the difference is that AF2 learns end-to-end
      (sequence in, structure out), whereas AF1 learns
      a potential from which it then gets a structure after
      gradient descent.
    - Less simply put: AF2 has some later stages that do some
      non-physical optimisation on a “triangle gas” to get a good
      structure and it can do postprocessing that involves an
      amber relaxation process. All explained in detail in the
      appendices of the AF2 paper.


### MSA
- There are many algorithms to generate MSA alignments, each one
  of them trying to detect a different property or enancing
  a particular feature. There would be any advantage on feeding
  different MSA generated with different algorithm or there will
  be just a MSA that will work better than others?
    - it is clear that MSA methods really aiming for aligning
      structurally equivalent parts (rather than reflecting
      evolutionary history) is more suitable for being used in
      alphafold. As the number of sequences available for many
      families is large, the challenges are different than when
      for example clustalw was developed. I have not seen
      analyses of how sensitive alphafold is on the accuracy of
      the MSA, this would be interesting.


### Deeper knowledge
- Have we already learned anything about HOW proteins fold from
  AlphaFold?
    - I believe the consensus is that AF2/RF are able to predict
      the final, folded 3D structures for protein domains and
      some whole proteins when there are many, 60-100 or more,
      homologous proteins in the same protein (super)family.
      AF2/RF does NOT explain how the unfolded to folded process
      takes place, or HOW proteins fold. Btw the methods are also
      not good, currently, at de novo prediction. You cannot use
      a random sequence, not related to anything in the sequence
      databases, and expect to get a good model.


### Folding Process
- Great talk Inge. While AlphaFold2 predicts brilliantly, could
  you comment on the prospects for this type of technology to
  give us insight on the actual folding process?
    - Thanks, Rein! Alphafold as such is not targeted to this
      problem. In order to use a similar approach to learn about
      the folding process, one would need to have example data
      informative for this process. You may know more than me
      about data available - I am not aware of large datasets on
      protein folding trajectories.
