### What is a dex?

- The term "dex" comes up frequently in astronomy but is rarely
  defined in textbooks. The basic idea is straightforward:
    > a dex is simply an *order of magnitude*. 

    - 1 dex = 1 order of magnitude
    - 1 dex = `10^(1)`
    - x dex = `10^(x)`

- More formally, a difference of `x` dex is a change by a factor
  of `10^x`. (ie change by a factor of an order of magnitude) 

- (Although the dex is almost always used in relation to some
  other quantity, it can in principle be used to represent
  a unitless number: 
    - Thus the number `x` can be written `10^x` dex.

- We use the terms "order of magnitude" and "factor of x" all the
  time, though, so why use the term dex? 

- The reason is it comes in handy when talking about _fractions_
  of an order of magnitude. It’s particularly useful when talking
  about metallicity. 
- Metallicity is measured logarithmically relative to the
  abundance of metals in the Sun, and accordingly it must be
  unitless.

- As an example, look at Fischer & Valenti (2005). The authors
  present a correlation between the metallicity of stars and the
  probability that the star hosts a planet: stars with higher
  metallicities are more likely to host a planet. 

This result is in Figure 5:
http://1.1.1.4/bmi/joe-antognini.github.io/images/fischer05.png

- We see that the sample of stars spans a range of [Fe/H]
  from -0.5 to 0.5, which is one order of magnitude, or one dex. 

- Although its just as easy to say order of magnitude as to say
  dex in that case, suppose we asked how wide the bins in this
  histogram were. There are 10 bins spanning a single order of
  magnitude, so each bin width is a factor of `10^(0.1) ≈ 1.26` 

- This is clumsy, so instead we simply say that each bin has
  a width of `0.1 dex`.

- A dex is completely equivalent to a "bel" or "decade" which
  occasionally come up in engineering and physics. 

- The bel is much more widely known with its "deci-" prefix as
  the "decibel." A decibel is therefore equal to 0.1 dex.

- Historical footnote: "dex" was coined by astronomer CW Allen of
  Allen’s Astrophysical Quantities fame. He originally proposed
  it in the 1948 meeting of the IAU in Zurich and in 1951
  published a short note advocating it. 

- The physicist JBS Haldane joined the cause of the 'dex' in 1960
  with a short note published in Nature advocating its wider use
  outside of astronomy and physics, particularly in biology.

- However it seems biologists and other scientists were not as
  keen on the dex because it remains limited to the jargon of
  astronomy.
