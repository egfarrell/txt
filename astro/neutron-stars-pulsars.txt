

### Don't pulsate!
    - Pulsars were the first tangible evidence for neutron stars.
    - _They don’t actually pulsate_ — they ROTATE, which causes
      them to emit a periodic signal. 
        - But they were the fabled missing link in gravitational
          collapse, posited by Landau, studied by Oppenheimer,
          and explored in meticulous detail by Wheeler and his
          disciples.

### Surface
    - NS surface is basically iron...
    - ... as you go deeper and deeper, it becomes more and more
      neutron rich
    - towards the centre "neutron drip": 
        - nuclear "lattice"?
        - neutrons leak out of the nuclei
        - a neutron "gas" is formed, that is a superfluid
        - protons also leak out, form a gas, and will create
          a superconductor
        - so we have a proton superconductor mixed with a neutron
          superfluid
        - neutron superfluid turns into vortices
        - proton gas creates magnetic flux tubes
    - but a key conclusion: 
        - this is NOT A PERFECT FLUID inside a NS!

### Supernova explosion
    - ... results in a NS
    - Supernovas explosians are the result of a 'bounce and
      rebound shock' off the iron core 
    - Pauli exclusion principle the reason that the neutrons
      don't collapse onto one-another
    - NS are the most dense objects in the universe
    - during an supernova explosion, its not only the matter that
      gets compressed, the magnetic field of the original star
      also gets compressed. Most stars have pretty weak magnetic
      fields, but when they get squeezed, they become very
      powerful

### Measure distance to Pulsars (Rotation Measure)
    - PAUL: For some sources, theres only radio wave lengths, we
      can't see the companion star. 
        - It's much harder to get the distances eg, for pulsars.
          In this case, we use a trick called _rotation measure_. 
        - Often the radio waves coming from these things are
          polarized, so the radio waves, will be going up and
          down in this direction. 
        - But as they travel through interstellar space, the
          radio waves go past electrons, and they cause the
          electrons to jiggle. And that rotates the polarisation.
        - But it tilts it differently depending on whether it's
          a low frequency or a high frequency wave. 
        - So the high-frequency waves are rotated less. 
        - The low-frequency ones are rotated more. 
        - So what you find is that as you change your frequency,
          you can see that the angle of polarization shifts. 
        - And that tells you basically how many electrons are on
          the line of sight from Earth to this thing. 
        - Then you have to guess the electron density of
          interstellar space, which is rather hard and probably
          not constant. 
        - But that also gives you a way of measuring distances if
          you don't see any optical light at all.



### SARA radio talk
    - Radio weak signals: use folding to find
    - Optical weak signals: use image stacking
    - Use Fourier if we don't know the period of a pulsar.
    - These are mathematical operations to find periodic
      (repeating) signals in a dataset.
    - In pulsar search, the fourier transform converts from
      "signal vs. time" to "signal vs. frequency". Note:
      frequency is `1 / time`
    - Frequency is measure in Hertz: cycles per second.
    - If you see transient emissions from a pulsar it could be
      RFI interference. But it also could be an eclipsing pulsar
      or even a nulling pulsar (some pulsar emissions turn off
      suddenly and we don't know why)

	- $MY_IMAGES/astro/how-to-identify-pulsars.jpg
    - Pulsar software: PRESTO


### Jana Levin talk: interesting comment
    - Pulsars are still not fully understood, even after 50 years
    - Audience question: The outside of a NS has the same
      solution to Einstein equations as a spinning black hole. 
      - Could Jana Levins calculation say anything about how the
        fields around a NS are formed turning it into a Pulsar.
        -  Jana: Yes a NS is just a black hole that didn't make
           it far enough. It's the Kerr solution. But the
           difference is because it doesn't have a no-hair
           theorem it has an intrinsic dipole, so it's able to
           sustain itself. 
        - But it is also charged. So you just do Gauss's law
          assuming a force-free magnetosphere, and you're going
          to have an electric field `V x B`. And that tells you
          there has to be charge on it by Gauss's law.
    - Random comment: whats the fall-off rate of a B-field around
      a NS?
    - Random: NS's can have quadropole as well as dipole
      B-fields? Jana says you get crazy complicated B-fields. 

### Jana Levin talk: Two NS problems

    1. What is the `EOS`? 
        - Whats the nature of super-conducting matter? Its a big
          mystery, so we don't understand the magnetic fields.

    2. We don't understand where these "gaps" are that allow for

        - Because if it's "4:3" (what's 4:3??) there should be no

          somehow there's these regions where the charges are
          breaking the "force free" rule and becoming

### Pulsars misc
    - discovered at 80MHz... nowadays: 300MHz - 3GHz
    - stars with masses > `8*M_sun`
    - only live millions of years, rather than billions
    - note: a neutron on its own in space, decays quickly into
      a proton and an electron. But bound up with others, it's
      quite stable...
    - very steady pulses. We have an object the mass of the sun
      spinning around. That's a lot of inertia!
    - Pulsars are losing energy as they rotate, but very
      predictably ... (spin-down rate)
    - Very hot - over 1 million ºF
    - Over 100 million times hotter than our sun????
    - have a main pulse and secondary "inter-pulse". If two
      magnetic poles, makes sense we see both
    - 50 years after discovery, still debate about pulse emission
      mechanism
    - all pulsars are neutron stars. But not all neutron stars
      are pulsars.
    - Because of narrow pulsed beams, we probably only see 10% of
      pulsars in our Galaxy
    - neutron stars areant just made of neutrons... they're made
      of iron etc too
