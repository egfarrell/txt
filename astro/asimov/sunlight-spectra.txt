61. WHAT IS SUNLIGHT?

- Sunlight seems simply to be white light, the purest light, and
  its fitting we get this kind from our sun. But at the same time
  its unfortunate, because what information can we get from
  something as pure and simple as white light?

- Human-made light, on the other hand, is not necessarily white.
  Flames produced by burning wood and other fuels tend to be red,
  orange, and yellow and lack the divinely pure quality of the
  heavenly light of the sun. 

- Furthermore, sunlight can be given color if it is passed
  through colored bits of glass, as in stained-glass windows. The
  results are very beautiful, but the color, it was felt,
  resulted from the addition of the impurities introduced to pure
  white light by man-made matter. 

- Even the ruddy light of sunrise or sunset seemed to be from
  sunlight passing through dusty air. In fact, the only case of
  colored visible light seemingly without human or earthly
  intervention was the rainbow, which was considered a divine
  product - a bridge used by the gods or a sign sent by God to
  promise there would never be another Flood.

- In 1665, Isaac Newton investigated sunlight by
  allowing a ray of light to enter a darkened room through
  a chink in a curtain and passing it through a triangular piece
  of glass called a prim. 

- The path of the light ray was bent as it passed through the
  prism, but it was not all bent the same way. Some parts bent
  more than others, and the light that shone on the wall beyond
  the prism was a rainbow. A band of color that started with red
  (the least bent part of the light), then orange, yellow, green,
  blue, and finally violet (the most bent part of the light), all
  the colors changing gradually and blending together This was
  exactly the appearance and the sequence of colors of the
  rainbow,

- Because the band of colors was an insubstantial phenomenon that
  had no mass, Newton called it a light spectrum (spectrum is
  from the Latin word for ghost). The rainbow, is a natural
  spectrum formed when sunlight passes through tiny air droplets
  of water that linger after rain.

Of course, some might have argued that the colors were produced
by the prism, even though the prism was itself colorless, but Newton
took care of that objection by allowing the light ray that had passed
through the prism and formed a spectrum to fall on another prism
that had been turned in the opposite direction from the first, Now the
light, instead of bending and separa ng as before, reversed itself and
came together again. Out of the second prism came white light. It was
clear, then, that sunlight was not pure, but a complex mixture of light
of various colors. When those various colors act on the re na of our
eye, the effect is what we call white light.
a62. WHAT ARE SPECTRAL LINES?

When Newton first studied the light spectrum, it seemed to him to be
continuous. All the varieties of color in the spectrum changed from
one to another without a break. But, in reality, the spectrum is not
quite continuous, and there are some ny gaps in which no color
exists. Historians of science wonder some mes how it was that
Newton didn't no ce this, but he was working with very crude
instruments and the gaps might not have been very no ceable. In
1802, a Bri sh chemist, William Hyde Wollaston (1766?1828), did
no ce a few gaps in the spectrum and reported them, but he didn't
consider them important and let the ma er go.

Naturally, equipment for producing and studying a spectrum (the
devices were called spectroscopes) improved. Eventually, the light was
allowed to pass through a narrow slit so that the spectrum became a
series of slits of different colors that all melted together, so to speak,
to form a nearly continuous band. There were some colors missing,
however, and where that color should have been, there was a dark slit,
or dark line, crossing the bright spectrum.

In 1814, a German physicist, Joseph von Fraunhofer (1787?1826),
was working with spectra produced by be er equipment than anyone
had yet used, and he found nearly six hundred such lines. (Modern
physicists have located some ten thousand.) These were called
Fraunhofer lines at first, but are now be er known as simply spectral
alines. As it turned out, these spectral lines proved to be of supreme
importance.

Different chemical substances give off light of different colors when
heated. Sodium compounds heat to give off yellow light; potassium
compounds yield violet; stron um compounds, red; barium
compounds, green; and so on. Such compounds are used in producing
the spectacular fireworks with which all sorts of occasions are
celebrated.

In 1857, a German chemist, Robert Wilhelm Bunsen (1811?1899),
produced a gas burner so well red with air that it produced a virtually
colorless flame. If it was used to heat a certain chemical, the light
given off would produce a color not confused with any given off by the
burner.

Bunsen's co-worker, the German physicist Gustav Robert Kirchhoff
(1824?1877), used the Bunsen burner to produce light from different
chemicals, He studied the spectra of such light and found that they
were not continuous, but that each was made up of a few sca ered
individual colored lines. What's more, each different element (each
different kind of atom) produced its own pa ern of colored lines. The
spectra thus provided a "fingerprint" for each element, and spectra
could be used as a way of analyzing the elements present in a
par cular mineral.

When a sample of a mineral that has been strongly heated produces
a series of colored lines that are not to be found in the case of any
known element, the implication is that the mineral contains an
uniden fied substance. If the mineral was treated in various ways,
fractions of it could be obtained in which the unknown lines were
stronger, and the unknown element could be isolated, eventually, and
astudied. In this way, Kirchhoff discovered the elements cesium in 1860
and rubidium in 1861. They were named a er the colors of the
spectral lines that iden fied them, cesium from the La n word for sky
blue, and rubidium from the La n word for red.

Kirchhoff went further in his study. When be shone sunlight through
sodium vapor, the vapor absorbed certain portions of the sunlight and
darkened some lines that were already there. He found that every
vapor, if cooler than a light source, would absorb exactly those
portions of the spectrum that it would emit if it were itself heated. In
other words, you could iden fy elements (or simple compounds of
those elements, for that ma er) as bright lines against a dark
background if the elements were heated and made to give off light, or
as dark lines against a bright background if the elements were
rela vely cool and were absorbing light. It was through spectral lines,
for instance, that carbon dioxide was first iden fied in the
atmospheres of Venus and Mars.
