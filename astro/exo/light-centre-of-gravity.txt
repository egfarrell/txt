### "Centre of Gravity" of Light in Kepler's Analysis

- The "centre of gravity" of light refers to the "centroid" of
  the light distribution recorded by the Kepler spacecraft's
  detectors.
- This centroid is the `average position of all light` from the
  star (or stars) in the image, and it helps determine where the
  light is coming from.

1. How to Determine the "Centre of Gravity" (Centroid) of Light:

    - Light Distribution:
        - When Kepler observes a star, the star's light is spread
          across several pixels on the detector, creating a light
          distribution or Point Spread Function (PSF). The PSF is
          usually symmetric and centered around the star's
          position.

    - Calculating the Centroid:
        - The centroid is calculated by weighting the position of
          each pixel by its brightness (intensity) and then
          taking the average position. Mathematically, if you
          have pixel positions (x_i, y_i) and their corresponding
          intensities I_i, the centroid (x_c, y_c) is given by:
        - `xc = (Σ (xi * Ii)) / (Σ Ii)`
        - `yc = (Σ (yi * Ii)) / (Σ Ii)`

        - `xi` and `yi` are the positions of each pixel,
        - `Ii` is the intensity of the light at each pixel,
        - `Σ` represents the summation over all relevant pixels.
        - This calculation gives you the "centre of gravity" of
          the light distribution on the detector.

2. Detecting an "Unexpected Shift" in the Centroid During
Transit:

    - Normal Shift (Expected):
        - During a transit, if the planet is orbiting the target
          star, you might expect a slight shift in the centroid
          due to the reduction in light. However, this shift
          should be small and consistent with the star's known
          position and the expected depth of the transit.

    - Unexpected Shift:
        - If the centroid shifts more than expected or in an
          unusual direction, this could indicate that the transit
          signal isn't coming from the star you think it's coming
          from. Instead, it might be from a background star (a
          star near the target in the sky but further away in
          distance).
        - For example, if the light's centroid shifts
          significantly to one side during the transit, it could
          mean that a fainter, nearby background star is being
          eclipsed, causing the observed dimming.

3. Deciding if the Shift is "Unexpected":

    - Comparing with Models:
        - The expected shift can be modeled based on the known
          properties of the star, the transit depth, and the
          instrument's characteristics. If the observed shift
          deviates significantly from this model, it is
          considered "unexpected."

    - Statistical Analysis:
        - By analyzing many transits, astronomers can establish
          a baseline for what is "normal" or "expected" in terms
          of centroid shifts for that star. Any deviation beyond
          this baseline can signal an unexpected shift.

    - Multi-epoch Observations:
        - Observing multiple transits and checking if the shift
          is consistent or erratic helps determine if it's
          unexpected. A consistent, minor shift might be normal,
          while an erratic or large shift suggests something
          unusual, like a background star being responsible for
          the transit signal.

- Conclusion:
    - In summary, the "centre of gravity" of light is determined
      by calculating the centroid of the light distribution on
      the detector. Any unexpected shift in this centroid during
      a transit, especially when compared to models or
      statistical baselines, can indicate that the transit might
      be due to a background star rather than the target star,
      leading to further investigation.
