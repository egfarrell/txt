### Spectral domain
    - Diagram: $MY_IMAGES/astro/exo/multi-approaches.jpg

    - KEY: Bigger `Rp/Rs` ratio = More light blocked (or
      scattered) by the planet, usually due to absorption by
      specific atmospheric gases.
    - 'occulted area' = another name for `Rp/Rs` ratio?
    - 'transit depth' = another name for `Rp/Rs` ratio?

    - When the planet-to-star radius ratio (Rp/Rs) gets bigger,
      it means the planet appears larger relative to the
      star at that specific wavelength, which indicates that more
      light from the star is being blocked by the planet's
      atmosphere.

    - Higher Rp/Rs Ratio:
        - A higher ratio means that the apparent size of the
          planet is larger at that particular wavelength.
        - This larger apparent size occurs because the planet's
          atmosphere is absorbing or scattering more of the
          starlight at that wavelength, effectively "blocking"
          more light.
        - Physically, this suggests the atmosphere is more opaque
          at that wavelength due to the presence of certain gases
          or particles that absorb or scatter light.

    - Lower Rp/Rs Ratio:
        - A lower ratio indicates that less of the star's light
          is being blocked by the planet at that wavelength.
        - This typically means that the atmosphere is more
          transparent at that wavelength, allowing more starlight
          to pass through with minimal absorption or scattering.

    - ### Summary:
        - Bigger Rp/Rs ratio = More light blocked by the planet,
          usually due to absorption by specific atmospheric
          gases.
        - Smaller Rp/Rs ratio = Less light blocked, indicating
          more transparency in the planet's atmosphere at that
          wavelength.

    - This relationship between the Rp/Rs ratio and the amount of
      light blocked is fundamental to understanding what the
      spectral domain plot reveals about the exoplanet's
      atmosphere.



### inclination angle
    - Diagram: $MY_IMAGES/astro/exo/inclination-angle.jpg
    - For exoplanets: two types of 'inclinations'
        - The inclination of exoplanets or members of multi-star
          star systems is the angle of the `plane of the orbit`
          relative to the plane perpendicular to the line of
          sight from Earth to the object. (i.e. sky plane)
        - An inclination of `0°` is a face-on orbit, meaning the
          plane of the exoplanet's orbit is perpendicular to the
          line of sight with Earth.
        - An inclination of `90°` is an edge-on orbit, meaning
          the plane of the exoplanet's orbit is parallel to the
          line of sight with Earth.

        - Since the word "inclination" is used in exoplanet
          studies for this `line-of-sight inclination`, the angle
          between the planet's orbit and its star's rotational
          axis is expressed using the term the "spin-orbit angle"
          or "spin-orbit alignment". 
        - In most cases the orientation of the star's rotational
          axis is unknown.

### Rossiter-McLaughlin effect
    - At the start of a transit, the planet obscures a small
      portion of the approaching limb of the star, thus reducing
      the intensity on the short wavelength edges of the observed
      spectrum lines. The lines therefore appear to move slightly
      to longer wavelengths (i.e. a red- shift). 

    - At the end of the transit, the planet obscures a small part
      of the star’s receding limb, and the spectrum lines appear
      to move to shorter wavelengths (a blue-shift). 

    - This change in the wavelengths of the star’s spectrum lines
      during a transit of its exoplanet is called the
      Rossiter-McLaughlin effect and careful computer modeling of
      the changes gives the angle between the equator and orbital
      plane. 

    - For HD 189733 b, for example, the angle is just 0.85° – as
      might be expected if star and planet formed from a single
      rotating nebula (Chap. 13) – for comparison the angle
      between the Earth’s orbit and the Sun’s equator is 7.25°.

    - interesting: 
        - The RMcL effect shows 45% - 85% of hot jupiters are
          misaligned with the star spin by more than 30 degrees

### My MPhil Masters abstract
    - Title: Population Synthesis Modelling - Eclipsing Binary
      Contamination in the PLATO field

    - Authors: Farrell, Farmer & Kolb

    - Abstract: 
        - We perform a series of binary population simulations to
          estimate the number and type of eclipsing binaries in
          a given PLATO field-of-view.

        - We present preliminary results outlining the
          distribution of simulated binary eclipse depths. This
          distibution is used to provide an estimate of the false
          positive rate for exoplanet transit detections from
          astrophysical blends and grazing binaries.  This may
          inform fine-tuning of the PLATO field selection and
          pointing. 

        - The population synthesis model uses a rapid stellar and
          binary evolution scheme as in Hurley et al. (2000,
          2002) and employs a 3D galactic dust extinction model
          from Drimmel et al. (2003).  The models allow
          a determination of both the number density and
          properties of eclipsing systems for a given magnitude
          range as a function of Galactic longitude and latitude.

    - References:
        - Drimmel et al., 2003, A&A, 409, 205

        - Hurley et al., 2002, MNRAS, 329, 897
        - Willems et al., 2002, MNRAS, 337, 1004

### thesis 0.2 percent EB probability
    - Geometric: `0.5` percent probability of earth sun analogue being lined
      up properly in your line of sight or `10%` at very best...

    - kepler FOV - full hand on sky
    - full moon between one of the gaps

    - size/radius of planet:
        - depth of light curve dip
        - ie size of dimming

    - period of planet
        - length of time between transit dips
        - ie intervals between dimming of light

    - Need 3 years of data to detect planets with 1 year orbital
      periods. Why 3 Years for a 1-Year Orbital Period?
        - First Transit Detection (`Year 1`):
            - When a planet with a 1-year orbital period passes
              in front of its host star, it causes a slight
              dimming of the star's light. ("transit"). The first
              time this happens, you can't be sure it's a planet.
              It might be a one-time event due to some other
              cause, like stellar activity or noise in the data.
        - Second Transit (`Year 2`):
            - After one full year, the planet (if it exists) will
              transit the star again. Observing a second transit
              at exactly the same time interval strengthens the
              hypothesis that there is indeed a planet orbiting
              the star. However, two transits might not be
              sufficient to rule out other possibilities, like
              a binary star system or some periodic stellar
              activity.
        - Third Transit (`Year 3`):
            - By observing a third transit, you have now seen the
              planet pass in front of the star three times, each
              separated by the same 1-year period. This third
              detection is critical for confirming the planet's
              existence and its orbital period. With three
              transits, the periodicity is well established,
              reducing the likelihood that the observed dimming
              is due to some other cause.
        - Why Not Just Two Years?
            - Confirmation and Consistency:
                - With only two transits, there's still a chance
                  that the observed pattern might be due to
                  a coincidental alignment of other factors, like
                  a binary star system or instrumental noise.
                  A third transit provides additional evidence,
                  solidifying the conclusion that a planet is
                  orbiting the star with a 1-year period.
            - Accounting for Gaps and Noise:
                - Observations might be affected by gaps in data
                  or noise. Having three years' worth of data
                  helps to ensure that even if one of the
                  transits is missed or noisy, the other two can
                  still provide strong evidence.
        - Thus, three years of observation ensure that the
          transit signal is consistent, periodic, and can be
          distinguished from other potential sources of
          variability.

### TEP signature, compared to EB
    - During transit, the star color doesnt change for a TEP.
    - For a TEP, you have the same transit depth on different
      instruments.
    - But another star transiting (EB) will result in a different
      color.
    - Explanation:
        - Transiting Exoplanet (TEP): A planet that passes in
          front of its host star as seen from Earth, causing
          a temporary dimming of the star's light. This dimming
          event is detected by instruments and is referred to as
          a "transit."
        - EB (Eclipsing Binary): An Eclipsing Binary is a system
          of two stars orbiting each other such that, from our
          viewpoint, one star passes in front of the other,
          causing periodic dimming. This can sometimes be
          mistaken for a transiting exoplanet, but there are key
          differences in the signatures.
        - Differences Between TEP and EB:
            - Star Color During Transit:
                - For a TEP, the star's color doesn't change
                  during the transit because the planet is
                  typically much smaller than the star and does
                  not affect the light's color significantly.
                - For an EB, the color can change because the two
                  stars involved might have different
                  temperatures and colors, so when one star
                  eclipses the other, the combined light from the
                  system can change in color.
            - Transit Depth Across Different Instruments:
                - For a TEP, the transit depth (amount of light
                  blocked) should remain consistent when observed
                  with different instruments, assuming the
                  instruments are calibrated similarly.
                - For an EB, especially if the stars have
                  different colors, different instruments
                  (sensitive to different wavelengths of light)
                  might detect different transit depths due to
                  the varying contributions of the two stars'
                  light.
            - These distinctions help astronomers differentiate
              between a true transiting exoplanet (TEP) and other
              phenomena like eclipsing binaries (EB).
