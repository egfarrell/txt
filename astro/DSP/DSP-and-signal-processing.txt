### High pass filter
    - https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=i76B1pBoN4Y
    - attenuate the low frequencies
    - used for edge detection or sharpening
    - ie: where is my signal changing the most? Where is it the
      most dynamic?
    - A simple high-pass filter is [`-1`, `1`]
        - this is the difference of the current data point, minus
          the previous data point
        - it's like taking the derivative!
    - the low frequency areas will go to zero... because there's
      not much difference between one point and the next


### Low pass filter
    - purpose: get rid of NOISE
        - noise is generally high frequency
        - not a lot of correlation with noise between
          neighbouring time intervals
    - ritvikmath https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=i76B1pBoN4Y
    - Low pass filter: basically smooth out high frequency parts
      of the signal where there's a lot happening (because it's
      probably noise)
        - i.e. let the low frequencies pass and _attenuate_ the
          high frequencies
    - A simple low-pass filter is [`0.5`, `0.5`]
        - This is a 2-element "kernel"
    - You _convolve_ this filter with the signal
        - How?
        - Sweep the filter across the signal, and for every pair
          of points, take the SUM of `0.5` of the left point, and
          `0.5` of the right point. That's the _transformed_
          signal.
            - That's it! You're really taking the _mean_ of
              side-by-side pairs in the signal...
            - Or the equal-weighted linear combination of
              side-by-side points in your signal...
            - Result: it _smooths_ out your signal
                - $MY_IMAGES/astro/DSP/low-pass-filter-convolve.jpg
            - we kept the areas of low frequencies and the areas
              of high frequency have been attenuated
                - $MY_IMAGES/astro/DSP/low-pass-filter-convolve-2.jpg

### white noise
    - ritvikmath
    - https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cr4zIXAmSRI
    - Must satisfy 3 criteria:
        - Mean = 0
        - Standard Deviation is constant with time
        - Correlation between lags is 0
        - $MY_IMAGES/astro/DSP/definition-white-noise.jpg
    - One of the biggest properties of white noise:
        - it is _not predictable_
    - Note: `y(t)  = signal + noise`
        - you try and fit a model (to the signal part) as best you can
        - anything that's left over are the residuals (errors?)
        - If you can prove your residuals are really close to
          white noise, then you've done all you can to model the
          signal
        - $MY_IMAGES/astro/DSP/why-is-white-noise-important.jpg


### Stochastic resonance
    - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_resonance
    - Stochastic resonance (SR) is a phenomenon where a signal
      normally too weak to be detected, can be boosted by adding
      white noise to it, which contains a wide spectrum of
      frequencies.
    - The frequencies in the white noise corresponding to the
      original signal's frequencies will resonate with each
      other, amplifying the original signal while not amplifying
      the rest of the white noise – thereby increasing the
      signal-to-noise ratio, which makes the original signal more
      prominent. 
        - Further, the added white noise can be enough to be
          detectable by the sensor, which can then filter it out
          to effectively detect the original, previously
          undetectable signal.
    - This phenomenon of boosting undetectable signals by
      resonating with added white noise extends to other systems
      – whether EM, physical or biological. It's an active area
      of research.

### filtering
    - Digital filtering aims to reduce noise in time series data
      or to compress data. Common examples include
        - LOW-PASS filtering
            - where high frequencies are suppressed
        - HIGH-PASS
            - where low frequencies are suppressed
        - PASSBAND filtering
            - where only a finite range of frequencies is
              admitted
        - a NOTCH FIlter
            - where a finite range of frequencies is blocked.

### What is stationary/non-stationary signal?
    - There is no stationary signal. Stationary and
      non-stationary are characterisations of the process that
      generated the signal.
    - A signal is an observation. A recording of something that
      happened. A recording of a series of events as a result of
      some _process_.
    - If the properties of the process that generates the events
      DOES NOT change in time, then the process is stationary.
    - We know that a signal `x(n)` is a collection of events
      (measurements) at different time instances `(n)`.
    - But how can we describe the _process_ that generated it?
    - Another viewpoint:
        - One more point: stationarity or nonstationarity are
          generally only applied to _stochastic signals_, not
          deterministic signals.
        - In general, when statistical tests are applied for
          stationarity or nonstationarity, the deterministic
          component must be removed first.
        - Hence, in my view, numbers 3, 4, 5 are nonsensical
          because they contain no stochastic component and,
          therefore, cannot be considered either stationary or
          nonstationary
            - 3. chirp
            - 4. sinus
            - 5. sum of multiple sinuses with different periods
              and amplitudes

### Wavelets
    - Fourier Transforms do not represent _ABRUPT_ changes
      efficiently. Why?
        - FT represents data as a sum of sine waves which are
          _not well localised_ in time or space
    - To analyse data with abrupt changes we need a new class of
      functions which are well localised in time and space.
        - Wavelet: rapidly decaying oscillation with zero mean.
        - Unlike sinusoids which exist to infinity, wavelets
          exist for a finite duration.
        - $MY_IMAGES/astro/DSP/types-of-wavelet.jpg
    - Two wavelet concepts:
        - SCALING
            - scaling factor affects frequency
            - $MY_IMAGES/astro/DSP/wavelet-scaling-factor.jpg
        - SHIFTING
            - slide the wavelet along a signal
            - "the wavelet is shifted and centred at `k`"
            - $MY_IMAGES/astro/DSP/wavelet-shifting.jpg
    - TWO wavelet transforms
        - Continuous Wavelet Transform
        - Discrete Wavelet Transform
            - Denoising
            - Compressing signals or images

### Intro MOOC course
    - The masterpiece is Shannon’s 1948 paper: “The Mathematical
      Theory of Communication”.
    - It revolutionized how communication systems are designed
      and built, and we mostly live in its legacy.
    - One of the key results is the sampling theorem for
      bandlimited functions (often attributed to Shannon, since
      it appears in the above-mentioned paper), the theorem which
      single-handedly enabled the digital revolution.
    - To mathematicians, this is a simple corollary to Fourier
      series, and they might suggest other ways to represent such
      functions.
    - However, the strength of the sampling theorem and its
      variations (e.g. oversampling or quantization) is that it
      is an operational theorem, robust, and applicable to actual
      signal acquisition and reconstruction problems.
    - Finally, the adjective "digital" comes from digitus, the
      Latin word for finger: 
        - it concisely describes a world view where everything
          can be represented as an integer number.
    - Counting, on one’s fingers or in one's head is the earliest
      form of abstraction; as children we learn that counting
      does indeed bring disparate objects (the proverbial “apples
      and oranges”) into a common modeling paradigm, i.e. their
      CARDINALITY.
    - DSP is a flavor of signal processing in which everything
      INCLUDING TIME is described in terms of _INTEGER_ numbers.
      I.e., the abstract representation of choice is
      a one-size-fit-all COUNTABILITY

### MOOC intro
    - In the history of science, 2 models for our world have
      always existed:
        - an idealized abstraction describiing things using
          functions and calculus,
        - a more pragmatic approach describing things via sets of
          numerical measurements.
    - DSP is the crowning achievement of this latter approach,
      and the ability (via the _Sampling Theorem_) to bridge DSP
      to classical mathematical models of reality is why we are
      so successful at harnessing information.

### Units
    - The dimension for frequency used to be "cycles/second";
      thats why the tuning dials of old radios indicate frequency
      as kilocycles/second (kcps) or megacycles/second (Mcps).
    - In 1960 the scientific community adopted `hertz` as the
      frequency unit of measure in honor of Heinrich Hertz, who
      first demonstrated radio wave transmission and reception in
      1887.
