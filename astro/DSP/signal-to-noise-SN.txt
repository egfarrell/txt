- "signal-to-noise ratio" (S/N or SNR) is a fundamental concept
  that measures the quality of a signal by: 
    - comparing its strength (the "signal") 
    - to the level of background noise or unwanted variation
      ("noise").

- Understanding this ratio is crucial for interpreting
  astronomical observations, where faint signals are often buried
  in noise.

### 1. Basic Idea of Signal and Noise
   - Signal:
        - a signal could be the light coming from a star, galaxy,
          or other celestial object. This light is collected by
          a telescope and detected by an instrument like a camera
          or spectrograph.

   - Noise:
        - Noise represents random fluctuations or interference
          that obscure the true signal. It can come from various
          sources, including:
        - Instrumental noise
            - (e.g., imperfections in the detector).
        - Sky noise
            - (e.g., light pollution, atmospheric turbulence).
        - Statistical noise
            - (e.g., photon shot noise, which arises from the
              random arrival of photons).


- The signal is the information we care about, the noise is the
  unwanted variation that makes it harder to accurately detect
  and measure that signal.

### 2. Mathematical Definition
    - The signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) is defined mathematically as:
    - `S/N = S / N,`
        - where:
            - `S` is the strength of the signal.
            - `N` is the strength of the noise.

- The ratio indicates how strong the signal is compared to the
  noise. A higher S/N means a clearer detection, while a lower
  S/N means the signal is harder to distinguish from the noise.

### 3. First Principles: Counting Photons
    - In astronomy, most detectors are photon-counting devices,
      meaning they count individual photons (particles of light)
      that hit the detector. The signal strength is proportional
      to the number of photons detected from the source, while
      noise usually has several components.

    - Signal (S):
        - The signal is proportional to the number of photons
          detected from the astronomical object (let's denote
          this as N_signal). If a detector collects N_signal
          photons over a given period of time, the signal
          strength is related to this photon count.

    - Noise (N):
        - The noise comes from multiple sources; in many cases,
          a key contributor is _Poisson noise_ (or _shot noise_),
          which arises because photons arrive randomly. The
          Poisson noise is proportional to the square root of the
          total number of photons detected, including both the
          signal photons and background photons (`N_total`).
          Mathematically, the noise due to 'shot noise' is
          roughly `N_total^1/2`.

- In cases where the noise is dominated by shot noise, the
  signal-to-noise ratio becomes:

    - S/N ≈ N_signal / sqrt(N_total).

    - If N_total ≈ N_signal (i.e., the background noise is negligible), this reduces to:

        - S/N ≈ sqrt(N_signal).

    - This means the signal-to-noise ratio improves as the square
      root of the number of detected photons. This relationship
      is important because it tells us that to increase the `S/N`
      by a factor of 2, we need to detect 4 times as many
      photons, which often means longer exposure times.


### 4. Other Noise Sources

- In addition to Poisson noise, there are other forms of noise
  contributing to the overall noise level:

   - Readout noise:
        - Comes from the electronic process of READING out the
          signal from a detector.
   - Dark current:
        - Noise due to THERMAL electrons in the detector, even in
          the absence of light.
   - Sky background:
        - Caused by the brightness of the night sky, which is
          very significant for ground-based observations.

- Each of these sources adds to the total noise, and they often
  combine in quadrature. For example, if you have multiple
  independent sources of noise (`N1`, `N2`, ..., `Nn`), the total
  noise `N_total` is:

    - N_total = sqrt(N1^2 + N2^2 + ... + Nn^2).

### 5. Improving S/N ratio
    - Several strategies can improve it:
        - Longer exposures:
            - Collecting more photons over a longer time
              increases the signal and, due to Poisson
              statistics, also increases the noise. However, the
              signal increases faster than the noise, improving
              the S/N.
        - Better instruments:
            - Reducing the contribution of instrumental noise
              (e.g., readout noise or dark current) can improve
              the S/N.
        - Larger telescopes:
            - A larger telescope collects more light, increasing
              the number of detected photons and thereby
              improving the signal.

### 6. Example in Astronomical Observations
    - Imagine you are observing a faint star. The signal you get
      from the star depends on the number of photons the
      telescope collects.
    - Let's say the telescope collects `1000 photons` from the
      star in a given exposure time. The Poisson noise in this
      case would be `sqrt(1000) ≈ 31.6 photons`.

    - Therefore, the S/N would be:
            - `S/N = 1000 / 31.6`
            - `≈ 31.6`

    - This is a relatively good S/N ratio, meaning the star's
      signal is clear relative to the background noise.

    - If you extend the exposure time and collect `4000` photons,
      the noise would increase to   `sqrt(4000) ≈ 63.2 photons`,
      and the S/N would now be:
        - `S/N = 4000 / 63.2`
        - `≈ 63.2`
        - which is twice as good.

### Summary
    - The S/N ratio is a critical metric in astronomy to assess
      the quality of observational data.
    - It is based on counting photons from celestial objects and
      comparing their signal strength to random fluctuations
      (noise).
    - A high S/N means a strong, clear signal, while a low S/N
      suggests the data is overwhelmed by noise.
    - Increasing S/N typically requires longer observations,
      better equipment, or more advanced noise reduction
      techniques.
