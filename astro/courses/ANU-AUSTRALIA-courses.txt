### Misc: Work out Stellar MASS

    - PAUL: So if we see a star out there, can we calculate how
      far it is and how massive?

    - BRIAN: So stars roughly tell us how _massive_ they are by
      how _hot_ they are and how puffy they are, what their
      _radius_ is.

    - Its easy to measure the temperature of a star. Just look at
      its color essentially. And the bluer it is, the hotter it
      is.

    - And it turns out we can measure the temperature to probably
      within 100 degrees pretty easily. And that gives us,
      essentially, through the black body equation, how much
      _energy_ it puts out if we know its area.

    - Now the _area_ is harder, but there is one indication we
      can do. 
        - And that is we can measure the _gravity_ of the star,
          because the gravity of the star affects the way
          `hydrogen lines` are formed.

    - PAUL: So you're talking about the surface gravity, how
      strong gravity is standing on the surface of the star.

    - BRIAN: Yes we know here on Earth, it's 9.8 meters per
      second squared. And we can essentially measure that same
      thing on stars.

    - Because it turns out the way `hydrogen spectral lines` look
      depends on how much gravity there is in that.

    - Once we've estimated surface gravity, then: whats that
      surface gravity equal to?
        - It's a combination of MASS and the RADIUS of the star.
          (mass and distance)

    - But we don't know that ... except for we sort of do.

    - And that's because when stars evolve, their nuclear
      reactors evolve. They sit in very specific places between
      temperature and the total energy they're putting out.

    - We can all these together and actually say e.g. "that star
      is a red giant about twice the mass of our sun that has
      this temperature and this surface gravity".
        - And we can sort of pick it out in that thing with that
          amount of information.

    - PAUL: OK. So what we can measure is _the temperature and
      the surface gravity_.
        - But the surface gravity doesn't tell us either the mass
          or the radius, but a combination of the two. So you
          need some other clue.

    And that _other clue_ comes from either

        1. Our _models_ of stars: Eg surface gravity is enough to
           tell us whether its a red giant or a red dwarf. And we
           know all red dwarfs are practically the same and all
           red giants are kind of the same too.

       2. Or it could be something we measure _empirically_:
          because often we will find stars that are much nearer,
          so we can actually measure the its distance by
          something like parallax.
            - We look, as the Earth goes around the sun, we see
              how the angle changes and work out how far away it
              is.
            - And if it's in a binary companion, we can use the
              orbital with a normal star. So maybe like Alpha
              Centauri, two yellow dwarf stars orbiting each
              other- we can use that to try and measure the mass
              of the two things.
            - So we can use that to calibrate our theoretical
              models.

    - BRIAN: That's right. So we have these models. And then
      there is this scaffolding of being able to measure
      distances through parallax, measuring different things such
      as the mass, and building the whole thing up and making it
      self-consistent. So 100 years of astrophysics really is
      calibrating those models.

        - And they're pretty good now. Once you have essentially
          that Hertzsprung-Russell diagram, which really tells
          you these things, you literally can use that to say,
          it's one of those stars. So it's very useful.

    - PAUL: So we get a detailed spectrum, look at the exact
      shape of the lines, and that gives-- and particularly the
      ratios -- different spectral lines. That tells us surface
      gravity.

        - We know the color, therefore we can measure the
          temperature. That tells us it's going to be, say,
          a 6,000-degree star, either a dwarf star or a giant
          star or possibly a white dwarf or a supergiant.
        - Surface gravity tells us which type of star it is. And
          once we know this, we can compare it with nearer ones
          or with theoretical models and know how massive these
          things are or how luminous they are or what their
          radius is.

    - BRIAN: Absolutely. So that's essentially the way we do it.
      Occasionally, we get lucky. And there's a way to measure
      the distance to the system itself.
        - In those cases we can literally read off how bright it
          is, how far away it is, and there has to be some sort
          of geometric conspiracy which gives us that little
          insight in this case, often related to parallax. 
        - But most of the time, we don't have that. So this is
          a way do it for most stars.

    - PAUL: I know for some sources, if there are only radio wave
      lengths, you can't see the companion star.
        - It's much harder to get the distances eg, for pulsars.
          In this case, we use a trick called rotation measure.
        - Often the radio waves coming from these things are
          polarized, so the radio waves, will be going up and
          down in this direction.
        - But as they travel through interstellar space, the
          radio waves go past electrons, and they cause the
          electrons to jiggle. 
            - And that rotates the polarisation.
        - But it tilts it differently depending on whether it's
          a low frequency or a high frequency wave.
        - So the high-frequency waves are rotated less.
        - The low-frequency ones are rotated more.
        - So what you find is that as you change your frequency,
          you can see that the angle of polarization shifts.
        - And that tells you basically how many electrons are on
          the line of sight from Earth to this thing.
        - Then you have to guess the electron density of
          interstellar space, which is rather hard and probably
          not constant. But that also gives you a way of
          measuring distances if you don't see any optical light
          at all.

    - BRIAN: That's right. So what astronomers have done is
      they've really mapped out essentially the number of
      electrons in the Milky Way, through another 100 years of
      astrophysics-- and so any direction we know where distance
      is versus how many electrons there are and how much the
      radio has rotated in its polarization. 
        - And that gives us probably a measurement, which is good
          to within a factor of 2 usually, sometimes quite a bit
          better. But it's a good rough measure.

    - PAUL: And there are other tricks you can play. Eg. if
      something's in front of a giant molecular cloud. Then you
      know if it was behind you wouldn't see it.
        - If you see absorption lines from something, say, from
          a nebula, then you know it must be behind it. So you
          can get relative distances sometimes this sort of way.
          It can be quite hard, though.

    - BRIAN: Yeah. But within a factor of two, and most of
      astronomy is factors of two, then we really can sort things
      out here.

    - PAUL: The bottom line: its possible to measure the masses
      of these stars. How you do so is complicated. And as Brian
      said: it's 100 years of astrophysics.
    - It's not always very reliable. Sometimes it can be very
      reliable. 
        - It's a bit beyond the scope of this course. So for the
          problems in this course, we'll just tell you the masses
          of the companion star.
