### Mass exchange

      a companion

    - IMPORTANT PARAMETERS:
    - Eddington Luminosity
        - Can radiation flux from a body be arbitrarily high? 
        - Photon energy: 
            - `E^2 = p^2c^2 + m^2c^2`
            - but m = 0
            - so `E = pc`
        - the action of radiation (photons) on a electron results
          in a change of _momentum_
        - the momentum is: 
            -`p = E/c`
                - because: `E = pc` 
        - and the momentum flux density (momentum rate per unit
          _surface_) is
            - => `p = Flux/c`
            - divide the "energy/flux" by light-speed

        - but we need to know the "surface" of the electron
            - ie need to know how "large" the electron is
            - ie need the rate of photon interception
            - ie need the Thomson cross-section `σ_T`
            - note: 
                - the Thompson cross-section is purely classical,
                  No Plancks constant used. That's kind of amazing.
            - note: this Thompson formula is for full ionised
              material

        
        - Eddington Luminosity formula
        - we express it in units of solar mass 
        - $MY_IMAGES/astro/bh-accretion/eddington-luminosity-derivation.jpg

        - the mass of the proton appears, not the electron
          mass... because 
            - the plasma is neutral 
            - and if you try to push away one electron, one
              proton will follow...

        - humans shine at the eddington luminosity!
        - Thompson Scattering AstroBaki 
            - https://casper.ssl.berkeley.edu/astrobaki/index.php/Thomson_Scattering

    - Mass
        - In astronomy, we frequently determine the mass of an
          object by the measurement of the motion of a satellite
          around the object.
        - If we can measure the velocity of the satellite, and
          the size of the orbit (or the period), we assume
          a Keplarian motion on a circular orbit: 
        - `GM/R = v^2`
        - so we just need the proper choice of satellite: 
            - binary stars: satellite = companion in the optical 
            - AGN: satellite = stars 
            - AGN: satellite = or gaseous clouds 
                - note: Broad emission lines (BLR) come from
                  gaseous clouds
                - we measure the velocity from the line width
                - we measure the orbit size from the delay of the
                  lines wrt the continuum

    - Measuring accretion efficiency 
        - radius of emitting source = shift in the photon
          (wavelength)
        - optical depth = number of photon scatterings 
        - We have two expressions (because we don't know in
          advance if this medium is optically thick or optically
          thin) 
        - But often in astronomy, if we have two limits, we often
          invent a kind of "bridging" formula
    - if you have `η >> 1` then they are blazars and you have

    - Soltan argument 1982 
        - to calculate mean quasar efficiency 
        - first the mass: the amount of mass which accumulates at
          the centre of a quasar of luminosity `L` (as a black
          hole) during its lifetime `T` is:
            - $MY_IMAGES/Soltan-argument-01.jpg
        - you dont know how the luminosity of the "stupid quasar"
          :) changed during its lifetime
        - instead of studying a single quasar we use statistics
          and calculate the total energy produced in 1 Gpc^3.
        - instead of integrating over time, because we don't know
          how to do it... we integrate over redshift as
          a proxy...
          - ie we assume the cosmological principle: the number
            of quasars of liminosity L and age t is statistically
            the same as the number of quasars of luminosity L and
            of redshift z (where `z` corresponds to time `t`)

        - Davis & Laor (2010)

            - higher mass sources have higher spin
