


### Review
    - I've always been interested in cosmology and purchased
      OBSERVATIONAL COSMOLOGY as a followup book to RELATIVITY,
      GRAVITATION, AND COSMOLOGY by Robert Lambourne which I had just
      finished working through. OBSERVATIONAL COSMOLOGY was mentioned
      as a book readers should consider following Lambourne's book.
    - Unfortunately, I found quite a gap between the two in my ability
      to understand them. Whereas I was able to understand and do
      almost all the exercises in RELATIVITY, GRAVITATION, AND
      COSMOLOGY, I was not as successful in OBSERVATIONAL COSMOLOGY.
      Be warned, Serjeant's book is clearly a graduate school level
      presentation of the topic.
    - Ch 1 Space and Time. Topics include Olber's paradox, Space-time
      metrics, Redshift, Cosmological parameters, Curvature of space,
      Distance measuring, and the Fate of the universe. The topics are
      mathematically rigorous and many assume previous knowledge of
      the topics. Many ideas are started mathematically assuming some
      prior knowledge of where the formulas come from...just as you
      would expect in a graduate level text!
    - Ch 2 The Cosmic Microwave Background. Explanations of some
      observations such as the cosmic microwave black-body radiation
      get very complex with ideas and mathematical issues the author
      describes as taking us into "very deep waters". Therefore, the
      author summarizes some arguments citing facts the reader has to
      take on faith because their complexity is beyond the level of
      this book to explain. This left me confused on many occasions.
      Most of chapter 2 requires an extensive background in Particle
      Physics, Nuclear Physics, Nucleosynthesis, and Thermodynamics.
      Even though I have a Master's Degree in physics I had trouble
      coping with a lot of the material. While some of the ideas were
      qualitatively understandable, their mathematical complexities
      overwhelmed me.
    - Ch 3 The Local Universe. I found the qualitative reading quite
      interesting and mostly understandable. Topics such as Evidence
      for dark matter, Hubble's galaxy classification, Details about
      spiral and elliptical galaxies, Galaxy clusters, and the Large
      scale structure of the universe were very readable. However, the
      author also refers to and discusses quantitatively things like
      galaxy surface brightness, star velocities, and mass-luminosity
      relationships using equations relating multiple quantities in
      complex analyses hard to understand if you're not a professional
      cosmologist. It was very interesting but too detailed for me.
      I liked it in a way, however, even if I couldn't understand it
      all. I liked it because it gives a glimpse as to how detailed
      observational cosmology really is.
    - Ch 4,5,6,7,8 The Distant Universe, Distant Optical Universes,
      Distant Multi-Wavelength Universe, Black Holes, Gravitational
      Lensing, The Intervening Universe. All of these chapters in the
      remaining of this book start out qualitatively but very rapidly
      become very complex mathematically. For me, I couldn't
      understand the majority of the mathematical presentations. The
      book is clearly geared to giving research level descriptions of
      the topics researchers in those areas would understand. As such,
      I feel the book would be a wonderful resource to an astronomy
      student going into graduate work in cosmology.
    - I'd say the book is well organized. Stephen Serfjeant is clearly
      a master of the material and knows what he is talking about.
      Many of the illustrations are in color. Also, very importantly
      for someone doing self-study, all the exercises in the book have
      solutions shown in the back
