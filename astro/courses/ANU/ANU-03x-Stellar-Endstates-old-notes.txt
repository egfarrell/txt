
### Frauenhoffer telescope
    - split mirror into two halves, took 2 images of the sky
    - could see if any star moved, by comparing the 2 images

### Bessel (Prussian)
    - used Frauenhoffer telescopes
    - bessel spent a huge time weeding out systematic errors
      (2 years)
    - but Brian Schmidt has spent 6 years with his telescope
      systematics
    - finally measured stellar parallax (he was the first to
      be believed because his data was so good)
    - then he took his telescope apart, reassembled it and

### Sirus (Dog Year)
    - Besssel found a (huge) wobble of 7 arcseconds for
      Sirus (it was moving in an ellipse)
    - what are the properties of the (hidden) star that is
      making Sirus orbit in an ellipse (circle)?

- so they found the hidden companion (Sirus B) with the mass
  of the sun but very faint.

- but how do you make something faint? well make it cool
  (cos we know from the Blackbody law, that hot things glow
  brighter!) So Sirus B was assumed to be cool...

- but 50 years later the Yerkes telescope took the spectrum
  of Sirius B, and they found it was hot!

- now good question: Sirius B is very hot, small and dense,
  so it must be undergoing _nuclear fusion_?

- now a discussion about how to calc the pressure in the
  middle of a star

# how many neutron stars?
    - 1 supernovae explosion every second across the
      universe
    - 1 supernovae explosion every 100 years in our galaxy
    - the Milky Way is 10^10 (10 billion) years old, and
      with a supernovae explosion very 10^2 years, there
      should be 10^8 (100 million) neutron stars in the
      Galaxy!

### ionosphere
    - its an ionised layer high up in our atmosphere
    - degree of ionisation correlated to sunspot activity.
      Lots of sunspots, the ionosphere was more ionised. so
    - somthing was reaching out from the sun and stripping
      electrons off atoms in the upper layer of the
      atmosphere
    - but we'd need x-rays or gamma rays to ionise Nitrogen
      in our atmosphere. But going from a blackbody
      spectrum, you'd need something millions of degrees hot
      to produce x-rays...
    - anyway eventually they launched V2 rockets are
      observed the sun in the x-ray (using Geiger counters).
      And the sun was glowing in x-rays just _above_ the
      _sunspots_ or solar flares. (Actually the _main body_
      of the sun was black in x-rays)
    - but then people thought you couldnt see anything else
      in the x-ray regime because other stars are so far
      away, and even the sun which is close is weak in
      x-rays
    - but then people thought there might be another
      measureable source of x-rays: the _moon_! The solar
      wind could accelerate particles into the moon and
      liberate x-rays... (Natures version of the LHC)
    - few good electronics tricks to remove _cosmic ray_
      background from x-ray detectors
    - 1962, instead of the moon, the found another
      (nonvisible) source emitting x-rays! Scorpius X1
    - nice calculation (wiens law) to show that Scorpius X1
      must be 30,000,000 Kelvin. But thats incredibly hot,
      so the total power being emitted must be absolutely
      staggering! `Power = (Area) σ T^4`
    - well the area must be small then, otherwise if the
      source had a large area it'd be so bright it'd be
      visible all the time even in daylight!
    - using `Power = (Area) σ T^4` we work out the radius is
      6km! very small!
    - but would a neutron star be this hot? A neutron star
      is formed when a massive star, 10 or 20 times the mass
      of the sun runs out of nuclear fuel and collapses down
      and forms a neutron star. And the centre of the
      neutron star is literally billions of degrees K when
      its born.

### but problems!
    - problem: majority of x-ray sources _arent_ associated
      with supernovae remnants
    - also the source of x-rays should be a tiny point
      source. So wait until the moon crosses the crab
      nebula, and you should see an an abrupt drop in x-rays
      as the limb of the moon crosses the source. But
      (unfortunately) there was a more gentle drop in x-ray
      brightness.
    - now most of the xrays seem to be coming from a "windy"
      bit around the neutron star - so maybe shocks etc? But
      shocks would die out quickly.
    - but maybe the _magnetic field_ was dragged down into
      the neutron star as it was formed. And this magnetic
      field is spinning as the NS spins, which sends low
      frequency EM radiation out into the surrounding
      medium, and that excites electrons etc to emit
      x-rays... well its a hypothesis! 
    - Think of loads of rubber bands dropping from the
      celing, you grab them together and start spinning
      around, then the bands would spin fast in the opposite
      direction?
    - good calculation: how fast can somthing spin without
      spinning itself to pieces


### neutrons that *arent* embedded in supernova remnants
    - when the looked at SCO X1 it just seemed to be a normal
      star
    - but maybe not! maybe its part of a binary system and
      we just see the normal companion
    - anyway they sent up uhuru and these sources were seen
      to _pulse in the x-ray_. These pulses were slower than
      the radio pulses of a pulsar, and also not
      particularly _regular_
    - so looks like you have a normal star and x-rays that
      are moving towards and away from us
    - so really its a binary system with accreting matter
      from a donor falling onto an orbiting neutron star.
      Because the neutron star has such a high gravitational
      potential the emitted radiation is in the x-ray
      region...
