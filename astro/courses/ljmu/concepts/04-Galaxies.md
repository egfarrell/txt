### Lecture 01: Galaxy Types, Structure and Contents

    - large stellar systems

    - composed of light from various different sources but
      we're most familiar with the optical view which is
      dominated by starlight

    - theyre complicated! Blind astronomers could have
      predicted gravitationally bound fusion reactors
      (stars!) because we know the physics pretty well. But
      not even the most optimistic expert on galaxies would
      say that we'd have predicted objects such as galaxies
      if we hadnt seen them.

    - we're getting some idea why things form on these
      scales (10s of kiloparsecs), with bayronic masses of

### why are galaxies interesting?

    - well they are the _hosts_ for important processes:

        - star formation

        - super massive black holes

    - galaxies are tracers for the expanding universe

    - fundamental uncertainties remain after 100 years of
      study: we dont know what most of the material is that
      makes up galaxies eg: "dark matter"

    - unifying physics concept that joins together different
      parts of the galaxies course and also links to other
      areas in the Concepts module:

        - the dominant force at work (on larger scales) is
          gravitaiton (within galaxies or between galaxy
          clusters)!

        - interplay between gravitational collapse and
          various "pressure" forces e.g infall and outflow
          of gas is central to galaxy properties

        - interesting physics is seeing what can resist that
          gravitational infall to stabilise structures on
          different scales (feedback forces)

### Galaxy contents

    - galaxies contain stars! Thats the main reason we see
      them in the optical.

    - the stars come in categories: old stars (billions of
      years) in pretty much every galaxy, but some galaxies
      have enough gas resevoirs that theyre still forming
      stars

    - also gas in different phases:
        1. molecular gas (H2) (coldest component)
        2. atomic gas (HI)
        3. ionized gas (HII)

    - _molecular H2_: dense gas, can clump together into
      giant molecular clouds which can then result in star
      formation.

    - _atomic Hydrogen Gas HI_: distributed throughout the
      disk of the galaxy in a thin plane. (sometimes a hole
      in the central bulge though) Very extended, extends
      beyond the stellar extent of the galaxy, and is
      generally detected by its radio emission. (gives rise
      to 21cm radio emission).

    - Note: the 21cm emission is an important component for
      determing the masses and dynamics of galaxies

    - _ionised gas HII_: this is gas collected together in
      the spiral arms and has been ionised by young stars.
      The young stars are the most massive, energetic and
      luminous which produce UV photons which strip
      electrons from the Hydrogen atoms. (when the electrons
      and atoms recombine, you get spiked emissions which
      reveal the presence of this gas)

    - In addition: Elliptical galaxies, and central regions
      of clusters of galaxies, contain very hot X-ray
      emitting gas (10 million K)

### Astrophysical dust

    - dust is very important but somewhat problematic. It
      blocks our view of particular regions of the galaxy.

    - our galaxy is a spiral which is dusty. It blocks our
      view out of the galaxy too. Good thing our galaxy
      doesnt have 10 times the amount of gas/dust or
      extra-galactic astronomy would be curtailed!

    - dust alters the appearance of galaxies as we view
      them.

    - you can get dust lanes that change the whole
      appearance of galaxies

    - the dust of a galaxy can also block _background_
      galaxies

    - also get dust in the spiral arms, where you get shocks
      of gas running through the arm and it concentrates the
      dust

    - think of dust as being the size of _smoke_ particles,
      not the dust in your living room! Smoke particles
      appear blue because they preferentially scatter _small
      wavelengths_ of light, and thats the same phenomenon
      for inter-stellar dust. So think of it as a "_smoke
      fog_" thats filling inter-galactic space..

    - dont confuse dust with dark matter! Dust only
      contributes < 1% of the mass of a galaxy!

### dark matter

    - look at the velocity of outlying material (eg. HI gas)
      in a (spiral) galaxy and the outlying stuff is still
      revolving at very high speeds. In order for the galaxy
      to be a gravitationally bound system against
      centrifugal forces, then these rotational speeds
      require the whole galaxy to be embedded in a dark
      matter halo.

### Virial Theorem

    - very important in terms of galaxy formation and
      evolution

    - twice the kinetic energy + the gravitational potential
      energy sum to zero `2 <K> + <U> = 0`

    - note: `< ... >` represent time averaged quantities. At
      any given instant there may be more or less
      kinetic/potential energy as indicated in this equation
      but over a long enough time-scale the averages hold.

    - note: the kinetic energy is always positive and the
      binding energy is always negative

    - note: the total energy of a gravitationally bound
      system is _negative_. The kinetic energy is only 1/2
      of the binding energy.

### consequences of the virial theorem

    - if you want to make a gravitationally bound system
      from an initially unbound system, there must be some
      way of _dissipating_ energy...

    - bound systems in virial equilibrium have _negative
      heat_ capacities. Yes! Why? If you:

        - put energy in, then you get an equilibrium state
          with _lower_ velocities.

        - if you take energy out, it results in _higher_
          velocities (taken eneryg out, but end up with
          a tighter bound system, the orbiting particle is
          lower in the potential well and is moving faster
          in equilibrium)

        - example: think of a satellite in high earth orbit

### spiral winding angle

    - not very important but links to galaxy classifications

    - fundamental part of Hubble tuning fork diagram, is
      different of spiral galaxies have different
      morphologies of spiral arms

    - bulge dominated galaxies tend to have smoother and
      more tightly-wound spiral arms

    - _Sa galaxy_: large central bulge, symmetric spiral,
      smooth spirals, tightly wound. (winding angle 9',
      estimate)

    - _Sc galaxy_: smaller bulge than Sa galaxy. Spiral arms
      broken up into blueish white clumps. (loosely wound)
      (winding angle 17' - estimate)

    - draw a circle centred on the nucleus of the galaxy,
      and the winding angle is the angle between the
      circular arc and the spiral arm.

    - if spiral angle zero, then the spiral arm is a circle
      and would wind back on itself.

    - Normal range of angles found: 5 - 30 degrees

    - _note_: the winding angle stays constant as a function
      of radius

### spiral winding problem!

    - theres a problem if we assume the spiral arms are
      coupled directly to the underlying material (stars and
      gas) of a galactic disk

    - but thats a problem because of flat rotation curves!

    - ideal pattern with constant winding angle: logarithmic
      spiral (remember guy in Australia!)

    - Lecturer simulation: takes less time for particles
      near the centre of the galaxy to orbit compared to
      those further out. Because of flat rotation curve, the
      have the same linear velocity of particles further
      out, so theyre going to complete one orbit in shorter
      time. So after 600 Myr, the spiral pattern is
      effectively destroyed. The arms are so tightly wound,
      they just merge together, and you have an almost
      uniform disk. (differential rotation)

    - simulation: the very centre has a linearly rising
      rotation curve. We call that a solid body rotation.
      Its just whats needed to keep everything rotating
      around with the same period, and the spiral pattern is
      preserved.

    - Problem! This is just the opposite of what we see. In
      real galaxies the central region is the spiral arm
      free bulge region, and the spiral arms would star
      further out.

    - so this model where we tie the spiral pattern, to the
      rotation of the galaxy does a very poor job of
      maintaining the structures we see in real galaxies.

### solution: spiral winding problem

    - the spiral arm isnt a physical entity, the arm isnt
      composed of a given set of stars and gas cloud.
      Instead its a density wave structure.

    - eg an ocean wave moves through water, but the
      individual water molecules that make up the wave just
      get perturbed into a circular path by the wave and
      then it moves on. (or more direct analogy: sound
      waves: energy passed on as a wave of compression)

    - so its the wave of compression that moves through the
      disk

    - the wave "surfs" across the matter that makes up the
      disk and decouples it from the underlying matter...
      (not completely independent of the matter though)

### spiral arms trigger star formation

    - check notes/formulas

    - gravity tries to compress the arms of the disk ->
      clump -> stars formed

    - resisting that gravity is velocity dispersion,
      pressure forces and rotational forces

    - on small scales, a galactic disk is supported by the
      random velocity of stars which is like the pressure of
      a gas due to the thermal motions of molecules

    - jeans length: the minimum size needed for a disk
      region to collapse under self-gravity (for a given
      density). Jeans originally did his equations for
      a spherical region but in the notes they've adapted it
      for a disk geometry

      maximum radius supported (by internal velocities) is:
      `Rj,d = π/8  ∙ σ^2 / G μ`

      - `σ` = velocity dispersion (pressure force)

      - `μ` = surface density of the material (the higher
        this is, the smaller the region that can be
        supported)

    - surface density - disc geometry (not
      sphere)

    - effects of rotation -> oort constant B (total angular
      momentum per unit mass), minimum
      rotaiton size

    - _Toomre stability_ criterion
        - (rotational scale size < jeans length)
        - critical velocity dispersion:
        - `σ > sqrt( 16/3 G μ/B )`

    - simple equation that does remarkably good job of
      predicting which galaxies are undergoing star
      formation and which arent

    - spiral density waves: they locally increase `μ` (the
      surface density)

### Galaxy luminosity function

    - astro-physicists doing a _stock-take_ of whats out
      there in the extra-galactic universe

    - mathematical the 'Schecter function' is: the _number
      density_ of galaxies as a function of total luminosity

    - note: has a 'faint end' and a 'bright end'

    - originally it was an observational concept - people
      doing photographic/CCD _surveys_ of different bits of
      the sky and adding up the number of galaxies of
      different types. 

    - But today its used more in computational _simulations_
      of the universe. Its a key constraint on their models.
      They have to produce the right numbers and right range
      of different types of galaxies.

    - mathematically: `Φ(L)` is 
        - the # of galaxies, 
        - per unit volume of the universe, 
        - with luminosities in some range between  `L and (L
          + δL)`

    - As an empirical result, its nicely fitted by:

        - `Φ(L) dL = N_0 (L/L*)^α exp(-L/L*) dL/L_0`

    - free parameters:

        - constant `N_0` (for normalisation). Tells you
          overall how many galaxies per cubic mega-parsec
          there are in the universe

        - constant `L*` . Characteristic luminosity

        - power-law part: `(L/L_0)^α`

        - cut-off term: comes in as an exponential:
          `exp(-L/L_0) dL/L_0`

            - (cuts off the bright end of the luminosity
              function)

    - its a declining function as you go from faint galaxies
      to bright galaxies. (Rule of nature: little things
      more common than big things. eg. more ants than
      wilderbeast!)

    - dwarf galaxies dominate at the faint end. (the
      straight line is for dwarf galaxies and defined by the
      `α` term in the power law)

    - 'knee': at some point it gets cut-off by the
      exponential term at the characteristic luminosity
      (`L*`). Note: the 'knee' and `L*` are somehow
      synonymous with one another. 

    - Overall: dwarves dominate the faint end, spirals
      dominate around the 'knee' or `L*`, and all the
      brightest galaxies we find in the local universe are
      the bright ellipticals and they dominate at the bright
      end (eg M87)

    - `α` is around 1.0 - 1.5. Important that its below 2.

    - but how do we work out a luminosity function? Well if
      we have a well defined cluster of galaxies, you can
      assume everything in the survey field (image) are in
      the same galaxy cluster. Measure all the ....
      ...
      ...

    - more problematic: field galaxy luminosity function
      from a general field survey (Hubble deep field).
      Galaxies here can be at any red-shift from z=0.1 up to
      z=6! ie the telescope isnt trained on one particular
      cluster. So you need to know the distance to each
      individual object so you know how to put it into your
      luminosity function.

    - There is a neat trick though, if you cant get around
      to measure the individual red-shifts.

    - Rather than sampling everything, just sample down to
      a certain apparent magnitude. Measure all the
      red-shifts for this reduced sample. Then the geometry
      (1/r^2?) gives you a geometric correction you can
      apply to the raw luminiosty function. (see notes for
      more detail) 

    - For more luminous galaxies in your magnitude limited
      sample, we'll have sampled a much larger volume of the
      universe (they can be much further away and still come
      within your magnitude limit) Also: we'll have
      undersampled the faint galaxies because they have to
      be much closer to us to be included in the survey.

### high-red shift galaxies (Lyman Break)

    - leader: Charles Steidel

    - short wavelength (UV) photons easily absorbed by
      Hydrogen gas (short mean free path). So if we look at
      a galaxy at very high red-shift, almost all of the UV
      photons, will only go a very short distance from the
      galaxy before they are blocked, by some cloud of
      Hydrogen. (within the galaxy itself, or just ouside by
      a cloud)

    - so you get a characteristic spectrum with a complete
      cutoff above the ionisation energy (912 A)

    - one point: we dont do full grade spectroscopy, but we
      can use use broad-band filters for this. Then we're
      looking for something you see in the Red filter but is
      completely missing in the blue(r) filter. Its called
      the "dropout effect"

    - take a sequence of images in different filters, and
      depends where a galaxy drops out, you can then
      calculate its red-shift
