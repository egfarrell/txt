This was the solution I initially went for but I'm now not sure this is right. The question asks for a distance measurement based on a colour-magnitude relation. The T-F procedure as set out in notes uses a magnitude-velocity relation. So I don't think it's as simple as that.

I think colour has to be measured by subtracting magnitude in one band from magnitude in another, e.g. B-K. If you look at the slope of the B and K lines on the diagram on page 29, you can see that they diverge, so there will be a linear relationship between B-K and magnitude. So I think you could use the B-K measure (which is distance-independent) to determine absolute magnitude, then compare that with apparent magnitude to get the distance measure.

Hi John,I am not sure, but I deduced the  correct values by capitalizing on the given hints along with a number of assumptions as follow:

observing the following:

1-the question provide us with FWHM for the two recombination lines (He and H) along with that of the instrumental line of the receiver.

2-In the hints they say that the distributions of the velocities and the responce function are Guassians.

3-In the hints they also talked about the fact that the convolution of two Guassians is again a Guassian distribution.

4-In the hint, there is also a mention about the fact that the variance of the convolution of two guassians is the sum of their variances.


And assuming that:

1-their are no redundant data or hints.

2-the standard deviation relates to the FWHM by the equation:

FWHM= √(8 ln(2)) σ

3-the obvious solution to all of this is to assume that velocity distribution of the constituents of the gas is the convolution of the two recombination lines (He and H) and the responce function of the receiver.


Now based on the above observations and assumptions and since the velocity dispersion is defined to be the "standard deviation" of the velocity distribution we could get it by calculating σ for each distribution and summing (after squaring the results) to get the variance of the convolution, so by taking the square root of the latter we get the "velocity dispersion".


Finally, since the kinetic temperature relates to the velocity dispersion by the equation:

T=σ^2 M/k

By substitution we get the kinetic temperature.
I used this argument and got the correct values (since it matches with the value taken from table 15.1). Maybe it is a coincidence maybe it is correct!!. However, all of us in the process of learning.
