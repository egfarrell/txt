
### What is power? 
    - It is the `square of your signal`(what exactly is that), integrated through one
      period, divided by the period length. 
    - For example, a sine wave of amplitude 1 has a power equal
      to 0.5. 
    - If the units of the signal were volts, the units of power
      in this sense is volts squared. 
    - If you had multiple frequencies present in your signal,
      this time domain definition would result in a sum of their
      respective powers.

### What is a power SPECTRUM? 
    - How the power is broken down into frequency components. 
    - If I have two sine waves at different frequencies but both
      with amplitude `1`, I would see a `0.5` value at each
      frequency location in the power spectrum. 
    - If the power spectrum was taken for a signal with units of
      volts, the units of the power spectrum would be volts
      squared. 
    - If I summed together a power spectrum for all frequencies,
      I'd get exactly the same value as I would with the time
      domain definition of power mentioned earlier. 
        - This is _Parseval's theorem_.

### - What is power spectral density? 
    - A curve that when you take the integral over from frequency
      `a` to `b`, gives you the power in that frequency band. 
    - That's really the only difference; you get units of power
      from a spectral density by taking the area underneath
      a curve where as you get power from a power spectrum by
      summing the heights of the curve. If the signal's units
      were in volts, the units of the signal’s power spectral
      density would be in V^2/Hz. The power spectrum and spectral
      density curves are interchangeable, however in practice
      (i.e. with digitally recorded broadband signals) the power
      spectrum is sensitive to your choice of frequency
      resolution, where as the spectral density is not. As you
      increase the frequency resolution (i.e. decrease dF) the
      power spectrum will collapse to zero for broadband signals,
      whereas the spectral density will remain the same height
      except be smoother or nosier. It avoids this effect by
      storing the power information in the area under the curve
      instead of the curve height. This is analogous to why
      density plots are used when you discretize a continuous
      probability distribution. Think about what happens if you
      take a histogram of a uniform distribution and gradually
      decrease the binwidth of the histogram. Eventually it will
      flatten to zero. The value of any given point in a spectral
      density in this sense is meaningless. It is the area under
      the curve between two points that provides the important
      information about power. The density plots are still useful
      to understand the relative differences in power between
      different frequencies, and is what is more commonly
      reported.
