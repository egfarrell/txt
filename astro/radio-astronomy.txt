### HN: high resolution
    -  Astronomical radio images are naturally overwhelmingly
       sharper than optical images, because radio telescopes
       today can have much larger apertures than optical
       telescopes.
    - The best-resolution optical images are made with two large
      telescopes and a temperature-controlled optical fiber from
      each one, arranged to interfere optically, yielding an
      image resolution equivalent to a mirror the size of the
      distance between the telescopes as the visual field is
      scanned on each simultaneously to sample pixels of the
      image, in sequence.
    - The electromagnetic interference operation must be
      conducted purely optically because we are not equipped to
      sample and record time-registered electromagnetic signals
      at 1 trillion samples per second. (Yet. It is possible in
      principle, it just hasn't been achieved.)
    - In contrast, output from radio telescopes can be sampled
      and recorded with careful time registration, and the
      samples interfered numerically, so signals from radio
      telescopes thousands of miles apart can be treated
      together, yielding images of overwhelmingly finer detail.
      Even when using just a single radio telescope, a reflector
      dish can be made much larger than any practical earthbound
      optical mirror.
    - So, if a radio-telescope image is indistinct, it is likely
      that the object imaged is itself indistinct.
    - The recent image of a black-hole accretion disc was
      composed from radio-telescope traces. No optical treatment
      could produce an image of such resolution.

### Sara Conference talk 2020
	- $MY_IMAGES/astro/Radio/SARA-talk-2020.jpg
    - Radio Astronomy signals are much weaker than expected.
        - At 1420 Mz the signals are weak even on large antennas. 
        - On a 10ft dish only the Sun is a few dB.
        - Most other signals like CasA, the Moon, Cygnus will be
          fractins of a dB.
        - It's all about _temperature_. You'll get tired of
          hearing Kelvin used in so many different ways in radio
          astronomy. 
        - Kelvin can be used in noise figure, the signal change
          and just physical temperature too.
        - Basically we're measuring energy changes. Everything in
          radio astronomy is about relating them to the RF energy
          created by a resistor heated to some degree kelvin.
        - Useful number: Room temperature (290K) this change is
          -174 dBm per Hz.
            - This allows you do some calculations in your head. 
        - Noise figure is the noise added by the receivers
          electronics. This is measured in equivalent Kelvin.

    - Gain drift in the electronics looks just like the signals
      we're looking for. "Often you'll think you have something
      and it's just a change in temperature in the electronics" 
        - Laptops, WIFI, smartphones, GPS adds spurious noise. 

    - Calibrate noise sources. Periodically inject noise into the
      system. You can calibrate your system based on the amount
      of change you see.
        - Problem: getting water in the co-ax. Periodic
          calibration will show this up (ie that your system has
          changed sensitivity).
    - Dead parts thermal noise looks disturbingly like working
      system noise.
    - Take good notes. The prefessionals have FITS headers on
      their data with lots of info about the observing
      conditions. 
    - Having a filter to protect an "LNA?" is very
      important.
    - Learn basic astronomy: get phone apps which identify the
      sky objects. Watch for Daylight Savings time! Get
      comfortable with sideral time, universal time etc.
    - Due south is key. Gives the ability to see the lowest in
      declination. 
        - The galactic centre is a bright radio source. But at
          high latitudes you may see _tree thermal nosie_ or
          ground noise first, making it more difficult. 
    - If you're not focussed too well the side lobes of the dish
      will get larger and instead of being 18 or 20 dB down, they
      may be higher and that will see thermal noise as you get to
      low angles.
    - Precision time is vital to making sense of data and sharing
      results.
    - GPS time is good if not internet access, other use internet
      time servers to keep your loggining computer accurate.
    - Take good notes: even the wind can cause the antenna to
      move and that'll show up in the measurements.

### Know too much!
    - In 1933 John Kraus (University of Michigan), attempted to
      detect the sun by using a searchlight reflector to focus
      the radio waves. He failed because the receiver was not
      sensitive enough. 
    - This was the first use of a reflector-type radio telescope.
      At the Serendipity meeting, Kraus stated that meaningful
      accidental discovery occurs only as the result of "being in
      the right place with the right equipment doing the right
      experiment at the right time.” 
    - Another noted astronomer, R. Hanbury Brown, added that the
      person should "not know too much,” otherwise the discovery
      might not be made!


### Phased Arrays (PHD 2010)
    - Phased array technology is new to the radio astronomical
      community. A phased array radio telescope consists of
      a large number of small antennas instead of a small number
      of large dishes.
    - Each of these antennas has a very large field-of-view
      (FOV), which means that they receive signals from all over
      the sky simultaneously.
    - The array is focused on the signal of interest by com-
      bining the signals from many antennas after correction for
      the geometrical delay over the array. This is schematically
      compared to focusing of an incoming wave by a dish in Fig.
      1.3.

### Polarisation
    - Radio astronomers (and electrical engineers) define
      circular polarization from the point of view of the light
      _EMITTER_.
    - In this perspective, if the electric field is rotating
      clockwise it is right circularly polarized and if rotating
      counter-clockwise it is left circularly polarized.
    - Most physics textbooks adopt the _OBSERVER_ perspective of
      the light. Right circular polarization still corresponds to
      clockwise rotation, but since this is seen from the
      opposite point-of-view, it corresponds to the radio
      astronomer’s counter-clockwise rotation or left circular
      polarization, and vice versa.
    - Be careful when reading about circularly polarized light to
      be certain you know which convention is being used.

### misc info
    - radio signals are often 1% of the system noise. (Everything
      is giving off radio waves via blackbody thermal emission)
    - radio signal measurements often given in units of
      temperature (antenna temperature)
    - The visible and radio skies reveal distinct "parallel
      universes” sharing the same space. 
    - Most optically bright stars are undetectable at radio
      wavelengths, and many strong radio sources are optically
      faint or invisible.
