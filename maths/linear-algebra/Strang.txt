    - What idea do you find most beautiful?
        - So we have a matrix, a rectangle of numbers, might even
          be a rectangle of data. Eg. every column corresponds to
          a drug, and every row a patient, and if the patient
          reacted favourably to the drug you put some positive
          number in there...
        - So the big problem is to understand all those numbers,
          what are the patterns? What's going on?
        - And one way is to break down that matrix into simple
          pieces using something called 'singular values'. That's
          come on as fundamental in my lifetime.
        - We had 'eigenvalues' before but they were restricted to
          square matrices, and data comes in rectangular
          matrices.
        - So 'singular values' are a way to find the important
          pieces of a matrix, which add up to the whole matrix.
          So you break a matrix into simple pieces: the 1st piece
          is the most important part of the data, the 2nd piece
          is the 2nd most important part etc. So if a data
          scientist can find those first and second peices stop
          there! The rest of the data is probably round off, or
          experimental error maybe...
        - So the beautiful bit: every (square, rectangular)
          matrix can be written as the product of 3 special very
          simple matrices.
            - `M = U ∑ V*`
            - `M = rotation * stretch (diagonal) * rotation`
            - $MY_IMAGES/maths/singular-value-decomposition.jpg

    - https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=23150699
    - I like this outline. It's very MIT-ish in the sense of
      teaching someone to solve practical engineering problems
      with matrices.
    - But I foresee some difficulties: I often ask undergrads who
      had linear algebra:
        - "what is the determinant?"
        - and seldom get the "best" conceptual answer: ```
        - "the determinant is the product of the eigenvalues."
          ```
    - The best answer should ideally be the most popular.
    - We'd consider it a failure if the most popular explanation
      of the fundamental theorem of calculus was not some
      variation of "integrals undo derivatives and vice versa".
    - I don't see Strangs approach solving that.
    - Also there's a lot of focus on _CR decomposition_ which
      says a linear transform from `R^m` to `R^n` might map to
      a subspace of `R^n` with smaller dimension `r < min(m,n)`
      and while it's true in some sense, it's quite "unphysical":
        - if a matrix contains noisy entries then it will
          generally only be degenerate in this way with
          probability zero.
        - You need perfect noise cancelation to get degeneracies,
          which amounts to a sort of neglected underlying
          CONSERVED QUANTITY which is pushing back on you and
          demanding to be conserved.
        - In that sense the `CR decomposition` is kind of
          pointless and is just working around some "perfect
          little counterexamples".
        - So it seems weird to present this as the most important
          thing!

    > seldom get the "best" conceptual answer, "the determinant
    > is the product of the eigenvalues."
        - I found the "best conceptual" answer depends a lot on
          taste, and which concepts you are familiar with.
        - In this case:
            - Calculating exact eigenvalues of matrices larger
              than `4 x 4` is impractical, since it requires you
              to solve a polynomial of degree `> 4`.
            - The `EV` exist only in algebraically closed fields
              (complex numbers), while the determinant itself
              lives in the base field (rationals, reals).
        - How about:
            - GEOMETRIC DETERMINANT
                - The determinant is the volume of the polytope
                  (parallel-epiped) spanned by the column vectors
                  of the matrix.
            - COORDINATE FREE DETERMINANT
                - The determinant is the map induced between the
                  highest exterior powers of the source and
                  target vector spaces
                  (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exterior_algebra)
                    - thats funny, I just came accross this
                      definition already today (Nov 2020)
            - REPRESENTATION THEORETIC VERSION
                - This version characterizes the determinant as
                  invariant under the Symmetric group acting by
                  permutation on the columns/rows of the matrix.


### Strang: Linear Algebra in a Nutshell

- (A is `n` by `n`)

- NONSINGULAR                                  SINGULAR
- -----------                                  --------
- `A` is invertible                           `A` is not invertible
- The columns are independent                 The columns are dependent
- The rows are independent                    The rows are dependent
- The determinant is not zero                 The determinant is zero
- Ax = 0 has one solution x = 0               Ax = 0 has infinitely many solutions
- Ax = b has one solution x = A-1b            Ax = b has no solution or infinitely many
- A has `n` (nonzero) pivots                  A has `r < n` pivots
- A has full rank `r = n`                     A has rank `r < n`
- The reduced row echelon form is `R=I`       R has at least one zero row
- The column space is all of `R^n`            The column space has dimension `r < n`
- The row space is all of `R^n`               The row space has dimension `r < n`
- All eigenvalues are nonzero                 Zero is an eigenvalue of A
- `A^T`A is symmetric positive definite       ATA is only semidefinite
- `A` has `n` (positive) singular values      A has `r < n` singular values
