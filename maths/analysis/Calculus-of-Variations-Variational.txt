

### A Variation: Why symbol `δ`?
    - $MY_NOTES/maths/analysis/Variations-Extremal-Paths.txt
    - There is no deep mathematical necessity — it's mostly
      historical convention, originating with Euler and Lagrange.
    - They wanted:
        - a symbol distinct from d, to avoid confusion
        - something suggestive of “difference’’ or “deviation’’
        - something easy to write alongside integrals
    - `δ` was a natural choice because `Δ` (capital delta)
      already meant a finite difference, and `δ` intuitively
      evokes an infinitesimal difference but of a DIFFERENT KIND
      from `dx`.
    - So the hierarchy became:
        - `Δx` = finite difference
        - `δx` = infinitesimal variation of a function
        - `dx` = infinitesimal differential of a variable


### The wrong path (interesting)
    - we’re interested in `functionals` that take in some
      function – usually a single-variable function, such as
      a curve `y(x)` – and return a _NUMBER_. 
    - But how do we express such a thing mathematically?

    - Well, maybe your first guess might be like:
        - F(y) = ( y(x) )^2

    - This would indeed be a “function of a function” – it takes
      in an entire function `y(x)` as its input.
    - But this does NOT return a number, instead it returns
      another FUNCTION of the variable `x`. 
        - For example, if you were to plug in `y(x)=x²`, you’d
          get `F(x)=x^4`. So, this is not a valid functional.
        - (so it's actually an "operator"! See below.)

    - Generally, a valid functional that takes in a full function
      and only returns a single number can be got by writing the
      functional as a `DEFINITE INTEGRAL`.


### Calculus of Variations used for:
    - Finding paths of shortest distance (geodesics).
    - Finding surfaces of minimal surface area.
    - Describing the motion of objects under gravity in GR.
    - Deriving equations of motion for systems in Lagrangian mechanics.
    - Modeling the dynamics of fields in field theories.
    - In optics: Describing the motion of light rays in materials.

### Feynmann lectures
    - “But watch out. Ordinarily we just have a function of some
      variable, and we have to find out which value of that
      variable makes the function least or most. 
    - For instance, we have a rod which has been heated in the
      middle and the heat is spread around. 
        - For each point on the rod we have a temperature, and we
          must find the point at which the temperature is
          largest. 
    - But now, for each `path` in space we have a number and we
      have to find the `path` in space for which the number is
      the minimum. 
    - That is a completely different branch of mathematics. It's
      not the ordinary calculus. In fact, it is called the
      calculus of variations.

        - Loosely, an operator (acting on a function space) 
            - takes functions to _functions_ (eg, `f(x)` to
              `−if′(x)`). 
        - On the other hand, a functional

      Whenever you differentiate a function, you actually apply
      the differential operator to a _function_ to obtain another
      function, namely its derivative. 
    - So differentiation is in fact an `operator` that maps
      functions to their derivatives (other functions).

### Gelfand: Calculus of Variations
    - Gelfand and Fomin wrote a wonderful, clear and concise
      intro to the calculus of variations.
    - It requires little more than a calculus and analysis
      background (say, 1st or 2nd year undergraduate)
    - Also, the end-of-chapter problems are straightforward, and
      often follow in-chapter examples, although the resulting
      algebra can be beastly.
    - A word of advice: keep in mind that this book is an older
      text and lacks some modern context. e.g.
        - the variational derivative of a functional is just the
          `Frechet derivative` applied to the infinite
          dimensional vector space of admissible variations.
        - They use a `norm` on a Sobolev space without defining
          it as such.
        - They make no mention of the `Hamiltonian` as the convex
          conjugate functional of the Lagrangian.
    - If you're just looking to solve variational problems, you
      might be fine with this.
    - On the other hand, if you're looking for more general
      insight, I'd recommend first learning some basic functional
      analysis (e.g. Kreyszig, Luenberger) and then match the
      concepts from this book to modern jargon.

### Applied Calculus of Variations for Engineers 2e
    - This is one engineers can read comfortably. 
        - (No rigorous proof)
    - Basically, it's a mixture of:
        - Weinstock: Calculus of Variations: with Applications...
        - Brunt:     Calculus of Variations (Universitext)

    - The book is excellent:
        1. Excellent organization and comprehensive contents.
        Systematic separation of topics. Good for computation.
        2. Contents are clear and easy-to-follow. Each step says
        what method is used to obtain the next equation.
        3. Progresses from specific cases to generalized cases,
        and no attempt made to establish one big generalized eqn
        (which is the goal of most pure math textbook).

    - Minor problems:
        1. It has some subtle typos that obscure clarity. Eg,
           Chapter 1 "by using the mean value theorem", should be
           "by Taylor series expansion".

        2. The mathematical notations are not precise. The
           variation starts with comparing two functionals
                - `I(y)` and
                - `I(y + epsilon*eta)`.
            - Throughout the book, `I(y + epsilon*eta)` is
              written as `I(epsilon)`, which is understandable
              but not precise.
