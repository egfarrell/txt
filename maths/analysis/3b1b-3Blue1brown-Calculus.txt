### oh key idea: 'area under a graph'
    - Notice all the little areas of all the little rectangles,
      ... as the 'widths' of these little rectangles get smaller
      and smaller...
        - you end up working out the `AREA under a graph` rather
          than the SUM of millions of little rectangles...
    - ... or said another way: 
        - `integration` has a similarity to computing the area
          under a graph
        - $MY_IMAGES/maths/integration-and-area-under-graph.jpg
        - $MY_IMAGES/maths/integration-and-area-under-graph-2.jpg

### But a question!
    - in the area of a circle problem above, there was an easy
      formula to get the area under the graph... it was just the
      area of a triangle
    - But what about the area under a parabola??? 
        - No handy formula there?

            - Interesing: apparent paradox: 
                - the velocity function `v(t)` takes a SINGLE
                  point in time `t`, but to calculate the
                  velocity we need TWO POINTS in time!
                - Although we work out the rise/run ratio for two
                  NEARBY POINTS IN TIME on the graph (in the real
                  world), in the Platonic Maths world we let the
                  distance between the two nearby points approach
                  zero (`dt -> 0`), and we get a tangent line to
                  the graph. And the SLOPE of this tangent line
                  is the pure math derivative
                    - $MY_IMAGES/maths/2brown-calculus/pure-math-derivative-01.jpg
                    - $MY_IMAGES/maths/2brown-calculus/pure-math-derivative-02.jpg
            - Even though change in an INSTANT makes no sense,
              this idea of letting `dt` APPROACH ZERO (but still
              remain finite) is kind of a "sneaky backdoor way"
              to talk about the rate of change at a single point
              in time. 
                - It's flirting with the paradox of change in an
                  instant without ever actually touching it.
            - Because "change in an instant" makes no sense, it's
              better to think of the derivative as the best
              CONSTANT APPROXIMATION of a rate-of-change around
              a point...

### Part-03
    - $MY_VIDEOS/maths/3blue1brown/calculus/003-Derivative-formulas-through-geometry.mp4
    - Why do students spend so much time grappling with
      derivatives of abstract functions... rather than
      concentrate rate-of-change problems?
        - Because real world problems are _modelled_ with
          polynomials, trig functions, exp functions etc
    - Derivatives:
        - how a tiny change in one quantity can result in a tiny
          change in another quantity
        - tiny nudges are at the heart of derivatives
    - Note: it's often better to view the _GEOMETRIC_ (2D or 3D)
      version of the function, when trying to work out the
      PRECISE FORMULA for a derivative, rather than the _graph_
      of the function
        - Gives more intuition for the formula that way ...
            - $MY_IMAGES/maths/2brown-calculus/derivatives-thru-geometry-0.jpg
            - $MY_IMAGES/maths/2brown-calculus/derivatives-thru-geometry-1.jpg
            - $MY_IMAGES/maths/2brown-calculus/derivatives-thru-geometry-2.jpg
            - $MY_IMAGES/maths/2brown-calculus/derivatives-thru-geometry-3.jpg
            - $MY_IMAGES/maths/2brown-calculus/derivatives-thru-geometry-4.jpg
            - $MY_IMAGES/maths/2brown-calculus/derivatives-thru-geometry-5.jpg
        - note: when MANUALLY working out the derivative of
          a function, you can ignore any differential higher than
          order 1... 
            - so ignore any bits that are dx^2, dx^3...
            - later: ah it's because anything like dx^2, dx^3 etc
              will be divided by dx later, and then `dx -> 0` so
              they'll all disappear anyway
            - $MY_IMAGES/maths/2brown-calculus/ignore-higher-order-differentials.jpg
    - nice way to think of the graph of `1/x`
        - It's whatever the HEIGHT of a rectangle of width `x`
          has to be, to keep its area = 1
            - $MY_IMAGES/maths/2brown-calculus/geometry-plus-graph-1-over-x.jpg
        - And if you nudge the width of the rectangle by `dx`,
          what should the height `1/x` lose? 
            - In order to keep the overall area = 1?
            - $MY_IMAGES/maths/2brown-calculus/geometry-plus-graph-1-over-x-2.jpg

    - Why the derivative of `sin(θ)` is  `cos(θ)`?
        - intuition 1:
            - look at the graph of `sin(θ)` and see if the
              rate-of-change (slope) is rising, or falling, or
              zero at each point. As you plot this slope, you'll
              end up plotting the graph of `cos(θ)`
                - $MY_IMAGES/maths/2brown-calculus/derivative-of-sine-1.jpg
        - intuition 2:
            - More precise, look at the unit circle itself. How
              much does the "height" of `sin(θ)` change, when we
              nudge the angle `θ` by a little bit, `dθ`
                - note: when we nudge the angle, it's like we
                  walk ahead a bit more around the outside rim of
                  the circle, because that's what radian angles
                  really mean
                    - $MY_IMAGES/maths/2brown-calculus/derivative-of-sine-2.jpg
                - a year later (Sept 2022): oh I got it now, with
                  the little triangle underneath the circle
                    - $MY_IMAGES/maths/2brown-calculus/oh-I-got-the-derivative-of-sin.jpg

### Part-05
    - $MY_VIDEOS/maths/3blue1brown/calculus/005-Whats-so-special-about-Eulers-number-e.mp4
    - derivative of `2^t `.... is `2^t * (0.69...)` 
        - ie `itself * constant`
    - All exponential functions are proportional to their own
      derivative, but `e` is the special function where the
      constant of proportionality is `1`
