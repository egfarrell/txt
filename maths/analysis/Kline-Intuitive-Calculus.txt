### Origins of Calculus
    - Summary: 4 classes of problems:
        1.  Celestial motions
        2.  Tangents to curves
        3.  Maxima and Minima
        4.  Lengths of curves, bounded areas

    - The `motion` of celestial bodies became important in the
      17th century. 
        - Objects moving near the surface of the Earth and also
          heavenly bodies, have _variable velocity_ and also
          variable acceleration. The branches of Maths before
          Calculus were not able to treat these.
    - The 2nd problem of the 17th century was the determination
      of `tangents` to various curves (Fig. 1-1).
        - $MY_IMAGES/maths/analysis/Kline/tangents.jpg 
        - This question was interesting as pure geometry, but
          also was significance because the tangent to a curve at
          a point represents the _direction_ of the curve at that
          point. 
        - Thus, if a projectile moves along a curve, the
          direction in which the projectile is headed at any
          point on its path is the direction of the tangent at
          that point. 
        - To determine if the projectile will strike its target
          head-on or just at a glancing angle, we must know in
          which direction the projectile is moving at the point
          on its path where it strikes the target. 
        - The invention of the telescope and microscope in the
          17th century stimulated great interest in `lenses`. To
          determine the trajectory of light rays after striking
          the lens surface, we must know the angle the light ray
          makes with the lens; 
            - ie the angle (Fig. 1-2) between the light ray and
              the tangent to the lens
            - $MY_IMAGES/maths/analysis/Kline/tangents.jpg
        - Thus finding the _tangent_ to a curve was a major
          topic. (_TODO_: get a screenshot of fig 1 and 2)
    - Another 17th century problem: `maxima and minima`. 
        - Eg what elevation angle maximises the range of
          a cannon. 
        - What are the max and min distances from the sun as
          a planet orbits it?
        - Again Calculus was needed for these...
    - They also studied the `lengths of curves` and the area
      bounded by a curve and the volume bounded by a surface.
        - It was possible to calculate this for simple shapes
          (bounded by line segments) but more complicated shapes
          were impossible. e.g. the Earth is an oblate spheroid,
          hard to calculate volume...

### function notation: double meaning
    - Strictly speaking, a function is a _relation_ between two
      variables, `x` and `y` say. 
    - The relation might be that `y` is the square of `x`. 
    - Therefore relation is neither `x` nor `y` but something
      connecting the two. 
    - Thus when one says that John is the _father_ of William,
      the relationship is fatherhood, and this relation is
      neither John nor William but a connection between them. 
    - Yet in speaking about these people one might use the word
      'father' to mean John, _whereas the word strictly refers to
      the relationship_. 
    - Likewise in dealing with functions we should distinguish
      between the relation and the value `y` of the function. 
    - We could use `f` to denote the relation between `y` and `x`
      and use `f(x)` to mean `y`. 
    - However, we often use `y` or `f(x)` to mean the RELATION.
    - Fortunately, this use of `y` or `f(x)` in the double sense
      causes no confusion and avoids a lot of unnecessary words.

### Limits!
    - An average speed is simply the distance traveled over an
      interval of time divided by that interval of time.
        - avg speed = distance / time
    - However the _instantaneous_ speed is not obtained by
      dividing a distance by a time interval. 
    - key: it is obtained by observing the number that the
      average speeds approach (`96` in this example)
    - In mathematical language, we say `96` is the _limit_ of the
      average speeds, as the time intervals over which the
      average speeds are computed approach `0`.
    - Hence the instantaneous speed is obtained by a totally new
      process: by finding the limit of average speeds.
    - By this process, we avoid the meaningless expression `0/0`
      which we'd have to face if we tried to compute the
      instantaneous speed as distance divided by time.
