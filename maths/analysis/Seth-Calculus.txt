### Infinitesimal: Not REAL number 
    - An infinitesimal is an infinitely small number, smaller
      than any positive real number, yet greater than zero. 
    - Like square roots of negatives (regrettably-named
      “imaginary numbers”), infinitesimals seem paradoxical: 
        - They manifestly _do not belong_ to the familiar system
          of real numbers

### 'Structural' theorems
    - the linearity properties (allows us to take derivatives of
      sums and constant multiples of functions)
    - product rule
    - quotient rule

### Lecture 2
        - Key idea: 
            - On an infinitesmal scale, curves are STRAIGHT.
            - ie, the curve and tangent line are
              indistinguishable
        - $MY_IMAGES/maths/analysis/Seth-Calculus/lec-02-01.jpg
        - $MY_IMAGES/maths/analysis/Seth-Calculus/lec-02-02.jpg
        - Curves are locally straight, but globally 'curvey'
        - There are a few odd exceptions to this about curves
          being locally straight, and in those cases calculus
          breaks down.
        - Now tie this idea to functions:
            - All curves are locally straight, so all graphs of
              functions are locally straight.
            - What sort of function has a straight line graph? 
                - `A linear function`!
            - So locally... all functions are _linear_ functions.
            - This is good news because linear functions are
              simple. That's why we study them in primary school.
        - Constant rate of change
            - Nice property: Linear functions exhibit a constant
              rate of change.
                - $MY_IMAGES/maths/analysis/Seth-Calculus/lec-02-03.jpg
            - Non-linear functions have a variable rate of
              change.
                - $MY_IMAGES/maths/analysis/Seth-Calculus/lec-02-04.jpg
        - Linear functions have a constant slope
            - $MY_IMAGES/maths/analysis/Seth-Calculus/lec-02-05.jpg
        - Non-linear functions have no global slope
            - but you can get the slope at a POINT
            - $MY_IMAGES/maths/analysis/Seth-Calculus/lec-02-06.jpg
        - "Derived" function
            - Hmmm... this really struck me about the 'derived
              function' or 'derivative'
            - for the original function `f` at point `a` you have
              `13` say...
            - But for the new derived function `f'` at point `a`,
              you get a different value: `2`
            - Never really thought of it like that. Both
              functions have the same domain, but `f'` outputs
              a totally different number at each point (compared
              to `f`)
            - I'm used to seeing the 'abstract' algebraic formula
              for `f'`
                - $MY_IMAGES/maths/analysis/Seth-Calculus/lec-02-07.jpg
            - We can ask:
                - where does `f`  send the point?
                - where does `f'` send the point?
        - Position function
            - Does the derivative of a position function have any
              significance?
                - Yes: the derivative gives us the local 'rate of
                  change' of `f`. It tells us how position is

### Lecture 3: Leibnitz notation
    - `∆x`: 
        - slightly unintuitive for some reason
        - -----------------------------------------------
        - `∆x` = (`x` after change) - (`x` before change)
        - -----------------------------------------------
    - `∆x` versus `dx`: 
        - Another point: `∆x` and `∆y` refer to a REAL change in
          `x` or a REAL change in `y`
            - as in a change in the REAL numbers
        - but `dx` is an infinitesmal change in `x`
            - aside: 
                - naming conventions: 
                - 'prime notation' versus 'Leibnitz notation'
                - $MY_IMAGES/maths/analysis/Seth-Calculus/lec-03-01-delta-x.jpg
    - small point:
        - With Leibnitz notation, we must specify which point...
        - ie we add 'when `x=7`' etc beside our `dy/dx` bit
    - what is the derivative?
        - it's a RATIO of INFINITESIMAL changes
    - Advantage of Leibnitz:
        - We see INSTANTLY what the independent variable is

    - Independent variables (subtle): 
    - ------------------------------- 
        - his example of the oven temperature `T` being
          controlled by the angle `θ` on the hands of a clock
        - So really, the oven temp `T` is also controlled
          (indirectly) by the TIME of day: little `t`!
            - $MY_IMAGES/maths/analysis/Seth-Calculus/lec-03-02-oven-clock.jpg
        - Constraint: 
            - the wider the angle, the greater the temperature
              `T`
        - Implication for `θ`: 
            - graph of `θ` v `T` is monotonically increasing?
            - means the slope is positive everywhere
                - $MY_IMAGES/maths/analysis/Seth-Calculus/lec-03-03.jpg
            - So it means the derivative `dT/dθ` is POSITIVE
              everywhere
                - $MY_IMAGES/maths/analysis/Seth-Calculus/lec-03-04.jpg
        - Implication for `t`: 
                - However `dT/dt` will NOT be positive everywhere
                - sometimes pos, sometimes neg
                    - $MY_IMAGES/maths/analysis/Seth-Calculus/lec-03-05.jpg
        - Key point:
            - Think of temperature `T` as a function of `θ`:
              derivative is always positive
            - Think of temperature `T` as a function of `t`:
              derivative varies in sign
            - So: not enough to speak of the derivative of
              a function. Must talk about the derivative WITH
              RESPECT TO a specific variable.

    - Higher order infinitesimal
    - --------------------------
        - (real)          * (real)          = real
        - (real)          * (tiny real)     = real
        - (real)          * (infinitesimal) = infinitesimal
        - (infinitesimal) * (infinitesimal) = infinitesimal `<--- 'higher order'`
        - -----------------------------------------------------------------------
        - When our concern is with REAL numbers, as it nearly
          always is, we can discount higher order infinitesimals
          as ZERO!
            - $MY_IMAGES/maths/analysis/Seth-Calculus/lec-03-06.jpg

    - Compute derivative
    - ------------------
        - from first principles...
        - When he creates his output point, he doesn't want to

### Microscopic resolution
    - In biology, the naked eye is perfectly good for some
      purposes, but a microscope lens reveals the microworld that
      ultimately explains 'reality' on our scale. 
    - Similarly, mathematicians found that `real numbers` suffice
      for BASIC measurement needs, but the PIXILATED image
      produced by the reals to our naked 'eye' can be sharpened
      by '_infinitesimal-sensitive_ lenses'. 

### Zoom in and out
    - Magnitudes that appear equal to the naked 'eye' (in terms
      of real numbers) may turn out to differ by an infinitesimal
      amount, when viewed through our infinitesimal-sensitive
      microscope, 
    - Conversely, magnitudes that differ by a mere infinitesimal
      when viewed under the microscope, correspond to the _same
      real number_ when viewed with the naked 'eye'.
