### Infinitesimals used by Physicists
    - The prohibition of infinitesimals didn't eliminate them
      altogether, but drove them underground instead.
    - Physicists and engineers continued to use them as
      a heuristic device for correctly deriving results in
      calculus for physical problems.
    - And differential geometers such as Lie and Cartan used them
      to formulate concepts later made 'rigorous'.
    - And, in a technical sense, they lived on in algebraists'
      investigations of `non-archimedean fields`.
    - The concept of infinitesimal even retained some public
      champions such as philosopher–mathematician Charles Sanders
      Peirce, who viewed the continuum (as did Brouwer) as
      arising from the subjective grasp of the flow of time and
      the subjective ‘now’ as a _nonpunctiform_ infinitesimal.
    - Some of his observations:
        - It is singular that nobody objects to `√−1` as
          involving any contradiction, nor, since Cantor, are
          infinitely great quantities objected to, but the old
          prejudice against infinitely small quantities still
          remains.
        - It's difficult to explain the fact of memory and our
          apparently perceiving the flow of time, unless we
          suppose immediate consciousness to extend _beyond
          a single instant_.
            - Yet if we make such a supposition we fall into
              grave difficulties, unless we suppose the time of
              which we are immediately conscious to be strictly
              infinitesimal.
        - [The] continuum does not consist of indivisibles, or
          points, or instants, and does not contain any except
          insofar as its continuity is ruptured.

### Infinitesimals aren't points!
    - Leibniz: 'A point may NOT be considered a part of a line'
    - An infinitesimal magnitude may be regarded as what remains
      after a (genuine) continuum has been subjected to an
      exhaustive analysis. In other words, as a continuum ‘viewed
      in the small’.
    - In this sense an infinitesimal may be taken to be an
      ‘ultimate part’ of a continuum.
    - We observe that the 'coherence' of a genuine continuum
      means that any of its (connected) parts is also
      a continuum, and accordingly, DIVISIBLE.
    - However, a point is by its nature _not divisible_, and so
      cannot be part of a continuum. (as Leibniz said)
    - Since an infinitesimal in the sense just described is
      a part of the continuum from which it has been extracted,
      it follows that it cannot be a point:
        - to emphasize this we shall call such infinitesimals
          `nonpunctiform`.


### Continuum
    - The traditional idea of a continuum: It's the domain over
      which a _continuously varying magnitude_ actually varies.
    - The characteristic features of a (connected) continuum are:
        - first, that it has `no gaps`.
            - It ‘coheres’ so that a magnitude varying over it
              has no ‘jumps’
        - secondly: it is `indefinitely divisible`.
    - Thus it has been held by many prominent thinkers that
      continua are _nonpunctate_ and NOT 'composed of' or
      'synthesized from' discrete points.
    - See for example:
        - Aristotle: ... no continuum can be made up out of
          indivisibles, granting that the line is continuous and
          the point indivisible.

        - Leibniz: A point may not be considered a part of
          a line.

        - Kant: Space and time are quanta continua ... points and
          instants mere _positions_ ... and out of mere positions
          viewed as constituents capable of being given prior to
          space and time, neither space nor time can be
          constructed.

        - Poincare: ... between the elements of the continuum
          [there is supposed to be] a sort of intimate bond which
          makes a whole of them, in which the point is NOT PRIOR
          to the line, but the line to the point.

        - Weyl: Exact time- or space-points are NOT the ultimate,
          underlying, atomic elements of the duration or
          extension given to us in experience.

        - Weyl: A true continuum is simply something connected in
          itself and cannot be split into separate pieces; that
          contradicts its nature.

        - Brouwer: The linear continuum is not exhaustible by the
          interposition of new units and can therefore never be
          thought of as a mere COLLECTION of units.

        - Rene Thom: ... a true continuum has NO POINTS.

    - Note that these views are much at variance with the
      generally accepted set-theoretical formulation of
      mathematics in which all mathematical entities, being
      synthesized from collections of individuals, are ultimately
      of a _discrete_ or _punctate_ nature.
    - This punctate character is possessed in particular by the
      set supporting the 'continuum' of real numbers – the
      'arithmetical continuum'.
    - As applied to the arithmetical continuum 'continuity' is
      accordingly not a property of the collection of real
      numbers per se, but derives rather from certain features of
      the additional structures – order-theoretic, topological,
      analytic – that are customarily imposed on it.


### Different from Robinson
    - A final remark: The theory of infinitesimals presented here
      shouldn't be confused with that known as 'nonstandard
      analysis', invented by Abraham Robinson in the 1960s.
        - The infinitesimals in his formulation are 'invertible'
          (arising, in fact, as the 'reciprocals' of infinitely
          large quantities).
    - But our Infinitesimals here, being `nilpotent`, cannot
      possess inverses.
    - The two theories also have quite different mathematical
      origins,
        - nonstandard analysis arising from `logic`, and
        - that presented here from `category theory`.
