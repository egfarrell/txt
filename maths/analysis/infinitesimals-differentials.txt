### 'nilsquare' Infinitesimal
    - nilsquare, nilpotent
        - https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JEr6sE1C2kc
    - Aside:
        - NEWTON   called infinitesimals: 'fluxions.'
        - Leibnitz called infinitesimals: 'differentials', dx, dy
    - Key concept:
        - an infinitesimal is a _non-zero quantity_ whose SQUARE
          is exactly ZERO.
    - Minute 7:00: this allows `dx^2` to be replaced with
      zero, BEFORE you divide by dx (which remains a non-zero
      divisor).
    - You then have `2xdx/dx = 2x`, a precise result which does
      not require `dx` to be treated as if it's zero.
    - As a consequence, however, you must discard the 'Law of
      Excluded Middle', and understand the deep implications of
      this.
        - Excluded middle: for every proposition, either this
          proposition or its negation is true.

### Discussion: difference from non-standard calculus
    - $MY_BOOKS/Physics/Maths/Analysis/Alt-View/Calculus-even-easier-infinitesimals.pdf
    - In standard calculus 
        - the fundamental concept is not the differential of
          a variable but the `derivative` of a function which is
          defined as a limit. 
        - The differential is then defined in terms of the
          derivative. The notion of a function and limits
          involves dependent and independent variables. 
    - In the infinitesimal approach 
        - the fundamental concept is the `differential` of any
          variable, regardless of dependence or independence, and
          the derivative is defined as a quotient of
          differentials.

    - What the passage means

        - In the usual limit-based calculus, the derivative
          `dy/dx` is defined first, using the limit of `Δy/Δx` as
          `Δx → 0`.
        - Only after that do we define the differential `dy` as
          `(dy/dx) dx`.

    - In an infinitesimal approach (historically in Leibniz, or
      formally in modern nonstandard analysis), the order is
      reversed:
        - Start by allowing actual infinitesimal quantities.
        - A differential such as `dx` is simply an infinitesimal change in `x`.
        - Any variable can have a differential, whether or not it is considered a “dependent” variable.
        - The `derivative` is then defined as the ratio `dy/dx` of two infinitesimals.

    - Instead of limits being the foundational idea,
      infinitesimals are. 
        - Derivatives are just quotients of differentials.

    - Simple example: `y = x²`
        - Start with an infinitesimal differential `dx`. 
        - It is not zero, but smaller than any real number in
          absolute value.

        - Consider the infinitesimal change in `y`:
            - dy = (x + dx)² − x²
            - dy = x² + 2x dx + (dx)² − x²
            - dy = 2x dx + (dx)².

        - Now `(dx)²` is an infinitesimal of second order: 
            - it is much smaller than dx and is discarded in the
              usual Leibniz-style reasoning. Keeping only
              first-order infinitesimals gives
            - dy ≈ 2x dx.

            - Since the derivative is defined as the quotient of differentials,
            - dy/dx ≈ 2x.

            - Because the approximation becomes exact when interpreted in the hyperreal framework, we conclude
            - dy/dx = 2x.

            - Notice what we never used: a limit. The derivative
              emerged directly from algebraic manipulation of
              infinitesimals.
