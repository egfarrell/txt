### 'Standard' calculus v 'Infinitesimal' Calculus
    - See more: $MY_NOTES/maths/analysis/infinitesimals.txt
    - $MY_BOOKS/Physics/Maths/Analysis/Alt-View/Calculus-even-easier-infinitesimals.pdf
    - In standard calculus 
        - the fundamental concept is not the differential of
          a variable but the `derivative` of a function which is
          defined as a limit. 
        - The differential is then defined in terms of the
          derivative. The notion of a function and limits
          involves dependent and independent variables. 
    - In the infinitesimal approach 
        - the fundamental concept is the `differential` of any
          variable, regardless of dependence or independence, and
          the derivative is defined as a quotient of
          differentials.

### Derivative / Slope
    - https://themathpage.com/aCalc/derivative.htm
    - "slope" only really applies for _straight_ lines
    - Now, since we consider `x` to be the independent variable
      and `y` the dependent, then any change `Δx` in the value of
      `x`, will result in a change `Δy` in the value of `y`.
        - In a straight line, the rate of change, so many units
          of `y` for each unit of `x`, is constant, and is called
          the SLOPE of the line.
    - However, Calculus IS CONCERNED with rates of change that
      are NOT CONSTANT.
    - If a curve represents distance `Y` versus time `X`, then
      the rate of change, the speed, at each moment of time is
      not constant.
    - The question calculus asks is: "What is the rate of change
      at exactly the `point P` ?"
    - The answer will be the SLOPE of the TANGENT LINE to the
      curve at that point.
    - And the method for finding that slope, _that NUMBER_, was
      the remarkable discovery by both Newton and Leibniz.
    - That is the method for finding what is called the
      derivative.

### What are infinitesimals?
    - $MY_NOTES/maths/analysis/Seth-Calculus.txt
    - By definition, an infinitesimal is an infinitely small
      number: smaller than every positive `real` number, yet
      still GREATER than zero.
    - NO real number satisfies this definition, so infinitesimals
      are _not real_.
        - Yet, by developing a "calculus of infinitesimals" (as
          it was known for two centuries), mathematicians got
          great insight into `real` functions, breaking through
          the static algebraic ice shelf to reach a flowing world
          of motion below, changing and evolving in time.
    - So, calculus is the mathematics of _continuous_ change.
    - But even as calculus ignited modern science, philosophical
      problems remained.
        - Do infinitesimals really exist?
        - Are they just useful fictions?
        - How could mathematics (of all subjects) risk having
          fictional foundations?
        - This wasn't resolved until the 19th century, when
          mathematicians developed 'limits' as a rigorous
          foundation for calculus.
        - The new 'limits' answered the philosophical questions,
          but made calculus less intuitive for average users (non
          philosophers!) who liked infinitesimals.
    - This book returns infinitesimals to the stage, restoring
      the intuitions that originally motivated them, putting you
      in touch with the thoughts of the intellectual giants who
      developed it.
        - You'll learn the same results as someone with a typical
          limit-based approach, but your path will be more
          intriguing, simpler and shorter.
        - (Most textbooks devote 500+ pages to developing
          single-variable calculus. Here- just 160 pages)

### Newton: 3 approaches!
    - $MY_BOOKS/Physics/Maths/Misc-History/Burton-history-maths-7e.pdf
    - Newton developed 3 different versions of his calculus,
      apparently searching for the best approach; or maybe each
      version served a different purpose:
        - to derive results effectively
        - to supply useful algorithms
        - to give convincing proofs
    - Thus Newton used:
        - Infinitesimals
            - largely a geometric approach,
        - 'Fluxions'
            - a kinematic approach,
        - 'Prime and ultimate ratios'
            - his most rigorous, "algebraic" approach.
    - The 3 methods weren't always kept apart when solving
      problems. See: DT Whiteside, Mathematical Papers Isaac
      Newton.
    - Or according to Keisler (the same 3 ways?)
        - $MY_BOOKS/Physics/Maths/Analysis/Alt-View/Calculus-Infinitesimal-Approach-Keisler.pdf
            - At various times Newton described the derivative
              of `y` (which he called the “fluxion” of y) in
              3 different ways:
              1. The INFINITESIMAL method
                - The ratio of an infinitesimal change in `y` to
                  an infinitesimal change in `x`.
              2. The VELOCITY method
                - The velocity of `y` where `x` denotes time.
              3. The LIMIT method
                - The limit of the ratio of the change in `y` to
                  the change in `x`, `Δy/Δx`, as `Δx` approaches
                  zero.
            - In his later writings Newton sought to avoid
              infinitesimals and emphasised methods 2 and 3.

        - Aside: "Transfer Principle" 
            - Leibniz consistently favored the infinitesimal
              method but believed (correctly) that the same
              results could be obtained using only real numbers.
            - He regarded the infinitesimals as “`ideal`” numbers
              like the imaginary numbers.
            - To justify them he proposed his law of continuity:
                - “In any supposed transition, ending in any
                  terminus, it is permissible to institute
                  a general reasoning, in which the terminus may
                  also be included.”
            - This “law” is far too imprecise by present
              standards. But it was a remarkable forerunner of
              the "Transfer Principle" on which modern
              infinitesimal calculus is based. Leibniz was on the
              right track, but 300 years too soon!

### Derivative: Explore Sensitivity
    - The next big idea to emerge was "differentiation": various
      techniques whose core idea is _ratios of change_.
    - Linear functions are special because the ratio of ...
        - change in output to change in input ...
        - is CONSTANT.
    - In the first millennium, Indian astronomers discovered what
      we think of as the `derivatives` of `sine` and `cosine` as
      they explored how changes in arc length affected changes in
      the corresponding lengths of chords.
    - They were exploring _sensitivity_, one of the key
      applications of the derivative:
        - understanding how small changes in one variable will
          affect another variable to which it is linked.
    - (I never got this idea at Uni, but came up in ML)

      ratios of change appeared in the guise of tangent lines.
    - And this was connected to the general study of rates of
      change.
    - Calculus was born with Newton when he came to realise that
      the techniques for solving problems of accumulation and
      ratios of change were inverse to each other.
    - Thus enabling natural philosophers to use solutions found
      IN ONE REALM to answer questions in the _other_.

### Fundamental Theorem of Calculus
      - do we recover the `f`'s from the `v`'s?
    - The whole relation between differential calculus and
      integral calculus rests on the fact that taking sums is the
      inverse of taking differences.
    - We saw the _discrete form of differentiation_ in equation
      (1); now we see the discrete form of integration in
      equation (2}.
    - Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (before limits).
        - If each `v_j = f_j - f_j-1` then
        - the sum v_1 + v_2 ··· v_n equals f_n - f_0
        - The area under the graph of `v` is the change in `f`.
        - Proof: really nice

        - Their meaning for integral is _area_ of a region
          bounded by a curve.
        - The net result is the FTC seems nothing more than a way
          to compute definite integrals. It adds nothing to their
          understanding of derivatives or integrals.
    - BETTER WAY, more generalisable
        - The standard statement of the FTC is _not_ true in
          general when integrals are areas and derivatives are
          slopes(!)
        - The integral `∫f(x)` gives the area of a region bounded
          by `x=a`, `x=b`, and `y = f(x)` 
            - ONLY when `y` and `x` are ordinate and abscissa in
              a _Cartesian_ coordinate system. 
            - It doesn’t work in any other coordinate system(!)
        - Also, in a polar coordinate system, the graph of
          `y=mx+b` is a spiral, not a line, making the definition
          of the derivative as "slope of a tangent" _unworkable_.
        - As I will clarify later,
            - ------------------------------------------------
            - integral as `accumulation` from rate of change and
            - derivative as `rate of change` of accumulation are
              NOT DEPENDENT on a coordinate system.
            - ------------------------------------------------
        - Pat Thompson's goal:
            - ... that students understand a calculus that arises
              from their reasoning about _quantities_ and
              relationships among quantities
            - Ah just like the key idea from JF Riley

### Interesting: Calculus Made Even Easier
    - Calculus Even Easier: Infinitesimal Differential Approach
        - $MY_BOOKS/Physics/Maths/Analysis/Alt-View/Calculus-even-easier-infinitesimals.pdf
    - (similar approach to Tevian Dray, especially the way in
      Physics we relate PHYSICAL QUANTITIES through EQUATIONS,
      rather than the independent/dependent variables of
      mathematical functions)
    - This book is intended for engineers who are required to
      take calculus and want a more intuitive presentation.
    - This is a non-rigorous infinitesimal approach focusing on
      `differentials` of variables that represent physical
      quantities rather than derivatives as limits of
      mathematical functions.
    - In science, variables are related in _equations_ so this is
      the focus rather than dependent and independent variables
      of _functions_.
    - These methods were originally conceived by Leibniz over 300
      years ago and used successfully by scientists ever since.
    - It is written in the spirit of 'Calculus Made Easy' by S.
      Thompson. Thompson wrote his book in 1910 before
      infinitesimals were legitimized. He used the concept of
      'little bits' of variables. Hopefully infinitesimal
      differentials will make calculus even easier.
