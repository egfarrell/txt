# LIMITS
--------

### How do we know if theres a horizontal asympotote?
    - Check limits at infinity. As x--> infinity, f(x) --> L

### what about sin(1/x) at zero?
    - DNE Does not evaluate. 
    - Has to keep going between 1 and -1 but at shorter and shorter intervals

### Misconceptions
    - asymptote - Cannot be crossed?
    - No! see `sinx/x`

    - horizontal left and right totes must be the same ? 
    - No! inverse tan of x

### Sandwich theorm
    - Evaluating Limits at `a`, Rational Functions
    - If you can plug in `a` and get something sensible then limit exists at `a`
    - If you get something insensible (zero/zero) then its an indeterminate form.
    - zero on the bottom, then verticle asymptote.
    - tricky square root sign above or below, multiply by conjugate.

tricks: factorise
tricks: divide by highest degree term and multiply by same term
       This zaps all the other terms down to zero

### Summary: 
    - degree on top higher? Goes to +-infinity
    - degree on top lower? Goes to zero.
    - Degrees equal? Goes to ratio of leading coefficients

### Trick with negative square roots!
    - We know `x` is negative so whats the sqrt(4x^6)? Its MINUS
      2x^3!!! 
    - Dont forget minus. Cos we're squaring an (ODD) cube
      function.

### Limits for absolute values.
    - Should: Convert into piecewise function.
    - basically for the standard formula of absolute value, 
    - stick in whatever youre trying to evaluate.

    - Has nice example with |x-3|/x-3 ... just watch you cant
      have zero in denominator. (Its a step function)


### two sided limits 
    - DNE sometimes
    - lim 1/x = + - infinity

    - How do we know if theres a vertical asymptote at "a"?
    - Well one or both of the left and right limits --> infinity
      at "a"



# INTEGRATION
-------------

### Substitution Methods
    - ie reverse the chain rule

    ### log trick
        - if you have a function `f(x)` as the denominator
        - and its derivative `f'(x)` as the numerator
        - then theres a quick shortcut and the integral evaluates
          to `ln|f(x)| + c`

### definite integrals - migrate the bounds!
    - if you substitute over to `t-land`, you must change the bounds of integration as well

### nth root of linear in x
    - (ax +c)^1/5 .... then let t^5 = (ax + c) and barrel away at the normal method

### trick with e^x and e^2x
    - notice that e^2x is actually (e^x)^2

### Integration by parts
    - ie reverse the product rule

    - mainly apply the formula with the correct choice of `u` and `dv`

    - start with the product rule written out
    - then solve for `u*dv/dx`
    - then integrate both side
    - then by the chain rule cancel all the dx's
    - youre left with: `∫udv = uv - ∫vdu`

### you get your original integral back!
    - sometimes you integrate by parts (twice) and you end up with your original integral on RHS.
    - then move it back over the equals sign and readjust by any constant

### integrate ln(x)
    - can do this by parts aswell! 
    - let `dx = dv`

### integrate inverse trig functions
    - do this by parts aswell!

### Trig integration
    - theres 2 trig identities that are used all the time
    - cos(x)^2 = 1/2(1-cos(2x))
    - sin(x)^2 = 1/2(1-sin(2x))
    - this comes from: cos(2x) = 2*cos(x)^2 - 1 

### odd powers of trig functions
    - ∫cos{x}^m*sin{x}^n
    - take odd power, or lowest odd power if both odd, and lower it by one, pairing the thing with dx
    - then write your new even power as cos{x}^2 or sin{x}^2
    - then turn them all into t's
