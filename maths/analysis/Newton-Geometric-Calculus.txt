### Geometric Calculus
    - $MY_BOOKS/Physics/Maths/Analysis/Alt-View/Series-and-Products-from-15th-Century-Ranjan-Roy.pdf
    - Book: Sources in the Development of Mathematics:
        - Series and Products from 15th to 21st Century,
        - pp. 97-119 (Chapter 7)

    - During the decade 1660–1670, the discoveries of the
      previous 25 years on the mathematics of infinitesimals were
      systematized, unified, and extended.
    - The earlier discoveries included the integration of `y
      = xm/n`, `y = sin x` or `cos x`; the connection of the area
      under the hyperbola with the logarithm; the reduction of
      the problem of finding arc length to that of quadrature;
      the method for finding the tangent to a curve; and the
      procedure for determining the max or min point on a curve.
    - Before 1660, the INTERDEPENDENCE between problems on
      _construction of tangents_ and problems concerning _areas
      under curves_ had been evident only in special cases, but
      during this decade, Isaac Barrow (1630–1677), James Gregory
      (1638–1675), and Isaac Newton (1642–1727) independently
      discovered the fundamental theorem of calculus.
    - Gregory and Barrow stated this result as a theorem in
      geometry, whereas Newton, deeply influenced by Descartes's
      _algebraic approach_, gave it a form recognizable even
      today.
    - Later Newton adopted the _geometric perspective_ of his
      Principia. It's interesting that evaluations of the
      trigonometric functions took a _very simple form_ when
      performed geometrically.
    - In the geometric calculus, one got direct visual contact
      with the _elementary functions_ and their properties...
        - whereas the abstract approach, while more general and
          widely applicable, gave less insight into its
          underpinnings.

### Tristan: Strange numbers? -4, -5, -7
    - Journal Paper: Newton and the Transmutation of Force.
    - 'Geometric Calculus'
        - $MY_BOOKS/Physics/Maths/Analysis/Alt-View/Tristan-Geometrical-Calculus-Newton.pdf
    - What does this mean?
        - With the exception of the pair `[1, -2]`, the integer
          power laws `(A = -4, -5, -7)` that are singled out in
          (17) are rather mysterious.
        - Their physical significance is unknown to me, and that
          in itself is strange, for the _music of mathematics_ is
          seldom played without an accompanying echo heard in
          Nature.
        - At the purely mathematical level, however, there is
          more here than first meets the eye.
        - For example, consider the following question (see [9]):
            - for which integer power laws that diminish with
              distance is the _polar equation_ of the orbit
              expressible in terms of elliptic functions?
            - It turns out that there are precisely three.
            - They are: -4, -5, and -7!
