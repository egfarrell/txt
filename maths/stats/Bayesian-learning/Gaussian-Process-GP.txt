### 1) Big-picture: What’s the problem?

- You have data points `(x_i, y_i)` scattered non-linearly.
- You want to predict `y` at new `x`, but don’t want to assume
  a specific fixed function form (e.g., `y = ax^2 + bx + c`).
- *Gaussian Process (GP) regression* is a way to predict with
  uncertainty *without* fixing a specific function shape.

### 2) The key idea: Functions as random variables

- Instead of guessing *one* function, imagine a *distribution
  over possible functions* that could fit your data.
- This distribution is defined so that *any finite collection of
  y-values at chosen x-values follows a multivariate Gaussian
  distribution* — hence the name *Gaussian Process*.
- The GP gives a mean function (our best guess) and a variance
  (how uncertain we are) at every `x`.

### 3) Analogy: Mountain range of functions

- Picture a landscape of infinitely many hills and valleys
  (possible functions).
- The data points you already know act like stakes hammered into
  the landscape, pinning it down near those points.
- The terrain between the stakes reflects where the function can
  flex — the spread of possible hills gives your prediction
  uncertainty.

### 4) Core components of a GP

- Mean function:
    - your *a priori* guess of the average function
    - (often set to 0 if you have no bias).
- Covariance function (kernel):
    - decides *how outputs at different x are related*
    - e.g., do points close in `x` have similar `y`?

  - Popular choice:
        - squared exponential kernel
        - `k(x, x') = sigma^2 * exp(- (x - x')^2 / (2 * l^2))`
        - where l controls smoothness.

### 5) How GP regression works

- Before seeing data: you have *prior* belief — functions drawn
  randomly from the GP.
- After seeing data: you *condition* the GP on your points `(x_i,
  y_i)` → *posterior* GP updated to fit known points.
- The GP posterior gives you:

  - predicted mean `y(x*)` at new `x*`
  - predicted uncertainty (variance) at `x*`.

### 6) Vivid example

- Suppose you measure temperature at a few times of day and want
  a smooth temperature curve across the day.
- GP regression predicts a curve passing through your
  measurements but also tells you *where* you’re confident (small
  error bars) or not (wide error bars).

### 7) Why it’s powerful

- Flexible: can fit almost any smooth function.
- Uncertainty-aware: gives error bars naturally.
- Non-parametric: complexity grows with data, no fixed number of
  parameters like in linear regression.

### 8) Downsides

- Computational cost: involves inverting large matrices → scales
  poorly with many data points.
- Choosing the kernel hyperparameters (l, sigma, etc.) needs care
  (usually by maximizing likelihood on data).

### Aside: Link to MCMC
    - Gaussian Process (GP) regression gives a posterior
      distribution over functions directly;
        - if kernel parameters are fixed, no MCMC is needed — you
          can calculate predictions analytically.

- But real problems often involve uncertain HYPERPARAMETERS
    - (e.g., kernel length-scale l, noise sigma).

    - Then you need to average predictions over these uncertain
      hyperparameters.

    - This is where Monte Carlo (esp. MCMC) comes in:
        - You draw samples of hyperparameters from their posterior.
        - For each sampled hyperparameter set, compute GP predictions.
        - Average these predictions → integrate over hyperparameter uncertainty.

- So `GP` + `MCMC` = Bayesian hierarchical modeling:
    - `GP` handles uncertainty over functions given
      hyperparameters.
    - `MCMC` handles uncertainty over the hyperparameters
      themselves.

- Without MCMC, you’d have to fix hyperparameters to point
  estimates (e.g., by maximum likelihood) → less fully Bayesian.


### GP History
    - Origins:
        - Emerged from work on stochastic processes in
          probability theory.
        - Named after Carl Friedrich Gauss; exploits properties
          of Gaussian distributions.
        - Early 20th-century stats: Kolmogorov, Wiener developed
          foundations of random functions.

    - Key figures:
        - Norbert Wiener: Wiener process links Brownian motion
          & Gaussian processes.
        - Harold Hotelling: stats on covariance functions.
        - In modern ML, Carl Rasmussen & Chris Williams (Gaussian
          Processes for Machine Learning, 2006) popularized GPs
          for regression/classification.

    - Core idea:
        - GP defines a distribution over functions: any finite
          set of points has joint Gaussian distribution.
        - Covariance (kernel) function encodes assumptions about
          smoothness, periodicity, etc.
