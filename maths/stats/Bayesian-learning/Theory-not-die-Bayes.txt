Over the years Fisher, Egon Pearson, and Neyman would develop a host
of powerful statistical techniques. Fisher and Neyman became fervent
anti-Bayesians who limited themselves to events that could
theoretically be repeated many times; regarded samples as their only
source of information; and viewed each new set of data as a separate
problem, to be used if the data were powerful enough to provide
statistically significant conclusions and discarded if not. As
anti-Bayesians, they banned subjective priors, although they did not
argue with Bayes’ theorem when the priors were known; the difficulties
and controversies arose when the prior probabilities were unknown.
Neyman, for example, denounced Bayes’ equal-prior shortcut as
“illegitimate.”37
