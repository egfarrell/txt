### Standard Error
    - Audio recording: 20200120-210846.txt
    - So the `standard error` is the `standard deviation` of our
      sampling distribution.
    - But the problem is we never take hundreds and thousands of
      samples to actually build the sampling distribution.
    - We actually don't know it. So we have to estimate the
      standard error.
    - So how do we do that?
    - We estimate the standard error from our one single sample.
    - So we get the standard deviation of our one single sample
      and that's divided by the square root of the number of
      samples.
    - And that gives us an estimate of the standard error.
    - So just to repeat, even though we know by the central limit
      theorem that the sampling distribution of the mean, for
      example, will be normally distributed,
    - we never actually create the sampling distribution but we
      do estimate the standard error.


### precision recall
    - Precision-Recall (PR) curve and the Receiver Operating
      Characteristic (ROC) curve might seem similar at a glance,
      especially in their shape and behavior, they actually serve
      different purposes and are affected differently by the data
      distribution, particularly in the context of class
      imbalance.
    - Here are some key differences and similarities:

    - Axes and Focus:
        - The ROC curve plots True Positive Rate (TPR, or recall)
          against False Positive Rate (FPR).
        - The PR curve plots Precision against Recall.
        - Both curves evaluate the performance of a classifier at
          different thresholds, but they focus on different
          aspects of the performance.

    - Behavior with Class Imbalance:
        - The ROC curve can present an overly optimistic view of
          the classifier's performance in cases of severe class
          imbalance because FPR does not capture the actual
          negative instances well.
        - The PR curve is more informative for imbalanced
          datasets as it focuses on the performance of the
          positive class, which is typically the minority class.

    - Interpretation:
        - A good classifier tends to move towards the top-left
          corner in the ROC space (high TPR, low FPR).
        - For the PR curve, a good classifier moves towards the
          top-right corner (high Precision and Recall).

    - Visual Similarity:
        - Both curves might appear "mirrored" or "inverted"
          versions of each other, especially in binary
          classification tasks. This is because they plot related
          metrics (TPR/Recall and FPR/Precision) but in different
          orientations.

    - In summary, while ROC and PR curves might visually resemble
      each other, they provide different insights. The PR curve
      is often more revealing in situations where you have
      a class imbalance or when the cost of false positives is
      high. The ROC curve is more general and does not focus on
      any class.

### Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC)
    - key:
        - graph of TRUE POSITIVES v FALSE POSITIVES
        - ie: "sensitivity" v "specificity"
        - $MY_IMAGES/maths/statistics/ROC-curve.jpg
    - Imagine you're the captain of a spaceship, and your mission
      is to navigate through a dense asteroid field. Your
      spaceship is equipped with a special radar that can detect
      incoming asteroids, but sometimes it's not perfect, and you
      get false alarms.
        - On the other hand, there are real asteroids that the
          radar fails to detect, which could be dangerous.
    - The `Receiver Operating Characteristic`, or ROC, is like
      a radar performance report that helps you understand how
      well your radar is working.
        - In this case, it's not about asteroids but about
          a binary classification system, like diagnosing
          a disease (positive or negative), approving a loan
          (good or bad credit), or detecting spam emails (spam or
          not spam).
    - The ROC curve shows how well your classifier (like the
      radar) can distinguish between the two classes by plotting
      its performance at various decision thresholds. Here's how
      it works:
        - Sensitivity (True Positive Rate):
            - Imagine this as the radar's ability to correctly
              detect asteroids. It tells you the proportion of
              real positives (correctly detected asteroids) to
              all the actual positives (both detected and
              undetected asteroids).
        - Specificity (True Negative Rate):
            - This is like the radar's ability to correctly
              ignore harmless space debris and not raise false
              alarms. It tells you the proportion of real
              negatives (correctly ignored harmless debris) to
              all the actual negatives (both ignored and falsely
              alarmed debris).
        - Decision Thresholds:
            - Your radar can be set to different sensitivity and
              specificity levels by adjusting its sensitivity
              threshold. Lowering the threshold might help you
              detect more asteroids, but it may also result in
              more false alarms.
    - The ROC curve is drawn by calculating `sensitivity` and
      `specificity` at various threshold levels. It helps you
      visualize the trade-off between sensitivity and
      specificity.
        - Ideally, you want both values to be high, which means
          you detect most real positives and ignore most real
          negatives.
        - The closer the ROC curve is to the top-left corner of
          the graph, the better your classifier is at
          distinguishing the two classes.
    - In summary, the ROC curve provides a clear picture of how
      your classifier is performing, just like the radar report
      shows how well your spaceship's radar is navigating through
      the asteroid field.
        - The goal is to have a high-performing classifier,
          represented by a curve that hugs THE TOP-LEFT CORNER,
          indicating excellent sensitivity and specificity at
          various decision thresholds.

### Difference Bayesian stats and ML
    - https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=34387081
    - Machine learning fundamentally cares about model
      performance on other _data_, like validation or test sets.
        - It is looking for models that perform well, but not
          necessarily model the underlying process.
    - Bayesian statistics, like most statistics, wants to
      accurately estimate parameters in the model.
        - It cares most about _models_ that are portraying the
          underlying data-generating process.

### Confounding variables
    - https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=30607692
    > ... because approaches like this often have virtually no
    > explanatory power.
    - Feynman responded to this when the public demand an
      "explanatory theory". The answer is usually "don't know":
        - why does gravity work like that?
            - Don't know, but we can calculate orbits to great
              precision;
        - why do photons or electrons travel like that?
            - Don't know, but we can make very precise
              predictions via QED.
    - The public demand an explanatory theory, because there's an
      underlying assumption they don't even verbalize: that such
      an explanatory theory can be understood _without the
      maths_, that the principles of the laws of physics operate
      on basic, understandable lego pieces.
    - What is somewhat inconceivable is that the explanation _is_
      the maths. To whit, we don't even have an explanation for
      the idea of inertia! We just observe it, and measure it (as
      mass). There's no explanatory theory.

        - _Confounding variables_ are a strong argument against
          this. Give me two sets of data with `no causal
          relationship`, and it's very possible (if not likely)
          I'll be able to demonstrate not only a causal
          relationship but also observational predictability
          through the existence of confounding variables.
        - Let me use ML models and this becomes trivial.
        - Do school names determine how good a school will be?
            - Of course not.
            - However, schools have names which tend to be
              reused: cultural icons, scientists, etc..
            - And schools in low-performing areas are going to
              have a different distribution of chosen names than
              schools in high performing areas.
            - So you can create a model that will not only
              demonstrate this but also offer legitimate
              observational predictability that a school's name
              will, on average, determine how well it will do.
        - The only way you could really refute this hypothesis is
          by understanding the true confounding variable, or by
          running some huge scale multiple decades long
          experiment where you changed the names of new schools
          and measured the performance difference.
            - That experiment is clearly not really practicable,
              so all you're left with is understanding.
        - And this is really where the risk is.
            - If we ever begin to build upon something that is
              unsound then it risks everything from that point
              onward being also invalid.
            - At best everything collapses and you realize you
              must have made a mistake.
            - At worse, you simply end up adding endless
              epicycles until at some point everything starts to
              completely stall out muddled in its own invalid
              fundamentals and irreconcilable complexity.

### Hogg INverting Matrices
    - One thing I throw into my papers these days is about
      numerical linear-algebra operators.
    - My advice:
        - Never use the `invert()` function to invert a matrix!
        - Why not? Because you almost never want the inverse, you
          want the inverse applied to a vector.
    - So when you are tempted to do `dot( inv(A), x )` you should
      instead do `solve( A, x )`.
    - The first gets a machine-precision (if you are lucky)
      inverse of `A` and applies it to `x`.
        - The latter gets a machine-precision (if you are lucky)
          estimate of `A-inverse` applied to `x`.
        - You see the difference?
    - Alternative method:
        - Well, all of that got complexified today when I learned
          (from Soledad Villar NYU) about `lstsq()`.
        - This does the same as `solve()` (and more) but it is
          zero-safe.
    - Meaning:
        - It gives good answers even when the `A` matrix has zero
          eigenvalues. I think my advice may be evolving.

    - But 'Expectation' is ...

        - Here the expectation value is `3.5`.
        - Its the weighted average (weighted by probability)
        - of the numerical value of each individual outcome?
            - `(1*1/6) + (2*1/6) + (3*1/6) + (4*1/6) + (5*1/6)
              + (6*1/6) = 3.5`

### Derive Bayes Theorem

    ### conditional probability
        - To find the conditional probability
          `P(E|F)` look at the event `(E and F)` as part of
          the _REDUCED_ sample space F.

        - ie `P(E|F) = P(E and F) / P(F)`

    ### Intuitive facts from this definition:
        - Once E occurs its (already) certain:
            - `P(E|E) = 1`

        - If E and F are mutually exclusive:
            - `P(E|F) = 0`

        - Rearranging the conditional probability definition
          gives us a multiplication rule:
          - `P(E and F) = P(E|F)*P(F)`

        - and if E and F are independent (`P(E|F)=P(E)`) this
          reduces to the normal multiplication rule.
            - `P(E and F) = P(E) * P(F)`

    ### Now bayes
        - "posterior" probability
        - same as "conditional" probability

        - derive bayes
        - P(Cause and Effect) = P(Cause) × P(Effect given Cause)
        - P(Cause and Effect) = `P(Effect) × P(Cause given Effect)`
        - therefore:
        - P(Cause) = `P(Effect) × P(Cause given Effect)` / P(Effect given Cause)

    - Q: Is there a calculus based approach to statistics? Like

    - A: Statistics/Probability theory is really deeply tied to

    - The trick is that they aren't Riemann integrals, but a more

    - The main difference between the two is that a Riemann

    - So you can make measurable functions that map
      `f(set) -> how likely that set happens `
    - (which is exactly the probability)

# sampling error
    - difference between population mean and sample mean
    - `μ - x̄`

    - my question: is the sampling error the same as the
      standard error?

    - answer: Standard error is a measure of sampling error!

    - There are other measures of sampling error, but
      standard error is by far, the most commonly used when
      dealing with survey data.

      But one important point: sampling error is _NOT_ the
      only reason for a difference between your survey
      estimate (based on your survey sample) and the true
      value in the population.

      Another (arguably more important) reason for this
      difference is _bias_. Bias can be introduced
        - when designing the sampling scheme,
        - writing the questionnaire / data collection form,
        - collecting the survey data,
        - or analyzing the survey data.

      Most forms of bias cannot be calculated nor measured
      _after the data are collected_, and are often
      therefore invisible. Bias must be avoided by using
      correct procedures at each step of the survey process.

      Bias has NOTHING to do with sample size which affects
      only sampling error and standard error. As a result,
      large sample sizes do NOT eliminate bias.

      In fact, the larger your sample size, the more teams
      you need to collect data for whom it is more difficult
      to provide the necessary supervision; thus, increasing
      the likelihood of bias in the data collection.

      I think it best to use a minimal sample size so that
      survey managers can provide good supervision and data
      quality checks to ensure a minimum of potentially
      invisible bias.

    - another answer: Sample error is an important concept
      for 2 reasons:

    1. in order to distinguish itself from other kinds of
       error or biases, such as sampling _bias_

    2. common statistics such as standard error and
       confidence intervals are ONLY _measures_ of the
       effect of sampling error.

    If you have other biases in your sampling technique,
    then the standard errors of your estimates won’t capture
    that, and so you can become overconfident of your
    statistical tests, which usually means risking too much
    money. Also, building _confidence intervals_ assumes
    that the _ONLY source of error_ is sampling error.

    - The relative frequencies must sum to 1, therefore the area
      under the normal distribution must sum to 1.
    - So theres a link between area under the curve and
      _probabilities_. (!!!) This link all comes from relative
      frequencies.

# Difference CLT and 'Law of Large Numbers'
    - On a very intuitive level, the law of large numbers tells
      you what happens to the sample mean of a _single sample_ as
      your number of observations becomes large.

    - The central limit theorem tells you what happens to the
      sample means of a _bunch of samples_ as you get more
      observations in each of them.

# z-scores
    * Definition 1: A z-score is a numerical measurement of
      a values _relationship_ to the _MEAN_ of a group of
      values. If the z-score is 0, it means the score is
      identical to the mean score.

    * The z-score may be positive or negative. A positive
      value means the score is above the mean and vice
      versa. Positive or negative scores reveal the _number
      of standard deviations_ a value is above or below the
      mean.

    * Definition 2: A 'standard score' (z-score) is the
      _signed_ number of standard deviations an observation
      is above/below the mean. A positive standard score
      indicates an observation above the mean, while
      a negative standard score indicates a datum below the
      mean. It is a dimensionless quantity obtained by
      subtracting the _population mean_ from an individual
      raw score and dividing this difference by the
      _population_ standard deviation.

    * The z-score is only defined if one knows the
      population parameters; if one only has a sample set,
      then the analogous computation with sample mean and
      sample standard deviation yields the _Students
      t-statistic_.

    * Definition 3: The standard score (more commonly
      referred to as a z-score) is a very useful statistic
      because it

        1. allows us to calculate the probability of a score
           occurring within our _normal distribution_ and

        2. enables us to compare two scores that are from
           different normal distributions.

      The standard score does this by converting (in other
      words, standardizing) scores in a normal distribution
      to z-scores in what becomes a standard normal
      distribution. T

# jake astrohack: Hoggs definition of a model

      data (D) given some parameters (Θ) ie `P(D|Θ)`
    - note `P(D|Θ)` is the opposite of what we care about. We

    - note: for `P(D|Θ)` the `Θ` parameters could be the slope

      generate the data. We can draw the line and ask whats the
      random scatter around that line and generate the data.

# Frequentist
    - Often this approach is really _computationally_ convenient.
    - You find a _point estimate_ and you perform some procedure
      that finds the confidence bounds around that.
    - Also if the situation is you're repeating something over
      and over and you want to make true probabilistic statements
      about how the procedure will perform in the future: then
      its a useful way to go.

# difference Frequentist v Bayesian
    - Frequentists are trying to find long term repetitions.
    - Instead of trying to find a probability, they are trying to
      find an _estimator_, ie. some sort of point estimate
      'theta-hat' `ˆθs` of our science parameters we expect.
    - Whereas Bayes is trying to come up with a _probability_ of
      the parameters given the data: `P(Θs ... | D ...)`

### Peter Coles blog
    > we find tentative evidence for Planck-scale structure
    > near black hole horizons at 2.9σ significance level
    > (corresponding to false detection probability of 1 in
    > 270).

    - What is wrong is that the _significance level_ is not the
      same as the _false detection_ probability.  While it is
      usually the case that the false detection probability
      (which is often called the false discovery rate) will
      decrease, the lower your p-value is, these two quantities
      are not the same at all. Usually the false detection
      probability is much higher than the p-value.

    - Basically it’s because the p-value relates to the
      probability of seeing a signal at least as large as that
      observed under a null hypothesis (eg. _detector noise_)
    - ... but says nothing directly about the probability of it
      being produced by an actual signal.
    - To answer this latter question properly one really needs to
      use a Bayesian approach...

### misc (summer 2016?)
    - Often when mathematicians talk about probability they
      start with a known probability distribution then talk
      about the probability of events.

    - The true advantage of the Central Limit Theorem is it
      allows us use the normal distribution as an approximation
      in cases where we do not know the true distribution.

    - You could ask your father a standard statistics question
      (but phrased as math) about the probability the mean of
      a sample will be greater than a given value, if the data
      comes from a distribution with mean `μ` and sd `σ`...
    - Then see if he assumes a distribution (which you then say
      we don't know) or says that he needs to know the
      distribution. Then you can show that we can approximate the
      answer using the CLT in many cases.

    - To compare Maths to Stats, I like the 'mean value theorem'
      of integration as an example. This says that for an
      integral from `a` to `b` there exists a rectangle from `a`
      to `b` with the same area, and the height of the rectangle
      is the average of the curve.

        - The _mathematician_ looks at this and says "cool, I can
          use an integration to compute an average",

        - The _statistician_ sees the same theorem and says
          "cool, I can use an average to compute an integral".

        - I actually have wall hangings in my office of the mean
          value theorem and the CLT (and Bayes theorem!).
