# Bell Curve (normal) (guaussian)

- In probability theory, the normal (or Gaussian) distribution is
  a continuous probability distribution often used as an
  approximation to describe real-valued random variables that
  tend to cluster around a single mean value.

- The graph of the associated probability density function is
  “bell”-shaped, and is known as the Gaussian function or bell
  curve:

    - where parameter `μ` is the mean (location of the peak)
    - and `σ^2` is the variance (the measure of the width of the
      distribution).

    - The distribution with `μ = 0` and `σ^ 2 = 1` is called the
      standard normal.


- The binomial distribution, which is discrete, "becomes" the
  normal distribution after many many trials.

- The binomial distribution is always finite, you can only have
  a finite number of values.

- But the normal distribution is defined over the entire real
  number line.
- So normal distribution could give a tiny tiny tiny probability
  of something mad happening, e.g. all the atoms in my body
  rearranging or something.

`f(z) = 1/sqrt(2pi) * e ^ -(z^2/2)`

- Thats its definition using the z score way, where the z score
  is: `(x-μ)/ σ`


# Binomial Distribution

- The binomial distribution, which is discrete, "becomes" the
  normal distribution after many many trials.

- The coefficents in the probability density are combinations. ie
  n choose k type stuff.

- Example 1: How many heads when I flip a coin 6 times

- Example 2: How many shots do I make if the probability of
  nailing it is 0.3 (He used Excel aswell)

```
P(X=k) = (k successful trials from n total trials)
                     * (Prob success)^K times
                          * (prob failure)^n-k times
```

    ### Random Variable X
    = How many cars pass by in one hour?

    ### Start:
    Get the _mean_ or _expectation value_ `E[X] = λ` by
    watching the cars go by. Say for this example, 9.3 per hour

    ### Now the key point:
    He models the Poisson by starting with a rough Bionomial
    distribution!!!!

    ### Write the key point again:
    He models the Poisson by starting with a rough Bionomial
    distribution!!!!

    ### Expectation of a Bionomial
    `E[X] = np`
    where
    `n` = number of trials,
    `p` = probability of success

    ### lets model his road-side observations as a _binomial_
    Expectation = E[X] :
    λ (empirical) 	 = n * p (binomial)
    # cars per hour  = (60 one minute trials per hour) * (λ/60 mins probability)

    P(X=k)			 = (60 choose k) * [`(λ/60    ) ^ k`   successes]
                                     * [`(1 - λ/60) ^ 60-k`failures ]

    ### So its roughly modelled as a binomial
    and not a bad approximation. But what if _more_ than one
    car passes in a minute?? At the moment, we're just
    counting successes by the minute...

    ### So lets make it a bit more granular:
    (now have a trial every second)
    P(X=k)	 = (3600 choose k) * [`(λ/3600    ) ^ k`     successes]
                               * [`(1 - λ/3600) ^ 3600-k`failures ]

    Thats a bit better again, not a bad approximation.
    But what if two cars come within half a second??

    - can keep making it more and more granular -

    - and in the limit as n -> ∞ we get the poisson!
