### 01 Statisticians – Acquiring data/Understanding customer needs

    - I reviewed the imputation methods for the Financial
      Services survey (FSS) addressing customers’ concerns about
      the quality of estimates for security dealers. I met with
      the customers (FSS team) to understand the issues and their
      impact. We discussed options to improve estimates and
      I noted their preferred solution and delivery deadline.
      I reiterated any unclear points to ensure I understood the
      problem. Afterwards I sent a summary email listing the
      issues, agreed actions and project timelines. I identified
      an analysis plan and sourced the data.

    - I recommended the FSS team carry out manual imputation
      because of the volatility of security dealer returns. This
      option is used when missing data is not suitable for
      automatic treatment. I identified the amount of data to be
      manually constructed and discovered this would cause an
      unacceptable burden on the customer as hundreds of values
      had to be manually input. As this would not meet customer
      needs, I built a software system to automatically generate
      a dataset based on historical responses as well as
      graphical trend plots. I pitched this new system as
      a time-saving tool to improve efficiency for the customer.
      To aid its adoption, I wrote a set of desk instructions for
      the team. I asked them about their existing IT skills and
      used that to pitch the instructions at the correct level.

    - Overall the project was a success and the customers
      commented on the improved quality of the data as well as
      the increased efficiency from the software tool.

### 02 Statisticians – Data analysis

    - For my Physics degree, I used *Cross-Validation* to
      fine-tune a Decision Tree model which predicts the distance
      to a galaxy based on telescopic colour measurements.
      Decision (Regression) Trees are used when the response
      variable is continuous and they can be fine-tuned by
      a maximum depth parameter. I chose this model because the
      input data doesn't need to be normalised and they handle
      non-linear relationships.

    - To assess the bias and variance of my model, I split the
      data into training, cross-validation and test datasets.
      I used the training dataset to create regression trees for
      a variety of depth settings. I then applied the trained
      models to the cross-validation dataset and plotted
      “learning curves”: training *errors* against
      cross-validation *errors* as a function of the depth
      parameter.

    - I determined the optimal parameter setting by repeatedly
      adjusting the depth based on the learning curves. Initially
      I found the model to be highly biased. I then adjusted the
      depth to higher values, re-checking the curves for high
      variance. (If the depth is too high, the decision tree
      learns the fine details of the training data and over-fits)
      I finally achieved an optimal depth and executed the
      optimised model on the final test dataset.

    - During the work I found the training data was too sparse
      for certain types of galaxies and some colour combinations
      are not-unique, resulting in catastrophic errors.
      I overcame this by sourcing alternative data with more
      sample cases. I successfully optimised my model and my
      results matched those of a published Astronomy paper.

### 03 Statisticians – Presenting and disseminating data effectively

    - I’ve disseminated my work in a variety of ways, including
      written reports and presentations, to both technical and
      non-technical audiences. I wrote a user manual for the
      statistical package I created for the Financial Services
      Survey (FSS) team, ensuring instructions were pitched
      correctly by avoiding jargon and also creating relevant
      diagrams. User feedback was excellent and the manual
      allowed the system to be quickly adopted. I produced
      a graphical tool for the FSS team which allowed large
      amounts of historical data to be quickly visualised,
      increasing FSS efficiency. In addition, customers commented
      on the clarity of a report I wrote on FSS imputation.
      A colleague asked for permission to distribute it further.

    - I am always mindful of accessibility requirements, having
      previously been employed to support visually-impaired
      students with mathematics. When I produced my FSS reports,
      I chose suitable plot types to match the data I was
      analysing. I selected scatter plots to highlight
      relationships and bar charts to compare categories. To aid
      accessibility I marked scales in legible fonts and chose
      accessible colour-schemes for readers who are colour-blind.

    - I gave a live demonstration to colleagues on the latest
      software for data dissemination (“Jupyter” notebooks). The
      team did not have existing knowledge of this tool and the
      aim of the presentation was to encourage its use. I chose
      the live demo format rather than documentation/screenshots
      as I judged it would better engage my colleagues. The demo
      was well received and colleagues expressed an interest in
      using the tool.
