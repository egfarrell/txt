# lesson 01 - Intro to Research Methods

    * _Observational_ Studies (e.g.: scatter plot) Used to
      show relationships only, where we take note of already
      existing data.

    * _Surveys_ - people are the subjects and we ask them
      questions we're interested in the answer to.

    * _Controlled Experiments_: we want to show causation.
      ie. that one particular variable causes another.

    * key ideas:
        - constructs
        - operational definitions
        - controlling for
        - lurking variables
        - population mean
        - sample mean
        - sample error


### when to _trust_ survey results
    Ask yourself:
    * good sample size?        how _many_ people were surveyed?
    * representative sample?  _who_ was surveyed?
    * sound methodology?      _how_ was the survey conducted?

### constructs
    * memory
    * happiness
    * guilt
    * love ...
    - Its very difficult to _define_ and _measure_ these.
    - Many different approaches to measuring a construct.

### how did BBC _measure_ memory?
    Answer: the percent of faces correctly recognised from
    test 1 and 2
    So the _operational definition_ is when the percent of
    faces recognised is higher.

### Which are CONSTRUCTS?
    * Gallons of gasoline?    No. Measured via gallons.
    * Intelligence?           Yes. Measured via IQ test.
    * Effort?                 Yes. Measured via minutes of homework, GPA etc
    * Age?                    Depends. If pure number of years then no, but
                              "maturity" or "wisdom" could be a construct.
    * Hunger?                 Yes. Measured: how often tummy grumbles
    * Annual Salary in USD?   No. Measured: number of dollars

### Constructs:
    So each construct needs an _operational definition_
    which allows measurement. (But there are many possible
    operational definitions.) Then once a construct is
    measured, the data can be analysed.

### Udacity study: does SLEEP influence memory?
    Using the BBC online face test, they identified all
    these factors:
    * time of day test was taken (_important_)
    * your stress level
    * 5-minute breaks
    * your age (_important_)
    * not paying attention

    If we hold one factor _constant_, we would trust the
    test results more. e.g. if everyone took the test at the
    same time of day (in their time-zone)

### lurking variables
    these are extraneous factors that can influence the
    outcome of a test/measurement that we may not have
    thought about.

### Averages
    population parameter:
        average for the whole population: `μ`
    sample statistic:
        average for the sample:  `x̄` (x bar)

### sampling error
    difference between population mean and sample mean
    μ - x̄

### Earliest writing about random
    John Venn (1888): Maybe the best example of the
    scientific meaning of _random distribution_ is given by
    the arrangement of rain drops in a shower. No one can
    give a guess at any instant where the next drop will
    fall, but we know a sheet of paper will gradually become
    uniformly spotted over. And if we mark out any two equal
    areas on the paper, these would gradually tend to be
    struck equally often.

    So? when we have a random sample, each person in the
    population has an _equal chance_ of being selected.

### Surveys
    Advantages:
    * one of the easiest ways to get info on a population
    * fairly inexpensive
    * can be conducted remotely
    * anyone can access and analyse survey results

    Disadvantages:
    * untruthful responses
    * biased responses
    * _response bias_: respondants don't understand the question
    * _non-response bias_: respondants refuse to answer


### Controlled Experiments:
    * Researchers want to test the effects of sleep
      medication.
    * A _random_ sample of people are selected (as opposed
      to a _convenient_ sample)
    * An active or inactive pill (placebo) is randomly
      assigned to each participant. (to have a comparison
      control group to those who took the active pill)
    * The experiment is _double-blind_.

### Factors controlled for:
    * The time participants took the pill - everyone took it
      at the same time.
    * The place where participants slept - the all slept at
      the lab.
    * What the pill looks like - same for everyone.

### Factors _not_ controlled for:
    Gender and age weren't controlled for. However we can
    minimise the impact of these variables on the results.

    Say for example, all women were given the active pill
    and all men the placebo. Then if people who took the
    active pill slept better we wouldn't know if it was
    because of the medication in the pill or maybe women
    just sleep better than men.

    Now in this experiment we're not controlling for the
    * Gender
    * Age
    variables but we can minimise their impact on the
    results by using _random assignment_. This means
    everyone has an equal chance of being assigned to either
    of the 2 treatments. Randomisation works best with
    larger groups.

### within subject design
    - Do 2 tests on the same subject.
    - E.g. have a person do the memory test after a little
      sleep.
    - Then get the same person to do the test after lots of
      sleep.
    - By using the same subject, we keep a lot of factors
      constant.

### Mode: true or false

    The mode can describe any type of data, numerical or
    categorical? (True)

    All scores in the dataset affect the mode? (False)
        _22_ 54 23 _22_ 31 17 _22_

    - Say you deleted 31, then the mode would still be 22!
    - Say you added an extreme value of 1000. The mode would
      still be 22!

    If we take a lot of samples from a population, the mode
    will be the same in each sample. (false)
    This is key. Because the mode changes with each sample,
    we can't use the mode to infer something about the
    population.

    There is an equation for the mode. (False)
    We have to count through various data points, or look
    for the highest bin, but we can't use an equation. Thats
    why the _mean_ is more useful.

# Lesson 05 - Standardizing
---------------------------


### shape of distribution
    What we're interested in, is the _proportion_ of data
    values _less_ than or _greater_ than a certain value in
    the dataset. So if I say my chess rating is 1800 you
    don't know what that means until you see the shape of
    the chess ratings distribution and the proportion thats
    less.

### Relative Frequency distribution
    If we're interested in a proportion, we should first
    convert all absolute frequencies to a relative
    frequency.
    They had a facebook friends graph, with sample size
    n=1000 and various absolute frequencies.
    Then they converted it to relative frequencies,
    basically by dividing by 1000. (because that was the
    sample size)

### Bin size problems
    Ideally they would like the bin size to be as small as
    possible to really pin-point how many facebook friends
    someone has compared to the rest of the distribution.
    But if you decrease the binsize to 1, then you get
    a _uniform distribution_ with either 0 or 1 in each
    bin!!

### predicament
    We want a small binsize to have as much info about data
    points relative to the rest of the distribution, but if
    we have a tiny binsize, we start to lose the _shape_ of
    the distribution.

    But if we have a large binsize, we can't tell the
    proportion less than any data value.

### solution to the predicament
    We use a _theoretical_ model for our distribution that
    follows a smooth curve for relative frequency. The
    smooth _continuous curve_ can be described by an
    equation that allows us to _calculate_ the proportion
    between any two values on the x-axis.

### Normal Curve
    The area of the relative frequency histogram was 1, and
    the area under the theoretical normal distribution is
    also 1.

    In this theoretical model, the mean, median and mode are
    _exactly_ equal. This doesn't normally happen in real
    life.

### Bell curve: standard deviations
    Approximately 68% of the data points lie within one
    standard deviation of the mean. 95% lie within two
    standard deviations.

### z score
    The location of particular points along the x-axis is
    often described in terms of standard deviations. We can
    convert any score along the x-axis into the _number_ of
    standard deviations it is away from the mean. Then if we
    know something is one standard deviation (in the
    positive direction) away from the mean, then we know
    approximately 84% of values are less than that value.
    (50% + (68%/2))

### twitter followers v facebook friends
    the girl has `x` amount of friends but the boy has a lower
    number `y` of twitter followers. Which is more unpopular?
    Rather than looking at the raw numbers, or even each
    number as a percentage of the average, its better to
    look at the number of standard deviations each number is
    away from the mean.
    Thats because the distribution of facebook friends is
    higher and thinner than the wider twitter distribution.

    It works out that the girl is 3.53 SD away from the
    facebook mean and the boy is 2.57 SD away from the
    twitter mean.

    Now _nice point_: when we standardise the distributions
    with zero as the mean (reference point), the girls "dot"
    is further away from the (standardised) mean than the
    boys "dot" is.

### calculating a standardised score
    convert a raw score into a number of standard deviations
    away
    `z = x - μ`
        `-----`
          `σ`

    we can start with any normal distribution and
    standardise it.

### whats a negative z-score mean?
    that the original value (x) is less than the mean.

### z-score distribution: mean
    the new mean will always be _zero_ because we're
    subtracting the mean from every data value which has the
    effect of shifting the entire distribution left, until
    the new mean is at zero. Note: the _shape_ of the
    distribution is not changed.

### z-score distribution: standard deviation
    the new SD of a z distribution will always be 1. Why?
    Well the new mean will be 0.
    So the z-score of σ will be:
    `z = σ - 0`
        `----- = 1`
          `σ`


### proper name
    what I call the "z-score distribution" is called: the
    "standard normal distribution".

### scaling and more scaling
    you can take any distribution, standardise it to the
    standard normal distribution, and then re-scale it to
    another (normal) distribution.

    e.g. you have a facebook friends distribution with
    * μ=190
    * σ=36

    Standardise this, and then re-scale to a new
    "popularity" distribution between 0-100 with:
    * μ=50
    * σ=10

    But for any particular score (data point) on any of the
    3 distributions, you still have the same _proportion_
    less on the graph. So even though everything is
    re-scaled, all proportions stay the same.


# Lesson 06 - Normal Distribution
---------------------------------

### PDF - probability density function
    We know from our normal distribution that if we know
    where on the x-axis a value falls _in terms of standard
    deviations_ we can determine the percentage (proportion)
    less than or greater than any value.

    Remember we use a theoretical curve to model the
    relative frequencies. And the relative frequencies sum
    to 1 so the  the _area under the curve_ sums to one as
    well. Because the theoretical curve is taken/generated
    from the relative frequencies.

### model a curve
    When we model a histogram/frequency table, the absolute
    raw frequencies turn into a relative frequency. And this
    is where probablities start to come in. And these
    modelled curves become PDF's: Probability Density
    Functions. We can visually model statistical concepts.

### Normal Distribution - PDF
    - Because this curve is generated using an _equation_, we can
      use calculus to find the area under the curve. 
    - And once you know the area under the curve, you can start
      to answer questions about what proportion is above/below
      a certain value.

### INTEGER standard deviations
    - using the 68%/95% rule, if a question has an integer number
      of standard deviation, you can quickly calculate lots of
      areas, The splits are:

    -       |       |      |      |        |
    -  2.5% | 13.5% |  34% |  34% |  13.5% |   2.5%
    -       |       |      |      |        |
    -       |       |      |      |        |
    -      _2σ_    _1σ_   _μ_    _1σ_     _2σ_

### NON-INTEGER standard deviations (z-score table!)
    - If you have a non-integer standard deviation value, then
      you can't use all your 68% values etc as above.
    - But no worries. Mathematicians have tabulated the _area_
      under the curve _LESS_ than a particular z-score value.

# Lesson 07 - Sampling Distribution
-----------------------------------

### Normal distribution - comparing single values
    By knowing the mean and SD of a normally distributed
    population we can compare any value in that population
    to the rest of the population by comparing the percent
    less than or percent greater than that _value_.


### Normal distribution - comparing samples
    how can we campre a sample of a particular population to
    other samples from that population?

### important point
    To find out _where_ a value falls in a population, we need
    to know the:
    * mean
    * and standard deviation
    of that pouplation.

### The population
    Population: 1,2,3,4
    Population mean:  2.5

    Pop     Mean    SS      Variance(σ^2)    SD (σ)

    4               2.25

   And the sample standard deviaion, or standard

### key relationship:
    Whats the ratio of the population standard
    deviation to the sample standard error?

     σ      1.118
    ---- =  ----- = 1.414 = sqrt(2)
     SE     0.79

    i.e. the square root of the sample size!

    in other words:
           σ
    SE = ------
         sqrt(n)

    And its the central limit theorem that guarantees this
    equation!!??

### Whats so good about the formula above for SE?
    Now we can find out how the _mean of a sample_ relates
    to the mean of any other sample from the same
    population.

    Because now we can work out the standard deviation of
    our sample. And this standard deviation is called the
    standard error SE.

### note about sample size
    if you choose samples with a size of n=1 then you'll
    simply just re-create the original population.

### Uses of the sampling distribution
    Because we know the sampling distribution of sample
    means we can see where any particular sample mean falls
    along this distribution. Then we can say if this
    particular sample is typical or not.

    The sampling distribution also allows us to work out
    probabilities. E.g. we wanted to see the probably of
    getting an average of at least 3 when rolling two
    tetrahedron dices. Well we built a sampling distribution
    from all samples of size n=2 (see below) and then got
    the area under the curve for anything > 3.

### taking KLOUT samples of 'n=35'
    First they calculated the sampling distribution of all
    samples of n=35 of KLOUT users. How did they
    create this sampling distribution? They assumed:

    * the sampling distribution would be normally
      distributed (from the CLT)
    * that the mean of sample means (M) would be
      the same as the KLOUT population mean which
      they knew already.
    * that they could calculate the standard
      deviation of the sampling distribution of
      the means (the standard error) using the
      formula: SE = σ/sqrt(n)

    Then they took a sample of `n=35` of KLOUT
    users who used the bieber twitter app. They
    found that the mean of this sample was 40. How
    does this compare to other sample means of
    n=35. i.e. where does the value 40 appear on
    the sampling distribution?

    Well they converted the 40 into a z-score and
    then worked out (1 - z-probability). They
    found this had a "low" proability of 0.2...
    i.e. its somewhat unlikey to have selected
    a sample from the whole KLOUT population with
    a mean KLOUT score of 40. So maybe the bieber
    tweeter app is playing a role in increasing
    a persons KLOUT score.

    Then they increased the sample size to 250
    (klout people who also used the bieber app).
    The larger sample size really narrowed the SE
    and gave a much lower probability (p=.0119)
