### Cross-Validation (Machine Learning)
    - One problem with classifiers is that they may not be
      flexible enough to model the input data (under-fitting) or
      may be too flexible and over-fit the data (ie. have too
      many tunable parameters). If the model under-fits the data
      it has high *bias*. Alternatively if it has too many
      tunable parameters it may suffer from high *variance*.

    - In order to quantify the effects of bias and variance
      a training data may be split into 3 parts:

        1. a training set
        2. a cross-validation set
        3. a test set

    - The training set should be about 60% of the samples, and
      the cross-validation and test sets about 20% each. By
      running the classifier on the cross-validation set, the
      meta-parameters of the algorithm can be varied and the best
      values pinpointed by plotting a "learning curve".

    - A learning curve is a plot of both the training and
      cross-validation errors as a function of the number of
      training points. The purpose of learning curves is to
      decide if it is useful to add more training samples.

    - The learning curve for a high bias classifier will show
      both high training errors and cross-validation errors. In
      this case it is not useful to add more training data
      because the classifier is inherently flawed.

    - For a high-variance classifier (which over-fits the data)
      the learning curve will show a low training error (good
      fit) but the cross-validation error will be much higher. If
      more training samples are added the training error will
      continue to rise but the cross-validation error will
      decrease until they meet in the middle. In this case it is
      appropriate to add more training data to fine-tune the
      classifier.



### Simulating the Sampling Distribution

    - In recent years, computational "sampling" methods which use
      modern computing power have become more popular. A recent
      talk called "Statistics for Hackers" has changed the way
      I look at data analysis. These computational methods are
      often more intuitive than traditional analytic methods and
      work even if there is no generating model for the data.

    - The basic premise is that calculating the sampling
      distribution may be hard but simulating the sampling
      distribution is often easy! These methods include:

    * Direct simulation of the sampling distribution
    * Shuffling
    * Bootstrapping

    - An example of simulating the sampling distribution is as
      follows: A coin is tossed 30 times and sees 22 heads. Is
      the coin fair?

    - The traditional approach is to use the binomial probability
      formula to create a binomial distribution. Then work out
      a p-value of 0.008. The hypothesis that the coin is fair is
      rejected at the p < 0.05 level.

    - However a simpler method would be just to simulate the coin
      flips using computer code. For example, construct
      a for-loop and simulate the experiment 10,000 times. Use
      a counter variable to store how many times the number of
      Heads is greater than 22. Dividing the counter variable by
      10,000 will result in 0.008 (approx). This gives the same
      answer as the traditional binomial method.

    - Overall computational sampling methods give another
      approach to statistical analysis and often use fewer
      assumptions (e.g. normally distributed) than traditional
      methods.

survey (FSS) addressing customers’ concerns about the

them about their existing IT skills and used that to pitch
the instructions at the correct level.

Overall the project was a success and the customers
commented on the improved quality of the data as well as the

### HSto Data analysis

Give an example of when you have demonstrated this competency

For my Physics degree, I used *Cross-Validation* to
fine-tune a Decision Tree model which predicts the distance
to a galaxy based on telescopic colour measurements.
Decision (Regression) Trees are used when the response
variable is continuous and they can be fine-tuned by
a maximum depth parameter. I chose this model because the
input data doesn't need to be normalised and they handle
non-linear relationships.

To assess the bias and variance of my model, I split the
data into training, cross-validation and test datasets.
I used the training dataset to create regression trees for
a variety of depth settings. I then applied the trained
models to the cross-validation dataset and plotted “learning
curves”: training *errors* against cross-validation *errors*
as a function of the depth parameter.

I determined the optimal parameter setting by repeatedly
adjusting the depth based on the learning curves. Initially
I found the model to be highly biased. I then adjusted the
depth to higher values, re-checking the curves for high
variance. (If the depth is too high, the decision tree
learns the fine details of the training data and over-fits)
I finally achieved an optimal depth and executed the
optimised model on the final test dataset.

During the work I found the training data was too sparse for
certain types of galaxies and some colour combinations are
not-unique resulting in catastrophic errors. I overcame this
by sourcing alternative data with more sample cases.
I successfully optimised my model and my results matched
those of a published Astronomy paper.
