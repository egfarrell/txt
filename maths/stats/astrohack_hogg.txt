# hogg on "models"
    - for him, a model is never just physics equations, it _also_
      has to refer to hardware (NOISE MODEL). ie your data are
      produced by physics and hardware/detectors (and the
      hardware is also governed by physics at the end of the day)

    - So data analysis is where _theory_ and _engineering_ meet!

    - So if a theorist says I have a model of stars, well you
      don't unless you also include photon counts, calibration
      errors, resolution variation, CCD artifacts, cosmic rays

# Hogg on jackknifing
    - Hes a big believer of using the jack-knife.
    - Whenever you're using an estimator you should jacknife it.
    - This is where you systematically _drop_ some of the data
      and refit.
    - You really get a sense of which parts of the data are
      affecting what. eg. which chunks of the data are really
      setting the slope and which chunks the intercept.
    - It also gives you a sense of when your data goes to hell.
      If dropping a few data-points causes your model to go to
      hell, then you need to take more data or theres something
      really wrong with your data.

# models
    - theres no statistical theory of how to construct models in
      the first place!
    - We construct models basically by investigating the data and
      seeing how the model does against the data points and thats
      how we see if we need to adjust our model.

# Hoggs team has a systematic approach:
    * trying to write science papers by explicitly stating
      assumptions in a bulleted list
    * show that their methods work under those assumptions
    * apply the method to the real data
    * discuss what would have changed if we made different
      assumptions (which is usually the code would have
      taken the age of the universe to run!)

# any time you do data analysis
    * you want to be clear about what your _assumptions_ are
    * you want to be clear that the _method_ you are using
      meets those assumptions

    - and if we find we can't meet our assumptions, then we relax
      some of the assumptions rather than putting ad-hoc changes
      into the method.

    Hogg asked how to fit a line through these measurements
    (which have _error bars_)

    Note: theres `N` data points

get the equation of the best fit line
    `y   = mx + b`
    `ỹ_n = m*x_n + b`

# Traditional method:
    - _Chi Squared_ method, like a kind of Euclidean distance
      metric. Physicists like it because its a dimensionless
      number.


            N
    `chi^2 = ∑    (y_n - ỹ_n) / (σ\_n)^2`
            n=1

    - Note 1: this is an "objective function"?

    - Note 2: the `(σ_n)^2` means that points with larger errors
      are given less weight in the chi-squared statistic


# assumptions

    - Note: most of these assumptions are trying to make the
      problem tractable!

    * _Gaussianity_: the error bars are Gaussian. Each point is
      somehow sampling away from its true value with some kind of
      Gaussianity

    * assuming _known error variances_. (or known 'noise'
      variances). The chi-squared assumes you know the variances
      `(σ_n)^2` of those Gaussian's. ie. that the variances are
      correct. But these errors are never correct 'for very deep
      reasons'! But you are assuming that you do.

    * you're assuming theres _no bad data_. You're assuming every
      data point is 'the same' in the sense that the only
      difference between the data points is they have different
      variances

    * _mixture_: the data were actually generated from a mixture
      of two different lines, and some of the data came from one
      line and some data from the other.

    * im assuming _INDEPENDENCE_. why? because I'm summing
      a separate term for every data point, and none of the data
      points refer to the other data points, which means the
      noise is added independently to every data point. So theres
      an independence going here that the sum `∑` represents.

    * What if the terms _weren't independent_? Then the summation
      thing for the chi-squared would become a matrix and it
      would be like the dot-product of 2 vectors. (so you have to
      switch up to linear algebra if the points become
      non-independent)

    * _negligible_ `x` errors. We only have to worry if the `y`
      values meet the predictions. We never ask if the `x` values
      meet the predictions. 
        - If you think the `x` values are also noisy things get
          much more complicated and you don't have as simple
          a solution. (but actually this is often the case
          really. When did you ever take data where you knew the
          x-axis exactly?) Hogg:
            > the only case I know is the Kepler data where the
            > x-axis is the spacecraft clock, which really does
            > have negligible uncertainty. But thats a rare
            > case...

    * model appropriateness assumption. We're assuming the
      data are roughly drawee from a _straight line_. (and
      assuming that theres zero scatter in the line, or the
      line has no width)

    * "estimator" meta-assumption: we're assuming all we
      need is an _estimator_. ie. an estimator is good
      enough. We're assuming all you need is to know the
      slope and the intercept, and you don't need to know
      a more complicated set of information like the full
      posterior information about the slope and the
      intercept. or the full shape of the likelihood
      function. We only care about minimising the scatter and
      getting the answer out.

    * assuming there is _no prior_ information of value about `m`
      and `b`. You're assuming all that matters is "this
      likelihood" (the chi-squared formula above)
        - ie. theres no other information you want to add in like
          the slope `m` is more likely to be 1 than any other
          value
        - or if the slope got very large it would mean the
          universe was only 100 yrs old and we wouldn't be here
          etc etc.
        - There might be other things you know that are relevant
          but you're somehow assuming theres no such
          information...
        - and thats probably false. Its rarely true.


# Next part of lecture (linear least square fitting)
    - re-wrote the whole chi-squared thing as linear algebra with
      2 vectors, and hence into a "design matrix"

    - end up with a _column vector of differences_ between the
      prediciton and the data


    But because you have a big matrix the whole thing is
    more flexible than the chi-squared formula... you can
    have different values for the off-diagonal elements of
    the matrix

    "this is like an object that happens in physics or
    geometry":
    `[ỹ- AΘ]^T C^(-1) [y - AΘ]`

    above is a geometric model that says what is the _distance_
    between the data and my model?

    Its written just like a _metric distance_ (squared
    distances) in special relativity.

    Also its very easy to take the derivative of this
    (_calculus_ on linear algebra operators) and this allows
    you find the optimum of this (wherever the derivative
    vanishes)

    The _best_ (min) value is just:

    Θ\_(best)  = `[A^(T) C^(-1) A]^(-1)  [A^(T) C^(-1) ỹ]`
                 ---------------------   ----------------
                    2 x 2                   2 vector
                    (positive               (takes the data
                    definite                and projects it
                    "shear"                 down to a 2 vector)
                    tensor)

                            "a projection and a shear"

    * so its projecting your data down to 2 statistics of
      the data and and then shearing them into your answer.

    * the estimator above gives the best `m` and best `b`
      (in a column vector?)

                Θ\_(best) = `[m_best]`
                            `[b_best]`

    `[A^(T) C^(-1) A]^(-1)` is the covariant matrix for the
    answer (ie it fully describes the posterior probability
    for the problem, only true in this case for the
    assumptions listed above. not true generally)

    `[A^(T) C^(-1) A]^(-1) = [σm^2  σmb  ]`
                            `[σmb   σb^2 ]`

    Notice that this object proports to be the _uncertainty_
    but it makes no reference to the data! Theres no `y` in
    there. How can we know the uncertainty in our answer
    without looking at the data?

    - But I strongly recommend you use _jacknife_ or _bootstrap_
      to get uncertainties on the slope and the intercept because
      these methods do make reference to the data and do account
      partially for the fact that the data are not perfectly
      generated by our model.
    - (easy to implement, good wikipedia pages, give an empirical
      estimate of your uncertainties given your data are not
      drawen your model)

# when are we happy with _only_ an estimator?

    * _computationally "impossible"_: sometimes you need to
      make a measurement many many times! Hogg and
      Foreman-Mackey were finding single transits in kepler
      data. They had to perform an enormous number of
      hypothesis tests, for every location of a transit, at
      any time, in any star we had to ask "yes or no?" So we
      needed a yes/no estimator because we couldn't do
      a full bayesian inference for every hypothesis you
      could make about the planet.

    * _initialise_: maybe you want to make a non-bayesian
      estimator to get yourself _started_ on a baysian
      inference.

    * if your posterior pdf is very _simple_ ie. your data
      is very informative, much more infromative than your
      prior beliefs, and negligible error on your
      estimates, then your esitmate is as good as any
      posterior. In fact, there are proofs that as your data
      become infromative the baysian inference and the
      asymtotically efficient estimator from frequentism
      actually converge! So when your data are very
      informative youre wasting your time sampling.

    * create _summary statistic_ or (lossy) _data
      compression_: as you go forward with your analysis,
      maybe all you need from the scatterplot of x's and y's
      is just the slope. So you just carry forward the
      _slope_. So the estimator (and an uncertainty on your
      estimator) are sufficent to carry forward your
      posterior beliefs.

    * Decison making: "Go" or "No Go" decisions -> remember
      decisions are always estimators. Theres no
      probabilistic decisions. (you could do fully bayesian
      decision making but it requires knowing things about
      your utility that are very hard to know, so often
      a well designed estimator beats any kind of bayesian
      decision theory you can pull off)
