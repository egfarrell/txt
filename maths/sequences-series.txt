
### "Finite Support Sequence"
    - This is a sequence (a list of numbers, for example) where
      most of the values are `zero`, except for a finite number
      of `non-zero` entries.

    - For example, in the sequence (0, 3, 0, 0, 5, 0, 0, 0, ...),
    - the "support" consists of the positions with the 3 and 5.
    - The support is finite because only two values are non-zero.

    - The word "support" in this context refers to the positions
      (or indices) in a sequence where the values are NON-ZERO.
    - In mathematics, "support" generally means the "part that
      holds something up" or the "active region."
        - For a sequence or function, it means the places where
          something is happening (i.e., where the values are
          non-zero). So, the support of a sequence is simply the
          set of indices where the sequence has non-zero values.
    - When we say a sequence has finite support, it means that
      the sequence is "supported" or "active" in only a finite
      number of places, and elsewhere it just "vanishes" (has
      zeros).
        - The zeros don't contribute to the sequence's "support,"
          so they're like the parts that don't hold any weight.


### GPT: what's a SEQUENCE of PARTIAL SUMS?
    - What is a Sequence?
        - A _sequence_ is an ordered list of numbers. 
        - e.g., the sequence `1, 2, 3, 4, 5` is a simple sequence
          where each number follows the previous one in a regular
          pattern.

    - What is a Series?
        - A _series_ is the sum of the terms of a sequence. For
          example, if we have the sequence 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, then
          the series would be 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5.

    - What is a Partial Sum?
        - A _partial sum_ of a series is the sum of the first `n`
          terms of that series.

    - Example:
        1. Consider a Sequence:
            - Let's take 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, ...

        2. Form the Series:
            - The resulting series from this sequence is:
            - 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + ...

        3. Calculate Partial Sums:
            - The _first partial sum_  : sum of first term:        `S_1`                     = 1
            - The _second partial sum_ : sum of first two terms:   `S_2` = 1 + 2             = 3
            - The _third partial sum_  : sum of first three terms: `S_3` = 1 + 2 + 3         = 6
            - The _fourth partial sum_ : sum of first four terms:  `S_4` = 1 + 2 + 3 + 4     = 10
            - The _fifth partial sum_  : sum of first five terms:  `S_5` = 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 = 15

        - So, the sequence of partial sums for this series is:
            - 1, 3, 6, 10, 15, ...

    - General Definition
        - In general, if we have a sequence `a_1`, `a_2`, `a_3`,
          ..., `a_n`, then the `n-th partial sum`, denoted as
          `S_n`, is defined as:
            - S_n = a_1 + a_2 + a_3 + ... + a_n.

    - Why are Partial Sums Important?
        - Partial sums are useful because they help us understand
          the series behavior, especially when dealing with
          infinite series.
        - By examining the sequence of partial sums, we can
          determine if a series 
            - _converges_ (approaches a specific value) or 
            - _diverges_ (increases without bound).
        - So, a sequence of partial sums is simply the sequence
          you get when you keep adding the next term of the
          original sequence to the sum of all previous terms.
            - This helps in analyzing the properties of the
              series formed by the original sequence.

### comparison test

Most useful test.

Its actually a fancy wrapped-up comparison with the geometric series.

Remember with a geometric series:
    - if `r < 1` then it converges
    - if `r > 1` diverges
    - if `r = 1` diverges

The setup:

- write lim n--> inf, Abs(An+1/An)
- and look at what kind of ratio pops out.

- Theres 3 possible outcomes:
    - r < 1, converges
    - r > 1, diverges
    - r = 1, Doesnt tell us anything, waste of time! 
            - Try something else (but not root test).

- Little hint:
    - Always use Ratio test when factorials involved: `n!`
    - Also when exponentials are there e.g. 3^n

- Examples:
    - When you have
        - something n+1/something n,
    - then group like terms together.

    He got ln(n)/ln(n+1). He embedded this discrete "n" into
    a continuous variable "x" and used l'Hopitals test to
    get the limit which is one.

- Comments:
    - If you get a ratio `r > 1` then maybe you should have used
      the Nth-term test.


### integral test

- Not a test for integrals! A test for series....

- When Useful
- Use when you've a power of `1/n` and a `ln(n)` somewhere.
- e.g. Sum 2 to infinity, `1/n_ln(n)`

- Setup:
- Basically convert discrete summation to an integral from 2 to infinity.
- Need one condition satisfied: that the integrand is decreasing.

- This integral diverges by the `p-test`. (Dont know what the p-test is)
- Therefore if the integral diverges, the original discrete series diverges.

- Remarks:
- What was the point of converting to an integral?
- Because you could do a substitution (Let t = ln(x)) and then evaluate.
- Theres no substitution you can make for the original series.

So if you dont do (or need) a substitution theres no point doing this integral test.
(Because series have their own p-test and comparison test)

Formal statement:
If f is a continuous decreasing function, and youve a series f(n), from n=a to infinity,
then the series and the integral either BOTH converge or BOTH diverge.


Remark:
How do you know `f` is decreasing. Differentiate it and see if its negative.
Something about series at infinity a bit like improper integrals at infinity.

Also he drew a nice diagram with the discrete series as a load of
boxes, and then the area under the continuous function which is
the integral. The integral is actually smaller than the series
but if the integral converges so does the series.



### root test

lim n--> inf, Abs(An)^1/n


Theres 3 possible outcomes (Just like _ratio test_!):

_ `r < 1`, converges

_ `r > 1`, diverges

_ `r = 1`, Doesnt tell us anything, waste of time! Try
  something else (but not ratio test).


### Comments:
    Use this test when nth root is easy to compute. This
    root test is really based on looking at a geometric
    series `r^n` and take the nth root which leaves the
    ratio `r`. (and we know the story with r)
