### ACTIVE and PASSIVE transformations
    - $MY_IMAGES/physics/active-passive-vector-transformations.JPG
    - Equation (3.31) is called an `active transformation`, since
      we use the matrix `A` to change one vector into another,
      namely `e i 0` into `e i` .
        - Note that in (3.31) all vectors are expressed in the
          same basis.
    - Equation (3.24a), on the other hand, is called a `passive
      transformation`, since we use the matrix `A` not to change
      the vector `v` but rather to change the basis which v is
      referred to, hence changing its components. All this is
      summarized in Table 3.1.
    - In most physics texts the notation isnt as explicit as
      ours; one usually sees matrix equations like `r' = Ar`
      (3.32) for both passive and active transformations, and one
      must rely on context to figure out how to interpret the
      equation.
    - In the active case, one considers the coordinate system
      fixed and interprets the matrix `A` as taking the physical
      vector `r` into a new vector `r'`, where the components of
      both are expressed in the same coordinatesystem, just as in
      (3.31).
    - In the passive case, the physical vector `r` doesn’t change
      but the basis does, so one interprets the matrix `A` as
      taking the components of `r` in the old coordinate system
      and giving back the components of the same vector `r` in
      the new (primed) coordinate system, just as in (3.24a).

### Youtube Video explaining Co/contra variant tensors
    - Covariant and contravariant basis vectors can be understood
      via vector analysis.  It's possible to have a basis of
      vectors in 3D space that isnt orthonormal:
        - theyre not orthogonal to each other, nor of length one.
    - From this basis, you can create a new set of basis vectors.
      They are called RECIPROCAL (dual) basis vectors.
        - Create the new set of RECIPROCAL vectors taking the
          cross product of any two original basis vectors and
          dividing by the scalar triple product.
    - If `a1`, `a2` and `a3` are the original vectors, then the
      new vectors `b1`, `b2` and `b3` are defined by equations:
      `b3 = (a1 X  a2) / [a1, a2, a3]`
    - Cross product:         `a1 X a2`
    - Scalar Triple Product: `[a1,a2,a3]`
    - These types of vectors appear naturally in crystallography.
    - The original vectors `a_i` and the new vectors `b_j`
      contain the essence of covariant and contravariant vectors.
      In fact, they are covariant and contravariant vectors for
      3 dimensions.
    - If you take the dot product between `a_i` and `b_j` you get
      a Kronecker delta.
        - (so they're ortogonal to each other?)
    - This idea of forming an additional vector system by cross
      product does not exist in higher dimensions.
        - Cross products only exist in 3D.
        - (Well it also exists _IN 7 DIMENSIONS_ !! but I have
          never heard of an application for these 7 dimensional
          spaces).
    - Even though in higher dimensions, you cannot create a new
      set of vectors from an original set using a cross product,
      you CAN still create this new set of vectors.
    - The idea is to take the inverse of the covariant metric
      tensor.
        - The _INVERSE_ of the covariant metric tensor is the
          contravariant metric tensor.
    - The inner product of the CONTRAvariant metric tensor and
      any COvariant basis vector equals a CONTRAvariant basis
      vector.
    - contravariant METRIC tensor = Inverse(covariant metric
      tensor)
    - contravariant BASIS vector =  inner product(contravariant
      metric tensor, covariant basis vector)
    - Note: the contravariant metric tensor is the dot product
      between contravariant basis vectors.

### Flournoy contravariant vectors
    - A spacetime vector has 3 parts:
        1.  `ds`   the "true" vector
        2.  `X^μ`  the components
        3.  `ê_μ`  the basis

    - To keep `ds` the same if you change the components `X^μ`
      (e.g. clockwise rotation) then the basis vectors `ê_μ` must
      change the opposite way (anti-clockwise)

    - extra: any vector can be expressed as coefficients in
      a linear combination of the basis vectors.
      - (so contravariant COMPONENTS are actually
        COEFFICIENTS in a linear combination)

    - extra Flournoy point: he started talking about "real"
      spacetime vectors with coordinate displacement components,
    - but then progressed to more "abstract" vectors (e.g.
      velocity) where the x-component DOESNT represenst
      a coordinate displacement in spacetime but instead the
      velocity in the `x` direction.
        - He also gave the electric field `E_x, E_y ...`
          components as an example of a more "abstract" vector.

### Tensor / Vector
    - YES! finally good explanation of contravariant components
    - Physics Videos by Eugene Khutoryansky
    - https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CliW7kSxxWU
    - ### CONTRAVARIANT components:
        - We typically describe a vector by _how many_ basis
          vectors we _add_ together to produce it.
        - If the basis vectors _increase_ in length, then the
          component values _decrease_ (contra-vary)
        - cos you need less of the basis vectors to cover the
          same distance.
        - (These are a `linear combination` of basis vectors)
    - ### COVARIANT components:
        - Vector components generated by _dot-product_ with basis
          vectors.
        - If the basis vectors _increase_ in magnitude, the
          components _increase_ too (co-vary). ie dot product
          values increase
        - (these are a `projection` onto the basis vectors)

      vectors to scalars. It is not actually a vector(!), but an
