### The real use of Linear Algebra?
    - Hubbard: linear algebra is more a convenient language for
      multivariate calculus than a subject in its own right(!)

### Require linear functions
    - For multivariate calculus, the strongest requirement we can
      make is that the functions should be linear.
    - Roughly speaking a function is linear if when you double
      the input, the output is doubled too.
    - The reason he starts talking about linear functions is
      because we have to impose restrictions on the functions we
      consider before any theory of multivariate calculus goes
      ahead.

### Linear approximations
    - M. Hubbard: at the end of chapter 1 we are going to talk about
      how to approximate a non-linear function by a linear function,
      specifically by its higher dimensional derivative.
    - So how will the derivative do this approximation?
    - The derivative really is a linearization of a function at
      a specific point.

### Directional derivatives
    - On Hubbard, 5th edition, page 129, a great explanation of
      directional derivatives.
    - Even better than Bachmann, who gave the mechanics of it in
      his calculus book.
    - So the directional derivatives are a generalization of
      partial derivatives.
    - The partial derivative describes how a function `f(x)`
      varies as the variable `x` moves from point `a` in the
      direction of a basis vector `e_i`.
    - However, the directional derivative describes how
      a function `f(x)` varies when the variable moves in any
      direction `v-hat`.
    - i.e. when the variable `x` moves at a constant speed from
      `a` to `a + v-hat` in time one.
    - That's a key thing. We're just assuming in time one, at
      a speed of one.


### Vectors: Magnitude AND direction?
    - Hubbard (5th edition, around page 34) disagrees with the
      idea that vectors always have a `magnitude` and
      a `direction`.
    - He says that focuses on the wrong distinction, suggesting
      that some quantities are always represented by vectors and
      others never are, and that it takes more information to
      specify a quantity with direction than one without.
    - For example, the volume of a balloon is a single number,
      but so is the vector expressing the difference in volume
      between an inflated balloon and one that has popped.
    - So that's just a one number vector.
    - The first is a number in the space in `R`, the space of
      real numbers, and the second is a vector in `R`.
    - The height of a child is a single number, but so is the
      vector expressing how much he or she has grown since their
      last birthday.
    - A temperature can have a magnitude, as in it cut down to
      minus 20 last night, but it can also have a magnitude and
      direction, as in it is 10 degrees colder today than
      yesterday.
    - Nor can static information always be expressed by a single
      number.
    - The state of the stock market at a given instant requires
      one number for each stock listed, as does the vector
      describing the change in the stock market from one day to
      another.
     
### Point or Vector?
    - An element in `R^n` is an ordered list of numbers, whether
      it is interpreted as a point or as a vector.
    - But we have very different images of points and vectors.
    - In linear algebra you should think of the elements of `R^n`
      as vectors. However, differential calculus is all about
      INCREMENTS to points.
    - It is because these INCREMENTS are vectors that linear
      algebra is a prerequisite for multivariate calculus.
    - It provides the right language and tools for discussing
      these increments.
    - So really the whole thing so far has been this idea of
      static data, static points and incremental data, going from
      one state to another, which is represented by a vector.
