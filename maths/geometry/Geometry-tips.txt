### Metric Spaces
    - Metric Spaces are often the first introduction to the more
      abstract ideas from topology.
    - A metric space is a mathematical set along with an
      associated `function` of two points within the set that
      defines a sense of "distance" or "metric" between them.
    - This idea of distance within a set allows interesting
      properties to be discussed such as openness, closedness,
      completeness, connectedness as well as varying forms of
      continuity of functions between metric spaces.
    - These ideas build on concepts from Real Analysis, including
      sequences, series and convergence (albeit in higher
      dimensional settings).

### Metric Space (PCM) .... Distance
    - What properties do we want for a sensible definition of
      distance? A metric space is an abstract notion that
      addresses this...
    - Let `X` be a set of "points". Given any two of these
      points, `x` and `y`, suppose there's a way of assigning
      a real number `d(x,y)` that could be regarded as the
      distance between them.
    - 3 properties are desirable for a 'distance' idea:
        - (P1)
            - `d(x,y) >= 0`
            - with equality iff x = y
            - distance always positive except when points equal
              (equal points, then d=0)
        - (P2)
            - `d(x,y) = d(y,x)`
            - for any two points x and y
            - distance is a symmetric notion.
            - From x to y, same as from y to x
        - (P3)
            - `d(x,y) + d(y,z) >= d(x,z)`
            - for any x, y, z
            - triangle inequality:
            - length of any single side never exceeds the sum of
              the other two sides
    - A function `d` defined on pairs of points `(x,y)` from
      a set `X` is called a metric if it has properties (P1) to
      (P3).
        - (hmmm.. the "metric" is a `FUNCTION` of points)
    - In that case, `X` and `d` together form a METRIC SPACE.
        - (ie the set + function)
    - This abstraction of the notion of distance is very useful.


### Eccentricity
    - Comes from Latin *ex-* (out of, from) and *centrum* (center):
    - Originally used in astronomy to describe orbits
      "off-center" from Earth.
    - Applied to conics in the 17th century as a measure of
      deviation from a circle (circle: center = focus).
    - Conics
        - circle:    `e = 0`     no deviation
        - ellipse:   `0 < e < 1` slightly off-center
        - parabola:  `e = 1`     boundary case
        - hyperbola: `e > 1`     focus further from the directrix → strongly off-center
    - So eccentricity reflects how "off-center" the conic is
      relative to a circle.


### Different geometries
    - $MY_NOTES/physics/metaphysics/saylor/Newtons-Laser-Sword.txt
    - Bolyai tried to deduce a contradiction.
        - He assumed that through a point, parallel to a given
          line, _many lines could be drawn_.
        - He deduced like crazy but failed to find any
          contradictions, eventually realising he had invented
          a new geometry, different from Euclid’s but just as
          respectable.
    - Riemann went the other way.
        - He assumed that through a point, parallel to a given
          line, _no line could be drawn_.
        - He realised that he too had invented another geometry:
          the geometry of `great circles` on a sphere.

### Preptorial review: The Geometry of Physics (Frankel)
    - $MY_BOOKS/Physics/Spacetime/GR-Prep-Frankel-Geometry-In-Physics.pdf
    - "Best of Class & Only Modern Text in Genre"
    - Some texts enhance understanding, while others aid
      practical computation, making them excellent for
      self-study.
    - In this edition, Frankel integrates a 34-page preface using
      Cartan's exterior differential forms and a "metal torsion"
      example applied to Cauchy's stress tensor.
    - Frankel covers a broad range of physics, from classical to
      quantum, excluding string theory but including numerous
      gauge applications in 750 pages filled with detailed
      calculations.
    - Despite claims of accessibility for grad students or
      advanced undergrads, I recommend advanced calculus
      (analysis) and analytic geometry for self-study or
      reference.
    - The book assumes knowledge of analytic geometry, focusing
      on its applications in physics and engineering.
    - This text demonstrates the relevance of geometric
      approaches in physics, even in _non-physical geometries_
      like vector bundles.
    - The "introduction" refers to applications and calculations,
      not basic understanding, requiring prior knowledge of
      analytic geometry.
    - The author explains advanced applications of analytic
      geometry but assumes a solid base in both analytic geometry
      and physics.
    - The text includes numerous diagrams and illustrations,
      enhancing the computational focus with didactic elements.
    - Illustrations provide "aha" moments, and the author
      explains the reasoning behind formulas for deeper
      understanding.
    - For instance, explanations of proportionality constants
      help generalize lessons beyond rote memorization.
    - Highly recommended with the caveat to brush up on analytic
      and differential geometry beforehand.

### Poor Man's Tensors
    - https://justincfeng.github.io/
    - Simply put, a tensor is a mathematical construction that
      “eats” a bunch of vectors, and “spits out” a `scalar`.
        - But is that the same as a differential form?
    - The central principle of tensor analysis lies in the
      simple,  trivial fact that scalars are unaffected by
      coordinate transformations.
    - From this trivial fact, one may obtain the main result of
      tensor analysis: an equation written in tensor form is
      valid in any coordinate system.
    - In my attempt to summarize tensor analysis in a single
      paragraph, I have left out many important details.
        - For instance, the definition for tensors in the first
          sentence is an incomplete one;
        - It leaves out the fact that tensors are `linear maps`,
          as well as the fact that tensors also “eat” other
          objects called `dual vectors`.

### affine geometry
    - No notion of DISTANCE measurement in the affine plane.
    - No notion of ANGLE MEASUREMENT either.
    - No ORIGIN.
    - Geometry of parallels. No notion of perpendicular!
    - Two pairs of parallel lines create a parallelogram.
        - Affine grid plane.

    - Then draw diagonals through the parallelogram. These
      construct a sequence of horizontal and vertical lines, all
      EQUALLY separated. So a plane of parallelograms, each one
      translated from the first one using only the notion of
      parallel lines.
