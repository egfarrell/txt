### Abstract
    - There are a wide variety of vector formalisms currently
      utilized in engineering and physics. 
    - E.g., Gibbs' three-vectors, Minkowski four-vectors, complex
      spinors in QM, quaternions for rigid body rotations and
      vectors defined in Clifford geometric algebra. 
    - With such a range of vector formalisms in use, it thus
      appears that there is as yet no general agreement on
      a vector formalism suitable for science as a whole. 
    - This is surprising, because one of the primary goals of
      19th century science was to suitably describe vectors in 3D
      space. 
    - This situation results in the unfortunate consequence of
      fragmenting knowledge across many disciplines, and
      requiring much time and effort to learn various formalisms. 
    - We thus historically review the development of our various
      vector systems and conclude that `Clifford's multivectors`
      best fulfills the goal of describing vectorial quantities
      in 3D and providing a unified vector system for science.

    - But it transpires that quaternions are NOT suitable to
      describe Cartesian vectors in 3 dimensions.
        - Why not? 
        - It turns out complex numbers & quaternions describe the
          _ROTATIONAL ALGEBRA_ of 3D spaces!
            - Complex numbers: so(2)
            - Quaternions: su(2)

    - Two parallel developments
        - Hamilton -> Gibbs, Heaviside
        - Grassman -> Clifford
        - $MY_IMAGES/maths/Clifford-Vectors/Vec-War-04.jpg

    - But... what is wrong with quaternions?
        - Prob 1: 
            - basis vectors square to MINUS 1 
            - So squaring a vector will give the negative of
              a Pythagorean length
            - so kinetic energy would be negative!
        - Prob 2:
            - all `basis vectors` anti-commute in Hamilton's
              quaternion system
            - therefore all operations with quaternions are
              anti-commutative
        - Prob 3:
            - instead of a simple 3-component Gibbs vector, we
              have to add a fourth scalar component 
            - (apparently unneccessary)
        - $MY_IMAGES/maths/Clifford-Vectors/Vec-War-05.jpg

    - After all those probs, what's the solution? 
        - Well generalise the quaternions... 
            - ... while retaining the quaternions to be used as
              a sub-algebra for rotations
        - Cliffords description of space:
            - Basis vectors: `e1`, `e2`, `e3`
            - He defines _positive square_ for them (so that
              they're suitable as Cartesian vectors)
            - Innovation: 
                - allows the BASIS ELEMENTS to be combined, such
                  as `bivectors` and `trivectors`
            - The bivectors 
                - square to `-1` and are isomorphic to the
                  quaternion basis vectors `i,j,k`
            - The one trivector 
                - is a _commuting_ quantity and is a natural
                  replacement for the UNIT IMAGINARY!
            - $MY_IMAGES/maths/Clifford-Vectors/Vec-War-06.jpg

    - Mulivector
        - Combine all of Cliffords basis vectors to form
          a general object called the `multi-vector`
        - Using bold symbols `v` and `w` to represent 3-component
          vectors, and `j = e1e2e3`, the whole thing can be
          simplified as `M` the multivector (with 8 components)
            - `M = a + v + jw + jb`
            - $MY_IMAGES/maths/Clifford-Vectors/Vec-War-07.jpg

    - All systems contained in `M` (multivector)
        - Multivector:  `M = a + v + jw + jb`
        - Quaternion:   `a + jw` 
        - Gibbs vector: `v` 
        - Complext numbers: both bivectors (e.g. `e1e2`) and
          trivectors `e1e2e3` square to `-1` and can represent
          the unit imaginary, and hence complex numbers!
