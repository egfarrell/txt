# Most interesting point of lecture
    - Stillwell implies Pythagoras theorem is more of
      a _definition_ that is baked into the Cartesian system.

    - Wildeberger disagrees. He says Pythagoras is not
      automatically true and is still a _theorem_. It is only
      automatically true when two sides of the triangle are
      parallel to the co-ordinate axes.

    - Otherwise, when you get the length of the 3 sides oblique
      to the co-ord axes, you must still prove that the square of
      one is equal to the sum of the other two squared.

# Pythagoras Theorem
	- (Euclid Proposition 47/48)
	- Babylonians and Egyptians knew of it - e.g. knotted rope
	- Greeks thought of theorem in terms of AREA, not lengths.
	- A notion of length IS NOT REQUIRED.

# Theorem
    - A triangle is right-angled <=> (precisely when) the sum of
      the area of squares on the shorter sides equal the area of
      the square on the longer side (hypotenuse)

# Comment
    - Area is a simpler notion than length - no square roots!
    - To define length we do need square roots - (thats
      a consequence of the Pythagoras theorem)
    - e.g. In Cartesian system:
        - length |AB| = sqrt(2^2 + 1^2)
        -             = sqrt(5)
        - and sqrt(5) is hard to calculate!

### Differential Geometry
    - combination of calculus and analytic geometry applied to
      curves and surfaces
    - key concept: idea of _curavature_
    - Huygens: invented pendulum clock (much better timekeeping)

    - PLANAR CURVES:
    - --------------
    - 1693: Huygens investigated the involute of a curve
        - $MY_IMAGES/maths/involute-of-parabola.jpg
    - involute: tightly unwrapping a string
    - you can start unwrapping from different points, so you end
      up with many involutes: `C'`, `C''`
        - $MY_IMAGES/maths/wild-diff-geom-01.jpg
    - Huygens proved all involutes are disjoint
    - He proved all involutes are perpendicular to the tangent of
      the curve at that point
    - DUAL NOTION: the _evolute_ of a curve.
    - First 2 things must be defined:

        1. CENTRE OF CURVATURE: have a tangent point `P` and
           another point `Q` right beside it. Draw a normal
           through `P` and a normal through `Q`. Where the
           normals intersect is the centre of curvature.
            - Its where adjacent normals to a tangent meet.

        2. OSCULATING CIRCLE:
            - 'osculum'         = Latin for "little kiss"
            - osculating circle = "kissing circle"
            - have a tangent point `P` and a point either side
              `R` and `Q`. Then these 3 points define a circle as
              the get closer together.
            - its the best circle that approxmates the curve at
              the point `P`
            - note: the centre of curvature at `P` is also the
              centre of the osculating circle.
            - $MY_IMAGES/maths/wild-diff-geom-02.jpg

        - note: the _radius_ of the osculating circle `ρ`  is
          called the radius of curvature.

        - note: the curvature at `P` is `1/ρ`. So the bigger the
          radius is, the smaller the curvature.
          - its often given the letter `k`

    - Now the EVOLUTE: its the path traced out by the centre of
      curvature as you vary the tanget point `P`
        - ie is the locus (trajectory) of the centres of
          curvature.

    - note: the evolute of an involute is the original curve!
        - $MY_IMAGES/maths/wild-diff-geom-03.jpg

    - involutes come up with cycloids. and the involute of
      a cycloid is another cycloid.

    - catenary
    - tractrix: (involute of catenary)
    - tractrix: taking a rock for a walk!
    - catenary: (evolute of a tractrix!)
    - evolute of a parabola is: semi-cubical parabola
    - $MY_IMAGES/maths/wild-diff-geom-04.jpg

    - SPACE CURVES
    - ------------
    - Key people: Clairout, Euler, Cauchy
    - osculating plane at point `P`. You've got 3 key directions:
        - Tangent
        - Principal Normal
            - (lies in the osculating plane, perpendicular to the
              tangent)
        - Binormal:
            - sticks out of the osculating plane, perpendicular
              to both the tangent and the principal normal.

    - SURFACES
    - --------
    - Euler: 1760
    - surface: z = f(x, y)
    - Euler: "I know the curvature of a curve, but what about the
      curvature of a surface?". Well I need to slice the surface
      until I get a curve.
    - How to "slice"  the surface. Well erect a plane that
      contains the normal. And where this normal plane intersects
      the surface, you get a planar cross-section curve traced
      out.
    - as we rotate this normal plane around, we calculate the
      curvature `k(θ)`. And we'll find theres two critical
      (principle) curvatures, the biggest and the smallest. `k1`
      and `k2`
    - signed curvatures.
        - positive curvature
        - negative curvature
        - Get a screenshot of him showing the two curvatures with
          his hand.

    - Now Gauss enters the scene:
        - 1755 - 1855 (proved FTA)
    - Theorem Egregium:
        - `k1 k2 = K` is determinable from the surface itself!
        - an ant with a ruler and protractor could NOT figure out
          `k1` and `k2` individually but it could measure `K`
        - Aside: how does the ant know its on a curved surface?
            - Maybe by working out the area of a circle on the
              surface. the area might not equal `πr^2` so the ant
              knows it's not on a flat surface.
            - $MY_IMAGES/maths/wild-diff-geom-04.jpg
        - Big `K` is called the 'Gaussian curvature' at a point.
        - For a sphere, the curvature is constant at every point
          and the radious is `ρ`.
            - so `k1 = 1/ρ`
            -    `k2 = 1/ρ`
            - and `K = 1/ρ^2`
        - There's actually another object with constant
          curvature: the 'pseudo-sphere'! Its a tractrix
          revolved. It has constant negative curvature.
          - The pseudo-sphere is an important model for
            hyperbolic geometry later on.
