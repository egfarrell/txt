This is a great fragment, and Hermann is being very characteristic here: enthusiastic, historically aware, and also a bit polemical. I’ll separate what is fairly solid history from interpretation, and I’ll flag where I’m making educated guesses rather than reporting a settled consensus.

––––––––––––––––

1. Who (or which group) had the fibre-bundle / derivations insight?
   ––––––––––––––––

There are really two closely related but distinct insights mixed together in your fragment.

A. Tangent vectors as derivations

The idea that a tangent vector at a point p is a derivation v: C∞(U) → R satisfying the Leibniz rule v(fg) = f(p)v(g) + g(p)v(f) crystallized in the late 1930s–1940s.

Key figures and lineage:
• Élie Cartan laid much of the conceptual groundwork earlier, though not in modern algebraic language.
• André Weil and the Bourbaki circle pushed the algebraic viewpoint hard in the 1930s–40s.
• Jean Dieudonné and Bourbaki texts normalized the derivation-based definition.
• Claude Chevalley helped transmit these ideas into Lie theory and differential geometry.

So the “insight” is not due to a single person. It is best seen as a Bourbaki-era synthesis: reinterpreting classical geometric objects as algebraic ones over rings of functions. Hermann is right that, conceptually, this is a very deep simplification.

B. Fibre bundles as the natural global language

The modern theory of fibre bundles was developed slightly later, roughly 1935–1955, and here the attribution is much clearer:

• Hassler Whitney introduced fibre bundles in a topological setting (1935).
• Heinz Hopf influenced the homotopy-theoretic viewpoint.
• Norman Steenrod systematized the subject; his book Topology of Fiber Bundles (1951) is exactly the “treasure-trove” Hermann mentions.
• Charles Ehresmann provided the decisive conceptual leap for differential geometry: connections, principal bundles, associated bundles, and a clean global formulation of Cartan’s ideas.

Ehresmann is the real hero of Hermann’s narrative. His connection theory (late 1940s–early 1950s) unified Cartan’s moving frames, parallel transport, curvature, and Lie groups in a way that is recognizably modern.

––––––––––––––––
2. Why did this have the “disastrous side effect” of cutting off communication?
––––––––––––––––

This is not hyperbole, but it is one-sided. Several overlapping factors caused the rupture.

A. Language and level of abstraction

The derivation viewpoint and fibre bundles replaced concrete coordinates with:
• rings of functions,
• sheaves,
• functors,
• universal properties.

Physicists (and many geometers) were trained to think in coordinates, components, and explicit formulas. The new language often made simple things look forbiddingly abstract.

From a physicist’s point of view in, say, 1950:
“Velocity = derivation on a local ring” felt like unnecessary rebranding of something already understood.

B. Bourbaki’s cultural dominance

The Bourbaki style deliberately prioritized:
• structural clarity over calculational utility,
• intrinsic definitions over examples,
• existence theorems over explicit constructions.

This created a sociological divide. Many mathematicians who were not aligned with Bourbaki felt excluded, and physicists felt outright ignored.

Hermann was very sensitive to this. He admired the ideas but disliked the loss of dialogue.

C. Cartan’s ideas became harder, not easier, to learn

Ironically, the fibre-bundle formalism clarified Cartan’s work at a deep level but raised the entry barrier enormously.

To understand “what curvature is” in Ehresmann’s sense, one suddenly needed:
• manifolds,
• Lie groups,
• principal bundles,
• connections as horizontal distributions.

For physicists used to Riemannian geometry via tensors, this felt like an alien world.

D. Timing: physics wasn’t ready yet

Gauge theory in physics only fully embraced fibre bundles in the 1970s (Yang–Mills, instantons, anomalies).

So in the 1940s–50s:
• mathematicians built a powerful formalism,
• physicists didn’t yet have physical problems that forced them to adopt it.

The result was decades of parallel development with little interaction.

––––––––––––––––
A balanced caveat
––––––––––––––––

Hermann’s complaint is emotionally true but historically incomplete.

The same ideas he says “cut off communication” later:
• revolutionized gauge theory,
• clarified global issues in GR,
• made anomalies, monopoles, and topological phases intelligible.

So the “disaster” was temporary. It was a failure of translation, pedagogy, and timing—not of the ideas themselves.
