### What is a representation?
    - https://www.math.columbia.edu/~woit/wordpress/?p=15155#comments
    - Gavin:	
        - One of the things I found confusing studying group
          theory was how mathematicians would define
          a `representation` of a group in terms of the
          `matrices` that represent the group elements, while
          physicists define a representation in terms of the
          `vectors` in the vector space. 
            - ie, “the electron is a spinor rep of the Lorentz
              group” instead of something like “the electron is
              a spinor in the spinor rep of the Lorentz group.” 
        - After I realized that’s what was going on, it made
          sense, since mathematicians are typically more
          interested in the group itself, whereas physicists are
          typically interested in particles/fields which
          transform under group transformations. 
        - But the lack of precision made it confusing to learn.
        - Over the years I found a lot of physics notation is
          like that: optimized for doing calculations but often
          not for pedagogical clarity or conceptual precision.
    - Peter Woit:	
        - Gavin, the confusion is inherent in the fact that
          a representation is a `vector space` together with
          `linear maps` on the vector space. 
        - If you want to be more _concise_, depending on the
          context you might just specify the `vector space` (when
          it’s clear what the representation is). 
        - Not just physicists do this, mathematicians too. 
        - Main difference is that sometimes physicists are hazy
          about what a representation is, and more likely to use
          ambiguous and confusing language to refer to it.


### What is a Lie group
    - $MY_NOTES/physics/books/curry-book-review.txt
    - It also has 3 chapters devoted to Lie Groups and Lie
      Algebras. Most elementary treatments describe Lie Groups as
      "Groups of Matrices." Yes, that is how they are often
      realized, but that misses the point.
    - A Lie group is a group which is also a `manifold`(!) 
    - And a Lie algebra is the set of all left invariant vector
      fields on the Lie group. 
    - They are connected by the `exponential map`, which sends
      a vector in the `Lie algebra` to a point along a path
      tangent to that vector down in the group. 
    - What is good about the way Professor Curry approaches this
      subject is that is mature, yet very accessible to beginning
      students.


### Rickles tips: Lie Groups
    - Any book on group theory will explain this basic idea;
      a good choice for physics applications is Chris Isham's: 
        - Lectures on Groups and Vector Spaces for Physicists. 
    - The Galilean group is strictly speaking a `Lie group` since
      the various parameters are _continuous_. 
    - e.g. A finite rotation of a planet would be generated (or
      'built up') by the accumulation of lots of infinitesimal
      rotations. 
    - A useful book on Lie groups, for those who know vectors and
      matrices, is Harriet Pollatsek's: 
        - Lie Groups: A Problem-Oriented Introduction via Matrix
          Groups.


### What is a group?
    - Algebraists say its a _set_ with two _operations_
      satisfying a load of easily-forgettable axioms.

    - Naturally this provokes a protest: why would any sensible
      person need such pairs of operations? "Oh, curse this
      maths" says the student (who, possibly becomes Minister for
      Science in the future).

    - Dont start with the concept of a group. We get a totally
      different insight if we start with the concept of
      a _transformation_ (a one-to-one mapping of a set onto
      itself) as it was historically.

    - A _collection_ of transformations of a set is called
      a GROUP if:

        1.	along with any two transformations, it contains the
        result of their _consecutive_ application

        2.	and an _inverse_ transformation along with every
        transformation.

    - That's all there is. The so-called "axioms" are in fact
      just (obvious) properties of _groups of transformations_.

    - What axiomatisators call "abstract groups" are just groups
      of transformations of sets considered up to _isomorphisms_
      (which are one-to-one mappings preserving the operations).

    - As Cayley proved, there are no "more abstract" groups in
      the world. So why do the algebraists keep on tormenting
      students with the abstract definition?
