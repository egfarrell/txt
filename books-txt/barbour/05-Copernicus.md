### 5.1 How Copernicus came to make his discovery

- It would be an amusing exercise for historians to list worthy
  scientists born too early to receive the Nobel Prize. Who
  should be awarded the first of these posthumous Nobels for
  physics? Given the topic of this chapter one might expect
  Copernicus to be nominated. I will argue the stronger case can
  be made for Ptolemy.

- The reasons become clearer if one imagines a citation along
  these lines: "For the introduction of the equant and the
  important stimulus this gave to the correct solution of the
  planetary problem by Copernicus and Kepler, and to the
  development of the dynamical conception of motion by Kepler and
  Newton."

Ptolemy introduced the equant as a rather ad hoc device when all
the standard methods of his time failed to reconcile theory with
observation. He himself apologized for this radical innovation,
saying he was compelled "to make some basic assumptions which we
arrived at not from some readily apparent principle, but from
a long period of trial and application."

Readers familiar with early quantum theory will notice a striking
parallel with Planck's introduction of quantization for emission
and absorption. Both Ptolemy and Planck were conservative
thinkers who reluctantly adopted an unusual device because
observations forced them to. Neither could have made their
discoveries without a deep immersion in the detailed experimental
and theoretical problems; in both cases the existing theories
contained substantial truth but had been pushed to their limits.

Both men appended an extraneous, incongruous device to principles
of motion that had centuries of successful use. The addition
seemed a desperate last resort to fit stubborn facts into the old
mould, yet in both cases these devices were the harbingers of
a new theoretical dispensation.

Although Ptolemy and Planck were similar in how they arrived at
their respective devices, what happened next differed sharply.
Planck's tentative step was followed within a few years by rapid
advances from Einstein and Bohr. By contrast, Ptolemy's device
was only corrected many centuries later, and in a roundabout way,
by a conservative and pedantic scholar who rediscovered
— unwittingly — much of the geometric craft accumulated by
Hellenistic astronomers. This scholar was primarily trying to
remove the equant.

Copernicus (1473–1543) could not tolerate the idea that celestial
bodies move nonuniformly. He admired Ptolemy highly, calling him
a master of the art, but Ptolemy's equant and its implied
nonuniform motion troubled him. In his early Commentariolus,
circulated in manuscript well before De Revolutionibus was
printed in 1543, Copernicus wrote that because of the equant "a
planet moved with uniform velocity neither on its deferent nor
about the center of its epicycle," and that this seemed "neither
sufficiently absolute nor sufficiently pleasing to the mind."

He went on to say that, having noticed these defects, he often
considered whether a more reasonable arrangement of circles might
be found "from which every apparent inequality would be derived
and in which everything would move uniformly about its proper
center, as the rule of absolute motion requires." After long
effort he eventually found a solution that used fewer and simpler
constructions than earlier models.

In the preface to De Revolutionibus, addressed to Pope Paul III,
Copernicus described how he came to entertain the mobility of the
Earth. He said he reread all the philosophical works he could
obtain to see whether anyone had proposed motions of the spheres
different from the school teachers' views. He found in Cicero
that Hicetas supposed the Earth to move, and in Plutarch that
others had that opinion. Encouraged by these reports, Copernicus
began to consider the Earth's motion himself. He acknowledged
that the idea seemed absurd at first, but argued that since
earlier astronomers were allowed to imagine arbitrary circles to
explain phenomena, he too ought to be permitted to investigate
whether a more convincing explanation of celestial revolutions
could be found by assuming some motion of the Earth.

From our earlier discussion (Chapter 3) the planetary problem
reduces to interpreting two inequalities in the observed motions:

- the first inequality corresponds, in a heliocentric frame, to
  the proper motion of an individual planet around the Sun;
- the second inequality corresponds to the Earth's motion around
  the Sun.

Ptolemy represented the second inequality (for superior planets)
by perfectly uniform epicyclic motion. He represented the first
inequality by eccentric deferents plus the ad hoc equant, which
required nonuniform motion of the deferent's guiding point.

Copernicus set out to cure what he saw as a defect — the equant
— in the theory of the first inequality. He also found an
interpretation of the second inequality in terms of Earth's
motion around the Sun, and he realized the great value of that
interpretation. He exploited its geometric and trigonometric
consequences thoroughly, but he did not go beyond those
implications for planetary theory.

Most of Copernicus's work remained focused on removing the
equant. He seems to have thought that his reinterpretation of the
second inequality as terrestrial motion was itself progress
toward solving the first inequality; in fact, it left the first
inequality essentially unchanged. Copernicus did not
significantly advance planetary theory beyond providing an
elegant reinterpretation: the bulk of De Revolutionibus is
devoted to attempts — sometimes ingenious — to undo the equant.

This preoccupation is reminiscent of the Venerable Bede's
complaint about Irish Christians: good people, but they celebrate
Easter on a noncanonical date. Like Bede, Copernicus could not
stop returning to a single perceived fault. Only about a fifth of
De Revolutionibus concerns the core Copernican revolution; much
of the rest is devoted to eliminating the equant.

Another puzzling aspect of Copernicus's work is that many of the
technical devices he used to eliminate Ptolemy's violations of
the ancient astronomical ideal — that every celestial motion
should be uniform circular motion or composed of uniform circular
components — had been anticipated in remarkable detail by Islamic
astronomers of the Maragha school. That school flourished near
Maragha under the leadership of Nasir al-Din al-Tusi (1201–1274).
Two centuries before Copernicus, members of that school developed
methods to eliminate the equant's nonuniformity. A closely
related later astronomer, Ibn al-Shatir (1304–1376), produced
a lunar model that matched Ptolemy's lunar longitudes but greatly
reduced the predicted variation in the Moon's apparent diameter
— a conspicuous flaw in Ptolemy's model. Copernicus's lunar model
is, aside from parameter differences, essentially identical to
Ibn al-Shatir's.

These coincidences naturally raise the suspicion that Copernicus
knew of the earlier Islamic work, even though De Revolutionibus
contains no acknowledgement. Equally, no direct evidence has so
far emerged proving a transmission route of Maragha-school
results to Renaissance Europe. This question has been discussed
by several modern scholars.

To conclude this section with some historical detail: although
Arabic astronomers contributed substantially to later astronomy
and some of that influence appears in De Revolutionibus (openly
in the case of solar theory elements, or perhaps indirectly in
technical devices resembling Maragha methods), the solid
foundation of Copernicus's work is still Ptolemy's Almagest. The
transmission of the Almagest to Western Europe was therefore
crucial for the Copernican revolution. Significant European
astronomy began with translations of astronomical texts,
including the Almagest, in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries.
A decisive development came in the mid-fifteenth century with
Georg Peurbach (1423–1461) and his student Johannes Müller of
Königsberg (Regiomontanus, 1436–1476). Peurbach wrote a popular
work, Theoricae novae planetarum (1454), and began an exposition
of the Almagest, the Epitome, which Regiomontanus completed. The
Epitome was published in 1496 and was the book Copernicus often
followed, sometimes even in preference to the Almagest itself.
Regiomontanus's clear presentation of Ptolemaic astronomy
effectively revived the Almagest and prepared the ground for
Copernicus. In particular, Regiomontanus pointed out the
possibility of treating all planets uniformly, avoiding the
epicycle–deferent inversion used by Ptolemy for the inner planets
— an observation that Swerdlow and Neugebauer regard as important
preparation for Copernicus's innovation.

Many readers know the outline of Copernicus's life. He was born
at Torun in eastern Poland and is generally regarded as Polish,
though national identities in that region and period are complex.
He studied in Kraków from 1491 for several years and was then
sent by his uncle, a powerful bishop, to the University of
Bologna. In Italy he studied Greek, mathematics, law, and
medicine (he later practised medicine among other duties). He
visited Rome in 1500, studied for almost four years at Padua, and
obtained a degree at Ferrara. He returned to Poland in 1503 and
served his uncle until the bishop's death in 1512. He then
settled permanently at Frauenburg (Frombork) in Warmia on the
Baltic coast and worked until his death as a canon of the
Catholic Church, carrying out many ecclesiastical and civic
functions, some of them difficult.

His interest in astronomy began in Kraków, where he acquired
several astronomical treatises, including the Alfonsine Tables.
The exact date when he hit upon the idea that transformed
cosmology is unknown; it certainly predated 1515 and may have
been as early as 1510. Soon after conceiving the idea he wrote
the Commentariolus, a brief outline of his scheme. Realizing that
a convincing presentation—especially an alternative to the
equant—required careful revision of Ptolemy's work and
redetermination of orbital elements, he left the Commentariolus
unpublished (though it circulated in manuscript) and embarked on
an ambitious programme of observations and calculations that
occupied him almost until his death. Copernicus later noted that
he had thought about his idea for many years.

Knowledge of his proposal spread, and Georg Joachim Rheticus, an
energetic young mathematician and pupil, was attracted to
Frauenburg. Rheticus and others persuaded a hesitant
Copernicus—whose main fear was ridicule, and whose calculations
were still sometimes unsatisfactory—to publish. Rheticus
published the Narratio Prima (1540), an early account of
Copernican theory, with Copernicus's approval. Copernicus died
just as De Revolutionibus was published; tradition says he was
handed a copy on his deathbed.

It is surprisingly delicate to explain precisely what Copernicus
proposed, so we will attack the problem in stages.

### 5.2 What Copernicus did: first approximation

A curious feature of the Copernican revolution is that it did not
occur at the historical stage where, in hindsight, one might have
expected it: namely, when the epicycle–deferent technique had
been developed. That technique, already familiar in the time of
Apollonius or earlier, gives an exact representation of the
zero-eccentricity behaviour of the planets. Ptolemy's account
makes clear that Hipparchus at least knew it captured the broad
planetary motions.

Because the simple epicycle–deferent theory (without Ptolemy's
later modifications) is the most straightforward starting point
for a transition to a heliocentric view, one might reasonably
expect Hipparchus to have proposed heliocentrism. Instead,
Ptolemy's discovery of the equant — and the problems it
highlighted — is what ultimately moved Copernicus. Why did
Hipparchus not make the leap?

Ptolemy reports that Hipparchus was aware the simple
epicycle–deferent theory was inadequate for exact planetary
descriptions. Hipparchus knew one had to handle complicated
effects such as the nonconstant sizes of retrograde loops and the
variability corresponding to the first inequality. These "messy"
aspects offered no hint of the attractions of heliocentrism; if
anything they made a heliocentric cosmology less appealing, since
it then became clear the Sun could not sit exactly at the centres
of planetary orbits. Whether Hipparchus ever seriously considered
Aristarchus's heliocentric suggestion is unknown; if he did, he
may have rejected it as not matching observations. In short, both
Hipparchus and Ptolemy may have been held back from heliocentrism
by knowing too much — a failure to see the wood for the trees.
This same difficulty explains why it is hard to state succinctly
what Copernicus actually accomplished.

To isolate the mathematical core of Copernicus's contribution, we
revert to the state of knowledge represented by Apollonius's
theorem: the pure epicycle–deferent model corresponding to zero
eccentricity and zero inclination. In this simplified picture the
Sun is assumed to lie at the common centre of concentric,
coplanar circular planetary orbits. Consider the Copernican
explanation for one of the outer planets.

Everyone knows that Copernicus proposed the Earth rotates about
its axis and simultaneously revolves around the Sun (he also
postulated a third motion to account for precession of the
equinoxes, treated awkwardly; Kepler later showed that is
unnecessary and we will not consider it here). But the
revolutionary core of Copernicus's idea was a simple, nontrivial
mathematical insight. The Greeks represented the motion of an
outer planet in a first approximation by putting the planet on
one end of an epicyclic spoke; the other end of that spoke moves
uniformly around a deferent circle whose centre contains the
observer. Copernicus's key insight was that if the fixed stars
are sufficiently distant, the apparent motions on the sky
produced by that epicycle–deferent arrangement are identical to
the motions observed if both Earth and the planet move in
circular orbits of different radii about a common centre.

A crucial requirement for this equivalence is that the ratio of
the epicycle radius to the deferent radius equals the ratio of
the Earth's orbital radius to the planet's orbital radius;
otherwise the two schemes are not mathematically equivalent.
Likewise, the rotation periods must match. This geometric
equivalence is illustrated in Fig. 5.1. Stripped of the messy
real-world complications, these insights are the essence of the
Copernican revolution: an alternative geometric explanation, as
precise as the ancients', for nonuniform apparent planetary
motions, especially retrograde motions.

It is important to stress that the success of this explanation
does not depend on the Sun being at the exact centre of the two
circles in the Copernican arrangement. For the explanation of the
observed planetary motions, the central point O in the figure
could be empty. Thus, the Copernican system is less
"heliocentric" in spirit than commonly supposed; it is more
properly a theory of Earth's mobility. Copernicus himself
anticipated this: he wrote that all planetary phenomena can be
explained "if the motions of the other planets are correlated
with the orbiting of the Earth." The somewhat ambivalent role of
the Sun in the system will become clearer later.

We can see how nontrivial Copernicus's insight is by comparing it
with the two simpler phenomena his Earth motions could explain.
First, the apparent daily rotation of the stars can be explained
either by rotation of the celestial sphere or by rotation of the
Earth. This idea — that the Earth rotates to produce the diurnal
motion — had been suggested by Heraclides and discussed by
Aristotle and Ptolemy; it also seems to have been common in
medieval discussion. Second, the apparent annual motion of the
Sun along the ecliptic can be explained if the Earth moves in
a circle around the Sun. Archimedes' account suggests Aristarchus
recognized that Earth's rotation and revolution could explain the
apparent motions of stars and the Sun. But there is no clear
evidence that Aristarchus possessed Copernicus's further insight:
that Earth's orbital motion can also explain the retrograde
motions of the planets. That additional insight is what makes
Copernicus's proposal scientifically valuable.

Putting the Earth into a daily spin merely exchanges one motion
for another; replacing a moving Sun with a moving Earth is
largely kinematic relativity. The marginal advantage, as noted by
Buridan and Oresme, is that a slower motion of a smaller body
(the Earth) is more economical than a rapid motion of the vast
heavens. But the real breakthrough is that the same Earth motion
explains both the apparent motions of the Sun and stars and,
without additional mechanisms, all planetary retrograde motions.
This simultaneous explanation is the first sophisticated
exploitation of kinematic relativity and is what lifts
Copernicus's idea from a trivial coordinate change to a genuine
scientific advance.

That first great insight led directly to a second important
consequence. Copernicus realized that if one component of each
planet's observed nonuniform motion (the second inequality) is
due to Earth's motion, then the radii of the planets' circular
orbits about the Sun can be determined from observed motions and
expressed relative to the Earth's orbital radius. As noted above,
the equivalence requires that the epicycle/deferent ratio be
reproduced by the ratio of the two orbital radii in the
Copernican scheme. It followed immediately that Mars, Jupiter,
and Saturn must lie outside Earth's orbit (in that order), while
Mercury and Venus must have orbits inside Earth's, with Mercury
closest to the Sun.

Thus, the aesthetic guesswork that guided Ptolemy's ordering of
celestial bodies was replaced by a rigorous deduction. The single
assumption that the second inequality in the observations of the
five naked-eye planets reflects Earth's motion around the Sun led
to an unambiguous ordering of the planets and fixed all relative
distances except for an overall scale factor, the radius of
Earth's orbit. The Copernican arrangement is shown in Fig. 5.2.

So far Copernicus's steps were mathematical. He found a striking
confirmation when he considered the consequences of his
assumption combined with observations. Ptolemy's system left the
Earth at rest and produced a heterogeneous picture: Mercury and
Venus forming one class, the Sun standing alone, and Mars,
Jupiter, and Saturn another class. In Copernicus's view the Sun
(known to be much larger than the Earth) could be treated as
essentially at rest while six planets shared the same basic
motion — simple circular motion about a common centre. The
epicycle–deferent inversion disappeared, as did the oddity that
the Sun showed only one motion while the planets displayed two.
Even more impressive was the clear correlation Copernicus found
between planetary distances and orbital speeds: Mercury was
fastest and nearest the Sun, then Venus, then Earth, and so on
— a qualitative anticipation of Kepler's Third Law.

This recognition of an organic, ordered structure gave Copernicus
great confidence. He criticized preceding astronomers for failing
to deduce "the structure of the universe and the true symmetry of
its parts," comparing their efforts to assembling detached,
well-depicted parts of a body that do not belong together,
producing a monster rather than a man. Ptolemy had accurately
modelled individual planetary phenomena, but he failed to unify
them; Copernicus supplied the missing link and knitted the
planetary system into a symmetric whole.

Kepler later emphasized the difference: the old Ptolemaic school
treated each planet separately, identifying causes for each
orbit, whereas the Copernican approach compared planets and
derived common features of their motion from a single cause — the
motion of the Earth. By exploiting Earth's mobility, Copernicus
answered the great late-Renaissance question, "What is the form
of the world, and where are its parts?" His confident answers in
De Revolutionibus helped win adherents at a time when
astronomical matters were widely debated.

One further technical point he might have made in the quoted
passage — and probably did not because it is a little technical
— concerns the phase of the epicyclic motion in the Ptolemaic
system for the outer planets and the phase of the deferent motion
for the inner planets. Why did those motions always march exactly
in step with the motion of the sun? In principle, the epicyclic
spoke could rotate at any constant rate as the epicycle moves
around the deferent, so why did it always turn at precisely the
rate of the mean sun and, moreover, point exactly toward it?
Copernicus explained this immediately: in his alternative
mathematical model for the retrograde loops, provided the sun is
close to the centre of the planetary system, the apparent
epicyclic motions follow directly from the earth’s motion about
the sun. What looked like a remarkable coincidence in the older
representation becomes a necessary consequence in the new one.
The epicyclic motions of the planets, and in particular their
retrograde loops, are revealed as parallax effects caused by the
motion of the earth around the sun. Since a single orbital motion
of the earth produces all these apparent epicyclic motions, it is
natural that they should be mutually in step and also in step
with the sun.

Let us conclude this section by asking again: did Aristarchus
possess Copernicus’s deeper insight into the origin of the
retrograde loops? It is instructive to compare what happened to
Aristarchus’s lost heliocentric treatise with the fate of
Copernicus’s Commentariolus, which circulated in manuscript.
Aristarchus’s work was read by the greatest mathematician of
antiquity, who was himself an active astronomer, yet Archimedes
apparently remarked only a pedantic quibble about it; other
commentators said even less. By contrast, Copernicus’s
Commentariolus caused enough of a stir that, in 1536, a cardinal
in Rome wrote to Copernicus urging him warmly to publish. The
cardinal’s summary of the heliocentric system, received
second-hand, is considerably more detailed than Archimedes’
first-hand report of Aristarchus. The cardinal wrote: I had
learned that you had not merely mastered the discoveries of the
ancient astronomers uncommonly well but had also formulated a new
cosmology. In it you maintain that the earth moves; that the sun
occupies the lowest, and thus the central, place in the universe;
that the eighth heaven remains perpetually motionless and fixed;
and that, together with the elements included in its sphere, the
moon, situated between the heavens of Mars and Venus, revolves
around the sun in the period of a year. I have also learned that
you have written an exposition of this whole system of astronomy,
and have computed the planetary motions and set them down in
tables, to the greatest admiration of all. (Copernicus reproduced
this letter in full before the preface to De Revolutionibus.)

The cardinal’s prominent mention of the planets, and the
comparative lack of such detail in the surviving accounts of
Aristarchus’s proposal, supports the conclusion that Copernicus
was likely the first person in history to realize — and to
persuade others — that a heliocentric cosmology could explain not
only the apparent motion of the sun but simultaneously the
principal anomaly in the apparent motion of all five planets.

### 5.3. Kinematic relativity in De Revolutionibus

Because his model relied on a nontrivial application of kinematic
relativity and because it had such dramatic consequences, the
Copernican revolution put the relativity of motion squarely into
public discussion in a way it had not been before. Although
Copernicus was not the very first to call attention to the
relativity of motion — several medieval writers such as Buridan
and Oresme anticipated parts of it — Copernicus’s statements were
the first important pronouncements since De Revolutionibus became
a seminal book for the later development of modern dynamics. The
question of relativity was unavoidable for him: his whole
argument for the earth’s mobility depended on it.

Copernicus invoked what may be called kinematic or optical
relativity. Commenting on the apparently nonuniform motion of the
planets, he first notes that their distances from the earth must
be assumed to vary, and then he warns that we must scrutinize the
relation of the earth to the heavens carefully, lest in examining
distant objects we misunderstand things that are nearest to us.
He writes that it is necessary to examine the earth–heavens
relation "lest, in our desire to examine the loftiest objects, we
remain ignorant of things nearest to us, and by the same error
attribute to the celestial bodies what belongs to the earth."

He then states the principle on which he argues that motion of
the earth is at least conceivable as an explanation of celestial
phenomena, however absurd it may seem: Every observed change of
place is caused by a motion of either the observed object or the
observer, or by unequal displacements of each. When two things
move with equal speed in the same direction, their relative
motion is not perceived. The earth, however, is the point from
which the celestial ballet is seen repeatedly before our eyes.
Therefore, if any motion is ascribed to the earth, the same
motion will appear in all things outside it but in the opposite
direction, as though they were moving past it.

This passage is worth reflecting on. Copernicus taught us
something about the earth by looking in the opposite direction:
he persuaded people that the earth moves by making them
re-examine the heavens. In his view, the most important evidence
could appear where we least expect it.

Later Copernicus reinforces this approach with an illustration
that would become ubiquitous in physics and relativity
discussions: when a ship moves steadily along, sailors see that
motion reflected in everything outside the ship yet often suppose
themselves at rest, together with everything on board. In the
same way, the earth’s motion can produce the impression that the
entire universe is rotating. On this basis he asks, "Why should
we not admit, with regard to the daily rotation, that the
appearance is in the heavens and the reality in the earth?" He
compares the situation to Virgil’s image of sailing out of
a harbour while "the land and the cities slip backward."

As Copernicus used it, the principle of relativity is purely
kinematic. There are fewer hints of the dynamical formulation
that Galileo later developed. Copernicus simply notes that if two
bodies share the same motion, each will appear at rest relative
to the other. With one important exception (the elaboration of
which occupied much of Galileo’s work), Copernicus did not ask
how two objects — for example, sailors and the ship — can come to
share a common motion. That question has two aspects: a kinematic
aspect (how does relative motion appear) and a dynamical aspect
(what property of nature makes it possible for the ship, its
contents, and the sailors to be carried together with no internal
sign of motion?). The dynamical question is the deeper one. Most
early discussion of Copernicus’s proposal accepted the existence
of such a state of affairs and concentrated on the observable
consequences of it. Kinematic relativity addresses how we can
find evidence in external phenomena for the motion of a ship — or
for the earth, which serves as the ship’s simile — when internal
evidence for motion is lacking. The emergence of the dynamical
element of the relativity principle, whose nascent form we saw in
Oresme, is tied to a growing understanding of the nature of
motion; that development occupies a large part of the histories
of dynamics that follow.

### 5.4. The significance of Copernicus's discovery

Copernicus’s most important insight was that, if the earth moves
around the sun, the diameter of the earth’s orbit provides
a trigonometric baseline. With that baseline the peculiar
retrograde motions of the superior planets are explained as
parallax effects arising from the earth’s motion.

Much of early astronomy advanced through the development of new
trigonometric techniques; Copernicus’s contribution was the most
important of these. By exploiting the earth’s orbital diameter as
a baseline, he effectively introduced an astronomical scale of
distance, the concept of the astronomical unit (the mean
earth–sun distance). About a century before Galileo pointed
a telescope at the heavens, Copernicus had realized that the
distance to the stars must be far greater than Ptolemy had
supposed — larger than Ptolemy’s estimate by a factor comparable
to the ratio of the earth–sun distance to the earth’s radius.
Even though Copernicus himself adopted too small a value for the
astronomical unit, his arguments increased the inferred distances
to the fixed stars by at least two or three thousand times.
Copernicus wrote: Yet none of these phenomena appears in the
fixed stars. This proves their immense height, which makes even
the sphere of the annual motion, or its reflection, vanish from
before our eyes. For every visible object has some measure of
distance beyond which it is no longer seen, as is demonstrated in
optics. From Saturn, the highest of the planets, to the sphere of
the fixed stars there is an additional gap of the largest size.

Although Copernicus retained much of an essentially Aristotelian
cosmology, his dramatic enlargement of its scale helped set the
stage for later notions of infinite space. In his diagrams the
interval between Saturn and the fixed stars is described as
"immense and even like the infinite." This shows how practical
astronomical considerations, in particular the trigonometric
viewpoint, began to displace older philosophical spatial
concepts.

In his own work Copernicus could not exploit this extension of
trigonometry much further. He could not point to many independent
confirmations of his distance scale or an ordering of the planets
beyond the qualitative agreement later summarized by Kepler’s
Third Law. The decisive confirmation arrived with Galileo’s
telescopic discoveries about seventy years after Copernicus’s
death. The phases of the planets seen through the telescope, most
notably the phases of Venus, provided strong confirmation of the
distances Copernicus had postulated and demonstrated beyond
reasonable doubt that Venus orbited the sun. Kepler later
illustrated how these phases arise for an earth-based observer.
There was now a second, independent way of determining
(qualitatively) planetary distances, and the two methods agreed.

What is interesting is that this significance of Galileo’s
observations was largely lost in the wider controversy about
whether the earth actually moves. Galileo rightly believed that
these observations confirmed Copernicus’s predictions and
therefore supported the earth’s mobility. But they were also
compatible with a version of the Ptolemaic scheme in which the
free parameters are chosen so that the deferents of Mercury and
Venus equal the earth–sun distance and the deferents of the
superior planets equal their distances from the sun. In that
arrangement — essentially the system later proposed by Tycho
Brahe — the sun circles the earth while the planets circle the
sun. As an observational scheme, the Tychonic model is
kinematically identical to the Copernican one except in its
relation to the distant stars. Initially it proved more popular
with many astronomers and with non-astronomers, because it
avoided the dynamical problems associated with a moving earth and
the much larger cosmos implied by Copernicus.

In short, Galileo’s observations confirmed the geometrical
relationships deduced by Copernicus; his opponents pointed out
that they did not by themselves force acceptance of a moving
earth rather than a suitably chosen geocentric model.
Nevertheless, no one seriously doubted that Copernicus’s distance
relationships were essentially correct.

This advance raises a recurring problem in the philosophy of
science related to the absolute/relative debate: to what extent
do physical theories give a true picture of reality? The issue is
particularly pertinent here because De Revolutionibus attracted
an anonymous foreword added by Andreas Osiander, who oversaw the
book’s final publication. Fearing controversy, Osiander claimed
the book should be read as a computational device rather than
a description of reality. He wrote that it is the astronomer’s
duty to devise hypotheses that enable the motions to be computed
correctly, and that such hypotheses "need not be true nor even
probable; if they provide a calculus consistent with the
observations, that alone is enough." This disclaimer must have
puzzled many readers, because Copernicus himself expressed belief
in the actual mobility of the earth throughout his work.

There has always been a substantial minority of philosophers and
scientists who are sceptical about bold ontological claims by
physical theories. Given that physics has undergone several major
revolutions in a few centuries — each changing our conception of
the world dramatically — some scepticism is natural and prudent.
Even now, centuries after Galileo’s trial, it is still subtle to
assert categorically whether the earth moves and, if so, in
precisely what sense. The basic standpoint adopted here is that
correct insights about interconnections among phenomena often
suggest broader concepts of the world that go beyond the
immediate empirical facts. These concepts may later be seen as
distortions of a deeper truth, even though they have been
extremely fruitful in revealing further objective
interconnections. Good theories contain substantial truth content
even if they are not final: their worth is measured by their
predictive power. Ptolemy’s theory, for example, had high
predictive content for appearances of the heavens as seen from
Earth; the dramatic advance of Copernicus was to extend the
ability to predict appearances from any point in the solar system
(in principle, the whole universe). This is why the Copernican
idea mattered so much: it allowed one to predict the sky as seen
from locations other than Earth, a capability that proved crucial
for later developments.

### 5.5. What Copernicus did: second approximation

Copernicus’s single great original idea produced
a near-miraculous simplification of the Ptolemaic system: at one
stroke it eliminated many of the Ptolemaic epicycles and provided
an elegant explanation for their appearance. It also gave the
planetary system a much greater coherence and symmetry. The
special inversion of epicycle and deferent used for the two inner
planets was removed; all five planets, together with the earth,
had similar orbital descriptions. Several puzzling features of
the old system found simple explanations in the new scheme. Above
all, the scales of observed retrogressions became directly linked
to rotational periods and distances from the system’s centre, and
the precise alignment of epicycle vectors with the sun’s
direction was naturally explained.

Despite these advantages, the final Copernican system is, to the
modern eye, surprisingly messy and contains incongruities. To see
why, we must understand the principles Copernicus insisted on and
the mathematical tools he could use. Certain features of the
solar system — notably the specific eccentricities of the six
planetary orbits — together with the type of observations
Copernicus inherited and made, constrained him to the particular
scheme presented in De Revolutionibus.

Copernicus was a purist with one inviolable principle: the
ancient rule that every celestial motion must be uniform circular
motion or a compound of uniform circular motions. For him this
was a rigorous law of motion. Expressed in modern vector
language, this principle meant modelling the motion of each
celestial body as the endpoint of a chain of rigid vectors, each
of fixed length and each rotating uniformly about the previous
vector’s end. The initial point of the chain is the observer’s
position; the last vector’s tip locates the celestial body, which
is seen along the resultant line of sight. The astronomer’s task
was to choose lengths and angular velocities for these vectors so
that the resulting motion agreed with observations. Besides its
philosophical appeal, this principle meshed with the idea of
celestial spheres, gave a tractable computational algorithm, and
enforced mathematical rigour. In its geocentric form this
approach had been adopted by the Maragha school of Islamic
astronomy, and Copernicus adopted the same method but with the
initial point of the vector chains near the sun rather than at
the centre of the earth. The particular point he chose would have
a decisive, and in retrospect unfortunate, influence on his final
scheme.

A major source of ambiguity in ancient astronomy stems from the
commutativity of vector addition: if the lengths and rotation
rates (relative to the fixed stars) of the vectors are unchanged,
the motion produced by a chain of rotating vectors is independent
of the order in which the vectors are taken. From Apollonius
onward the main astronomers recognised this fact to varying
degrees; Copernicus’s clear recognition of it may owe something
to Regiomontanus. That freedom allowed astronomers to rearrange
their circles and obtain sometimes surprising equivalences.

Many of the clever but technical innovations in Ptolemaic theory
by the Maragha school and by Copernicus, which eliminated
Ptolemy’s violations of uniform circular motion, exploit special
combinations of two vectors where one rotates with a period that
is an exact multiple of the other’s. One such construction, known
in modern literature as the Tusi couple (after Nasir al-Din
al-Tusi), was originally obtained as a geometric theorem. Take
a circle of radius R/2 rolling without slipping inside a circle
of radius R. Any fixed point on the circumference of the smaller
circle then moves back and forth along a diameter of the larger
circle, executing simple harmonic motion. The Tusi couple is
equivalent to a linkage of two vectors OA and AB of equal length
R/2, with AB rotating exactly as fast as OA but in the opposite
direction. The successive positions of the ends trace out the
required rectilinear motion. The Tusi couple thus translates
uniform circular motions into strictly rectilinear motion and
shows how rectilinear oscillations can be represented as
compounded circular motions.

Tusi used this device to eliminate the equant in Ptolemy’s
scheme. In Ptolemy’s construction an equant spoke rotates
uniformly about an off-centre point, but the length of the spoke
varies with the planet’s position, so the basic uniformity
principle is violated. For small eccentricities, the variation in
the spoke’s length can be represented approximately by simple
harmonic dependence on the anomaly. Tusi showed that by fixing
the spoke length and attaching a Tusi couple at its end, one can
reproduce the apparent variation in distance: the point
determined by the couple oscillates along the spoke while the
spoke itself rotates uniformly, so the apparent motion around the
equant point is preserved. For small eccentricity the resultant
motion closely matches the Ptolemaic motion.

An alternative device, described by Qutb al-Din al-Shirazi,
introduces a new point H between the observer and the equant
point. Around H rotates a vector HQ of radius equal to the
nominal deferent radius and with the orbital period of the
planet; at its end is attached a second vector QC of smaller
length that rotates in the same sense but at twice the angular
rate of HQ. When the planet is at apogee, QC points toward H.
This arrangement replaces the Ptolemaic eccentric deferent and
equant by a simple eccentric plus an epicycle in such a way that
the resulting motion is essentially the same as that produced by
the Tusi couple. Both devices therefore provide formal
eliminations of the equant, achieving Ptolemaic-type motions
while preserving uniform circular components.

Copernicus used both of these devices in different parts of his
work. His method for eliminating the equant in De Revolutionibus
mirrors Qutb al-Din’s construction, while the Tusi couple appears
in his theory of precession and in his model for Mercury. His
lunar theory closely resembles that of Ibn al-Shatir. Because of
these close correspondences, Copernicus has sometimes been
suspected of plagiarism. Independent rediscovery is common in
science, especially during periods of “normal science” when the
ground rules and the problems to be solved are clear; given
Ptolemy’s solutions and the goal of reconciling them with uniform
circular motion, it would have been natural for several competent
mathematicians to arrive at similar constructions. Still, the
resemblances raise legitimate doubts. The point here is not to
defend or accuse Copernicus but to outline the mathematical tools
available to him and to note that, although he professed to
eliminate the equant, the effect described by Ptolemy remained
effectively present in his scheme, reproduced by equivalent
mechanisms. In that sense Copernicus both eliminated and
preserved the equant’s observational consequences — a situation
that must have pleased him.

This brings us to the last preparatory point before examining
Copernicus’s details: his attitude toward Ptolemy’s work. After
Ptolemy’s death, no one seems to have undertaken a comprehensive
observational test of the whole Ptolemaic system. What was
commonly done — and often done successfully and accurately,
especially for the sun — was re-determination of particular model
parameters rather than a full-scale empirical reassessment of the
entire system.

What Copernicus did: second approximation

Now, in the first place, many of these parameters had not been
determined very accurately by either Hipparchus or Ptolemy;
secondly, several of them had changed appreciably in the interval
(mostly because of the precession of the equinoxes). For example,
the lines of the apsides shifted by about 20° relative to the
vernal equinox between Ptolemy and Copernicus, with additional
smaller but sometimes significant shifts relative to the fixed
stars. As a result, later observers, including Copernicus
himself, often found deviations — sometimes large — from the
parameter values given by Ptolemy and Hipparchus. Copernicus, who
tended to trust earlier observations (especially Ptolemy's),
accepted these deviations at face value and concluded that the
parameters of the Ptolemaic models changed with time. In that he
was right; however, by treating both earlier data and his own
observations with too little regard for their possible errors, he
weakened the value of his painstaking effort to redetermine
Ptolemy's parameters, mixing genuine changes with spurious ones.
What Copernicus did not do was question the basic correctness of
the Ptolemaic models. Instead he set himself three tasks: convert
the Ptolemaic models from geocentric to heliocentric form,
eliminate Ptolemy's violations of uniform circular motion, and
redetermine the parameters of the Ptolemaic models after adapting
them to the first two aims. Because the first two aims were
mostly formal, Copernicus made no attempt to alter the essentials
of Ptolemy's work. He appears to have given no consideration to
the possibility that Ptolemy's models contained genuine errors or
that placing the sun near the centre of the planetary system
might have far‑reaching implications beyond the purely kinematic
consequences he appreciated. By doing so he missed a major
opportunity, which prompted Kepler to remark: "Unaware of his own
riches, Copernicus merely set himself the task of reproducing
Ptolemy, not the nature of things, to which however he had come
the closest of all."

Let us now see how Copernicus approached his task. Having adopted
the idea that the Earth revolves around the sun rather than vice
versa, his first step was to transcribe the Earth–Sun motion.
Here he made his biggest mistake: he simply inverted the
Hipparchan solution. Just as Hipparchus and Ptolemy had modeled
the sun as circulating uniformly about a point displaced from the
Earth, Copernicus modeled the Earth as circulating uniformly
about a point displaced from the sun. That led to two errors. He
was right to displace the centre of the Earth's orbit from the
centre of the sun, but, following Hipparchus and Ptolemy, he took
the eccentricity (the displacement) to be too large by a factor
of two. Second, he assumed that the Earth's motion about this
displaced point was perfectly uniform. He postulated that the
Earth moves uniformly in a circle, which he called the orbis
magnus (the great circle), about a point located at twice the
distance of the actual centre of the Earth's orbit.

A clear understanding of the exact location of this point is
crucial. Expressed in Keplerian terms, it is the second (void)
focus of the Earth's orbit. This measures Copernicus's error: as
we now know, the centre of the Earth's orbit in heliocentric
space lies exactly at the midpoint between the two foci.
Copernicus therefore misplaced the centre of the Earth's orbit by
one eccentricity — an error of roughly 1.8 solar diameters.

It is worth noting what happens to the Ptolemaic concept of the
mean sun. In Ptolemy's scheme the mean sun is merely a direction
from the Earth that points toward the position a uniformly moving
body would have if it coincided with the true sun in the apsides.
In the Copernican arrangement these lines all pass through the
second focus of the Earth's orbit, so one can regard the mean sun
as a fictitious body occupying that void focus. Following
Swerdlow and Neugebauer, we may call this the Copernican mean
sun; it sits at the centre of the orbis magnus.

There is no indication in De Revolutionibus whether Copernicus
ever considered alternatives to his chosen arrangement. He must
have realized that an equant‑type motion for the Earth was at
least a logical possibility. Since this question ties into his
treatment of the other planets, I defer further discussion;
I note only that his solution — which reproduces the observed
solar motions very well — suited his taste for circular motions
and likely helped suppress any remaining doubts he might have
had.

This error by Copernicus, probably made around 1510, had already
troubled astronomy (in its geocentric form) for about fifteen
centuries and persisted for nearly another century until Kepler
finally identified and removed it. The mistake was disastrous for
the new astronomy because, as Copernicus understood better than
anyone before him, correctly interpreting the celestial spectacle
depends crucially on understanding the motion of the observer. If
the observer moves, the apparent dance of the heavens changes.
Paradoxically, Copernicus committed precisely the error he had
warned against: "Hence I deem it above all necessary that we
should carefully scrutinize the relation of the earth to the
heavens lest, in our desire to examine the loftiest objects, we
remain ignorant of things nearest to us, and by the same error
attribute to the celestial bodies what belongs to the earth."
Consequently, several of the motions Copernicus introduced to
explain planetary motions have the same origin as the epicycles
of the original Ptolemaic system — a mistaken awareness of the
Earth's true position. These new motions are analogous to
aftershocks following the main adjustment: secondary corrections
to the new equilibrium position. They nevertheless caused real
headaches — they particularly drove Brahe and his assistants to
distraction.

So far we have considered only the Earth–Sun relationship. It was
in placing the planets within his overall cosmology that
Copernicus introduced his most incongruous ideas. Here the
specific values of the planetary eccentricities once again
produced their subtle effects.

Let us begin with the three outer planets. On the face of it,
Ptolemy had already solved their kinematics: the
epicycle–deferent construction effectively mapped the planets'
motion around the sun into the motion of deferent guide points
around the Earth. It would seem that Copernicus only needed to
convert the Ptolemaic deferent motion about the Earth into actual
planetary motion about the sun. Moreover, the deferent part of
the epicycle–deferent theory was already very nearly correct. But
not exactly — and that slight mismatch proved Copernicus's
undoing. The deferent theory, with its equant and eccentricity,
would have been first‑order correct in the planets'
eccentricities (ignoring the tiny ellipticity corrections) if
Ptolemy had aligned everything on the true sun rather than on the
mean sun. Copernicus, following Ptolemy faithfully, aligned the
deferent constructions on the mean sun. Thus the formal
transcription of the Ptolemaic scheme for Mars, Jupiter, and
Saturn led Copernicus to align their apsidal lines on the mean
sun as well: he assumed that the lines through the centres of
their orbits and through the corresponding equants (which he
retained as computational auxiliaries) converged on the centre of
the orbis magnus — the ghostly position of the mean sun rather
than on the true solar centre. Copernicus was unaware that this
was a serious affront to the sun.

It is instructive to see the physical reason why this mistake
could be made and why it went unnoticed for sixty years despite
Brahe's efforts. Figure 5.7 shows the sun and the positions of
the centres of the orbits and of the equants for the Earth and
for Mars, Jupiter and Saturn. The continuous lines are the
apsidal lines, and they all converge on the sun. The dashed lines
show Copernicus's result from his mechanical transcription of the
Ptolemaic system; the false convergence point in that diagram
measures the residual errors in the Ptolemaic scheme. One can see
why these errors escaped detection: they are much smaller than
the principal deviations that Ptolemy had removed with his
epicycle–deferent scheme. Those main deviations correspond to the
large displacements of the centres and equants of the planetary
orbits away from the solar centre, which are much larger than the
corresponding displacements for the Earth.

The same diagram simultaneously explains another residual error
of the Ptolemaic epicycle–deferent theory for the outer planets
— the assumed perfectly uniform rotation of the epicycle spoke of
fixed length about the void deferent guide point. Recall that
Ptolemy's scheme is an accurate first‑order approximation in the
eccentricities of the outer planets' orbits but only zeroth order
in the Earth's eccentricity. To reach first order in both
eccentricities would have required Ptolemy to adopt a much more
complicated construction, involving eccentric and nonuniform
equant‑type motion inside the epicycle as well. He did not,
because (i) the Earth's eccentricity is much smaller than those
of the three outer planets and (ii) the observability of the
Earth's eccentricity in the epicyclic motions of the outer
planets is further reduced by the ratio of their semimajor axes
to that of the Earth. Thus, in the apparent motions of Mars,
Jupiter, and Saturn the effects of their own eccentricities are
on average about 8.5, 15, and 31 times larger, respectively, than
the effects due to the Earth's eccentricity. This is why the
Ptolemaic models could be so good and yet very misleading for
Copernicus: the observed near‑uniform epicyclic motions of the
outer planets seemed to confirm his transcription of the
Hipparchan–Ptolemaic solar motion — they indicated that the Earth
must circle uniformly about the mean sun, just as he had found
for the solar model. Figure 5.8 shows Copernicus's formal
transcription for the outer planets.

We have not yet considered the two inner planets. Before doing
so, it is worth emphasizing an important constraint under which
Copernicus worked that did not apply to Ptolemy. The Ptolemaic
system treated the motions of all celestial bodies independently;
a priori there was no requirement that the different observed
motions be connected. Nevertheless, observations displayed
apparent correlations: the epicyclic motions of the outer planets
and the deferent motions of the inner planets seemed strongly
related to the motion of the sun. Because Ptolemy sought no
deeper reason, his system tolerated some mismatch among these
motions. Copernicus's radical suggestion of terrestrial motion,
by contrast, implied that these seemingly separate motions were
merely reflections of a single motion of the Earth. The
inescapable consequence was that they all had to be exactly
correlated.

This point Copernicus understood well, but his uncritical
attitude toward the accuracy and essential correctness of the
Ptolemaic models led him into severe difficulties with the inner
planets. Here eccentricities led him into a peculiar error. It is
especially ironic because Ptolemy's model for Venus was, by
chance, one of his best fits and might have provided Copernicus
with a valuable hint. Recall that Ptolemy had effectively
determined the Earth’s orbital elements twice: once (incorrectly)
in the solar theory, yielding an eccentricity twice too large,
and once (more or less correctly) in his construction of Venus's
motion. The same effect between the Earth and the outer planets
appears here. Venus's eccentricity is 2.45 times smaller than the
Earth's, and the semimajor axis ratio (1.39) means that in the
apparent motion of Venus the influence of the Earth's
eccentricity shows up on average about 3½ times more strongly
than that of Venus itself (both effects are small compared to the
outer planets). This again produced the impression that Venus's
epicyclic motion was perfectly uniform circular motion. In the
Ptolemaic system, however, the deferent motion (with its
eccentricity and equant) corresponded to the Earth's motion. If
Copernicus had taken that at face value, his straightforward
transcription would have led him to conclude that the Earth moved
on an eccentric with motion governed by an equant, and that the
equant lay at the Copernican mean sun while the centre of the
eccentric was displaced accordingly. Such a transcription would
have brought him close to the truth. But the Ptolemaic scheme
gave him contradictory hints: the earlier transcriptions for the
sun and for the three outer planets had all told him the Earth
had no equant and simply circled uniformly around the mean sun.
The transcription for Venus therefore forced the centre of the
Earth's orbit to appear in two different places simultaneously.
Facing this impossibility, Copernicus had to choose. The sun,
Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn all supported the view that the Earth
circled uniformly about the mean sun; Venus (and to some degree
Mercury) suggested otherwise. Copernicus chose to trust the
majority and the outer planets.

If the Earth indeed circles the mean sun, how can that be
reconciled with Venus's apparent motion? Copernicus adopted
a remarkable workaround: he kept his previous solution for the
Earth's motion and transferred the "excess motion" required by
Ptolemy to Venus. In Ptolemy's Venus model the Earth would be
described (following earlier practice) by an eccentric plus an
epicycle whose guide point rotates around the eccentric at twice
the angular velocity of the guide point itself. Copernicus
instead shifted that correction to Venus: he made the centre of
Venus's circle move itself in a very small circle twice during
the time the Earth completes one orbit. In effect he
redistributed the required corrections so that, from the
observer's point of view, Venus's motion reproduced the same
apparent path. The Copernican transcription for Venus, and the
crucial role of the commutativity of vector addition in it, is
illustrated in Fig. 5.9.

Copernicus does not discuss these difficulties in De
Revolutionibus; he merely states that "this planet differs
somewhat from the others in the pattern and measurement of its
motions" and then gives his transcription without explanation.
For Mercury he was forced to introduce an even stranger
correlation with the Earth's motion on the orbis magnus: Mercury
required two motions — one in a small circle and one along the
diameter of another small circle controlled by a Tusi couple
— both executed twice in a terrestrial year.

We are not yet finished with the oddities of the Copernican
system, because we must mention the particularly bizarre
mechanisms Copernicus devised to account for planetary motions in
latitude, i.e., motions out of the ecliptic. We have largely
ignored latitude so far, but it proved important for Kepler. The
planes of the planets’ orbits are inclined at small angles to the
Earth's orbital plane (the ecliptic), so planets sometimes lie
north and sometimes south of the ecliptic. Because the
longitudinal motions are nonuniform, the rate at which a planet
moves from north to south is also nonuniform, and planets do not
spend equal times on the two sides of the ecliptic. Moreover, as
the Earth moves, changing its distance to the other planets, an
apparent additional latitude deviation appears. This effect is
especially pronounced at opposition for an outer planet: the
planet then typically deviates more strongly from the ecliptic
while performing its retrograde motion, thereby tracing a loop
against the background stars. Examples of such retrograde loops
for Jupiter at different parts of its orbit are shown in Fig.
5.10.

In principle, these latitude effects should have provided some of
the most convincing arguments for the heliocentric theory, since
latitude motions — like the epicyclic motions of the outer
planets and the deferent motions of the inner planets — can be
explained kinematically as reflections of the Earth's motion by
assuming the planets' orbital planes are fixed, inclined by
certain angles to the ecliptic, and pass through the centre of
the sun. Although Copernicus made those planes pass through the
mean sun rather than the true solar centre, that error alone
would have little influence on the small latitude effects. He was
aware of the attractive simplicity of this explanation, but he
was misled by the unsatisfactory state of Ptolemy's latitude
theory as given in the Almagest. Ptolemy must have been working
with quite inaccurate latitude data when he wrote the Almagest,
because his latitude theory contains many defects; he was forced,
for example, to introduce variable tilts of the epicycle planes
relative to their deferents, with the tilts varying with the
sun's position in the ecliptic. Evidently Ptolemy continued to
refine his latitude theory after the Almagest: his later Handy
Tables and Planetary Hypotheses contain a much improved theory
— one that corresponds well to a transcription from heliocentric
to geocentric descriptions and, in particular, eliminates the
variable tilts.

It seems this later Ptolemaic work did not become widely known,
and Copernicus gives no sign of being aware of it. He therefore
tried to reproduce the Almagest's geocentric latitude theory in
his own system. Part of the latitude motion could be explained
correctly, but for the remaining spurious components inherited
from the Almagest he invented a wobble of the orbital planes so
that their tilts varied in phase with the Earth's annual motion.
This Copernican mistake may have had a silver lining: Kepler,
recognizing the wobble as monstrous, soon tested it against
Brahe's observations and easily showed it to be false. From that
work he established two important facts: (1) the planes of the
planets' orbits have fixed inclinations in heliocentric space,
and (2) they all pass through the sun, not through the centre of
the orbis magnus as Copernicus believed. This result was later
called Kepler's Zeroth Law and played an important role in
establishing the law of inertia and other foundations of
dynamics, demonstrating their validity over astronomical
distances.

By trusting Ptolemy so implicitly in latitude matters, Copernicus
missed a great opportunity to achieve radical simplification in
both the larger and smaller celestial motions. When all the
pieces fit together coherently and accurately, a theoretical
scheme gains real persuasive power; Copernicus failed to attain
that coherence. It was in connection with the lamentable latitude
theory of De Revolutionibus that Kepler made his remark about
Copernicus not appreciating his own riches (divitiarum suarum
ipse ignarus).

The numerous errors in the Copernican system — the transcription
of the Hipparchan solar theory for the Earth's motion, the
identification of the apsidal convergence point with the centre
of the orbis magnus, the peculiar correlations between the
longitudinal motions of the inner planets and the Earth's motion,
and the strange latitude motions (all born of excessive fidelity
to Ptolemy) — produced the most remarkable feature of the
Copernican scheme. Although Copernicus recognized the importance
of the sun and made the Earth a planet like the others, his final
system remained, in many respects, geocentric. First, the Earth's
motion was quite different from the planets': alone among them it
was assumed to move uniformly in heliocentric space. Second, the
motions of the five planets were not centred on the sun but on
the centre of the Earth's orbit, the orbis magnus; moreover they
were strongly correlated with the Earth's orbital period. Only
with this understanding do we grasp the meaning of Copernicus's
remark to the pope that he would "correlate the motions of the
other planets … with the movement of the earth."

As noted earlier, Copernicus contemplated the heliocentric idea
"not merely until the ninth year but by now the fourth period of
nine years"; yet it apparently never occurred to him that the
Earth should behave like the other planets or that the planets'
motions should be centred on the sun rather than on the centre of
the Earth's orbit. Before we dismiss his scheme too harshly,
remember that Brahe and other astronomers worked comfortably with
it for a couple of generations without detecting a fundamental
problem, and nearly a century after Copernicus's death even
Galileo did not suspect this particular flaw. That shows the
conception of motion was not yet ripe for change. This point will
be taken up again later and in the chapter on Kepler.

We should also recognize that, from Copernicus's perspective, his
work was a success. Apart from proposing heliocentrism and
genuinely improving Ptolemy's lunar theory, the abolition of the
Ptolemaic equant — an aesthetic and conceptual affront that had
much to do with heliocentric considerations — must have been
deeply satisfying to him, especially if he independently
rediscovered the elegant device that eliminated it. The equant's
importance as a stimulus to re‑examine Ptolemy's models should
not be forgotten; we owe Copernicus's quixotic mission to purify
astronomy a debt for the greatest revolution in the history of
science.

As he wrestled with celestial motions over many years, Copernicus
faced several difficulties. Some were his own preconceptions;
many stemmed from a paucity of observations adequate to pin down
the numerous small effects he wanted to accommodate; others
resulted from the actual eccentricities of the planetary orbits.
In addition, throughout his work he confronted the problem of
kinematic relativity: the apparent motion of any body can include
a component due to the observer's motion rather than the
object's. He highlighted and exploited this point brilliantly,
but it also worked against him in several ways, most notably in
his treatment of Venus. Ironically, what he failed to see for
Venus, Mercury, and the latitude motions he did recognize in
another curious issue: the exact position of the centre of the
universe. As we shall see next, Copernicus had a fairly precise
notion of spatial ordering, yet he was at a loss about where to
place the universe's exact centre.

Strictly, q, p and r should be associated with the Earth's motion
and only s with Venus; Copernicus, however, grouped p and r with
s so that, as for the outer planets, the Earth appears to circle
uniformly around O.

He was frustrated by the same combination of factors that plagued
his other work: inaccurate observations, leftover defects from
the Ptolemaic system, and his own peculiar mix of heliocentric
and geocentric ideas. His first and most important task had been
to express the earth–sun motion in heliocentric terms. That
effort led him to the concept of the orbis magnus and to
a distinguished centre for it. This, however, dealt only with the
sun's large-scale annual motion. Superimposed on that were very
slow secular changes that had accumulated since Ptolemy's time.
In the observational record available to Copernicus these slow
effects, together with the precession of the equinoxes, could not
be cleanly separated from observational errors. In effect
Copernicus was trying to transcribe into heliocentric language an
amalgam of phenomena some genuine and some mere artefacts of
inaccurate data, above all an apparent irregularity in the
apsidal precession.

The scheme Copernicus devised to account for these slow
variations can be described as follows. Place the sun at S.
A point A moves slowly around a circle centred on S from west to
east with a period of roughly 53 000 years (corresponding to
a mean annual apsidal precession of a little more than 24 seconds
of arc). Around A, a point B moves in a much smaller circle in
the opposite direction with a period of about 3434 years. The
moving point B is the centre of the earth's annual orbit, that
is, the centre of the orbis magnus.

Copernicus was openly uncertain — and he admits this quite
candidly — whether to take the sun as absolutely at rest and let
the centre of the orbis magnus move as just described, or to hold
the orbis magnus fixed and let the sun execute analogous motions.
Both choices would produce the same observational consequences;
only the designation of the formal centre of the universe would
change. Thus, in Book I, Chapter 9, he writes that "the sun
occupies the middle of the universe", but a little later in
Chapter 10 he says that "near the sun is the centre of the
universe", which there he seems to identify with the centre of
the orbis magnus. Later, discussing the details of this theory,
he remarks: Nevertheless I am also not unaware that if anybody
believed the center of the annual revolution to be stationary as
the center of the universe, while the sun moved with two motions
similar and equal to those which I explained in connection with
the center of the eccentric [III, 20], all the phenomena would
appear as before — the same figures and the same proof. Nothing
would be changed in them, especially the phenomena pertaining to
the sun, except the position. For then the motion of the earth's
center around the center of the universe would be regular and
simple (the two remaining motions being ascribed to the sun). For
this reason there will still remain a doubt about which of these
two positions is occupied by the center of the universe, as
I said ambiguously at the beginning that the center of the
universe is in the sun [I, 9, 10] or near it [I, 10].

This frank admission of indeterminacy anticipates the debate
about motion that would dominate later seventeenth-century
thought and that still resonates: What is motion? By what
criteria are rest and motion defined? If motion is defined only
by relation to particular identifiable objects, then there are as
many motions as there are reference objects. Copernicus could
treat this problem without alarm because he still had a final
fallback: the fixed stars. To him the uncertainty arose simply
because the corresponding effects were far too small to be seen
in parallax displacements. In principle he thought he had nothing
to fear. The possibility that the "fixed stars" might not be
fixed at all was not yet a pressing threat; that realization lay
about a century in the future. This issue will be important for
understanding his concept of place.

This completes the technical account we need from De
Revolutionibus. There is much more material in the work that
I have omitted here, including his theories for the moon and for
Mercury. Judged only by improvements in the models of celestial
motion, Copernicus's contribution was modest if one ignores his
heliocentric hypothesis: the moon theory was his main advance
over Ptolemy. Still, he rendered a useful service by updating
Ptolemy: he recomputed orbits and determined new numerical
parameters, and his elements were used in the compilation of the
new astronomical tables, the Prutenic Tables. Many of his
immediate successors regarded these practical improvements,
together with his ingenious device for removing the equant, as
his chief achievements. Later scholarship has shown that working
astronomers in the second half of the sixteenth century did make
use of his work; despite claims to the contrary, his book was
read by professionals. Intriguingly, however, none of them
publicly advocated the motion of the earth until the young
Kepler, at twenty-five, gave eloquent support more than fifty
years after De Revolutionibus first appeared.

### 5.6. Copernicus's concept of place & ultimate frame of reference

Begin by noting a typically Renaissance cosmological idea that
appears strongly in Copernicus, Kepler, and Galileo: the idea of
the well-ordered cosmos, which goes back to Pythagoras. He used
the Greek word cosmos to express the notion of ordered
arrangement; the Latin mundus conveyed a similar idea, the
ornamented or ordered world. This revival of a Pythagorean sense
of cosmic order provided a powerful psychological stimulus for
the study of astronomy and celestial motions, one amplified by
a renewed appreciation for Platonic ideals of geometric
exactitude and symmetry. At the same time, it shaped the view of
motion as an expression of the overall harmony of the cosmos. If
the cosmos derives harmony from the felicitous mutual arrangement
of its parts, then position and motion are naturally seen in
relational terms. This cosmos-based concept of motion is
therefore relational and matter-based; natural motions must at
least preserve the overall harmony resulting from the disposition
of the parts. This attitude is prominent in both Copernicus and
Galileo. Paradoxically, although Kepler's cosmology was as
Pythagorean and Aristotelian as theirs, his conception of
celestial motion was considerably more modern and physical.

If Copernicus's concept of motion can be seen as a kind of
proto-Mach principle — a prescientific, even artistic, emphasis
on relational definitions — then Kepler's view moves closer to
a modern, physically based form of that principle. One of the
main aims of this chapter and the next is to explain how this
shift in the concept of motion occurred. The crucial element in
the transition away from aesthetic geometro-kineticism was
Ptolemy's introduction of the equant, which Copernicus was so
determined to eliminate. Before turning to that topic, however,
let us examine Copernicus's notion of place more closely.

The Aristotelian element in Copernicus is particularly
interesting here. He was concerned that his concept of motion be
epistemologically sound. He rejected the idea that motion takes
place relative to empty space; motion must be defined relative to
observable matter. Like Aristotle, he appeals to the idea of
a universal frame of reference; the difference is that for
Aristotle that frame could be conceived as rotating, whereas for
Copernicus it is, almost by definition, at rest. For example,
early in Book I he argues: "since the heavens, which enclose and
provide the setting for everything, constitute the space common
to all things, it is not at first blush clear why motion should
not be attributed rather to the enclosed than to the enclosing,
to the thing located in space than to the framework of space." By
"the heavens" Copernicus meant the immensely distant sphere of
the fixed stars.

This notion of position and motion comes from Aristotle, but
centuries of practical astronomy also shaped Copernicus's
thinking. He needed a single container or frame; his world is not
an onion of nested topological shells but a single ultimate
boundary. In Copernicus the outermost ouranos of Aristotle is
combined with the Platonic idea — which Aristotle rejected — that
space is a dimensional extension between points on the containing
surface. The region inside the container is therefore conceived
not as a stack of envelopes but as a tightly spanned domain of
trigonometric relations: an invisible membrane whose rim is
defined by the fixed stars.

Kepler provided a clear illustration of this idea in a diagram
showing the stars (the "studs") around the rim of a drum marked
with the zodiac. Successive points on the rim represent
successive great conjunctions of Jupiter and Saturn. Lines drawn
across the face of the drum highlight how metrical geometry is
used within the ultimate frame of reference defined by the stars.
Copernicus was similarly open-minded about what lay beyond the
fixed stars, writing "Let us therefore leave the question whether
the universe is finite or infinite to be discussed by the natural
philosophers." But he never forgot that those studs on the rim
are what ultimately define position.

He could not afford to forget them, for he was asserting that the
earth moves. The earth-bound frame — the solid ground on which
Ptolemy stood — is the one frame that is useless for
demonstrating the earth's mobility. There is a curious tension in
Copernicus's proposal that reveals something about his concept of
motion and the difficulty of conceiving motion: he wanted to set
the earth free from its traditional bearings, but he could not do
that without at the same time enclosing it securely. Without that
enclosing rim he could not assert that the earth moves at all.
This gives De Revolutionibus an oddly claustrophobic flavour
compared with Ptolemy: Ptolemy stands on the earth and looks
outward; Copernicus first checks that the walls are present and
only then works inward. Following Aristotle, Copernicus accepts
that "beyond the heavens there is ... no body, no space, no void,
absolutely nothing." He begins his cosmology: "The first and
highest of all is the sphere of the fixed stars, which contains
itself and everything, and is therefore immovable. It is
unquestionably the place of the universe, to which the motion and
position of all the other heavenly bodies are compared."

There are several other passages in De Revolutionibus where he is
equally explicit.

It is worth emphasizing that four major theoretical astronomers
— Ptolemy, Copernicus, Kepler, and Poincaré — made important
contributions to the development of dynamics, and when pressed to
decide "What is motion?" they all instinctively referred motion
to observable matter. By contrast, none of the three giants
typically associated with dynamics — Galileo, Newton, and
Einstein — were working primarily as astronomical theorists in
the same hands-on way as the other four; interestingly, they
tended to think of motion as taking place relative to space.
Maxwell, who was hardly less influential, also moved in that
direction. We will return to this distinction in a later volume.
(A caveat about Newton is needed: he did work on astronomical
problems, but he undertook that work in earnest after formulating
the elements of his dynamics, and he used it mainly to confirm
details of his theory of universal gravitation.)

This is a convenient moment to review how the idea of a frame of
reference developed up to Copernicus's time. Primitive people
naturally referred motion to the earth's surface. Early atomists
later postulated an infinite void, but in practice their void was
just an invisible extension of the earth's framework; even then
they retained notions like Up and Down. Aristotle halted that
speculative excess and insisted on epistemological rigor,
defining place by visible matter and even endowing place with
a certain dynamical potency: the elements are drawn to their
proper places.

The Hellenistic astronomers did not develop that incipient
dynamism; instead they focused on accurately charting and
explaining celestial motions. Early astronomers effectively used
two equivalent frames: the earth and the fixed stars, related to
each other by a simple uniform rotation. Then a kind of crisis
occurred: the discovery of the precession of the equinoxes by
Hipparchus sometime after 135 BC introduced complications.

A modern (post-Newtonian) account of the precession is the
following. The earth's orbital plane defines the ecliptic on the
celestial sphere. The earth's diurnal rotation axis is inclined
to the pole of the ecliptic by about 23½° (the obliquity of the
ecliptic). Because the earth is oblate and its rotation axis is
tilted, the sun's gravitational pull exerts a torque on the
spinning, oblate earth, causing the rotation axis to precess
uniformly around the pole of the ecliptic. The axis traces
a circle on the celestial sphere with radius equal to the
obliquity (about 23½°) and completes that circle in roughly 26
000 years. Consequently, the positions on the ecliptic
corresponding to the equinoxes and solstices slowly drift around
the ecliptic in the opposite direction to the sun's annual
motion. The tropical year, defined by the equinoxes and
solstices, is therefore about twenty minutes shorter than the
sidereal year, which is the time for the sun to return to the
same position relative to the fixed stars.

Hipparchus discovered precession by comparing observations over
many years, and the phenomenon forced Ptolemy to reconsider what
should count as the ultimate frame of reference. The tropical
year is defined in the earth frame: a gnomon fixed to the earth
measures the interval between successive times when the noon
shadow is shortest. The sidereal year is defined in the geoastral
frame by the sun's return to the same position on the ecliptic
relative to the stars.

Ptolemy wrestled with how to define the length of the year and,
more generally, whether any unambiguous frame of reference exists
for motion. Practical considerations prevailed: he chose to
define the year by the tropical cycle. He wrote that "We must
define the length of the year as the time the sun takes to travel
from some fixed point on this circle back again to the same
point. The only points which we can consider proper
starting-points for the sun's revolution are those defined by the
equinoxes and solstices on that circle. For if we consider the
subject from a mathematical viewpoint, we will find no more
appropriate way to define a 'revolution' than that which returns
the sun to the same relative position, both in place and in time,
whether one relates it to the [local] horizon, to the meridian,
or to the length of the day and night; and the only
starting-points on the ecliptic which we can find are those which
happen to be defined by the equinoxes and solstices. And if,
instead, we consider what is appropriate from a physical point of
view, we will not find anything which could more reasonably be
considered a 'revolution' than that which returns the sun to
a similar atmospheric condition and the same season; and the only
starting-point one could find [for this revolution] are those
which are the principal means of marking off the seasons from one
another (i.e. solsticial and equinoctial points)."

Ptolemy was an astronomer with his feet on the ground. He
measured everything relative to the earth and assumed that,
besides the diurnal rotation, all the stars (and with them the
entire planetary orbits, though not the sun's) are subject to the
slow additional motion of precession. He also anticipated the
conceptual problem at the heart of later debates about Mach's
principle: if all bodies in the universe move relative to one
another, how can one pick out an ultimate, privileged frame of
reference? He remarked that it seems unnatural to define the
sun's revolution by its return to some fixed star, since one
could equally say that the length of the solar year is the time
between two conjunctions of the sun with Saturn or any other
planet; in that way many different "years" could be defined.

Both Hipparchus and Ptolemy had a certain distrust of the stars'
absolute fixity. Hipparchus at one point suspected that some
stars near the ecliptic might be very slowly moving planets,
because he observed their longitudes increasing slowly with time;
later, with sparse evidence, he inclined to the correct view that
the effect was common to all stars and represented precession.
Ptolemy, having access to Hipparchus's accurate observations made
about three centuries earlier, compared them with his own and
confirmed the precession interpretation. Hipparchus is credited
with compiling the first systematic star catalogue, and Ptolemy
went to considerable trouble to use that catalogue to test
whether the "fixed" stars exhibit proper motions relative to each
other. He listed alignments of three stars on a single line and
compared positions over a span of three hundred years, finding no
visible changes. He carefully preserved Hipparchus's alignments
and added his own so that later observers would have long
baselines for comparison. Eventually, in 1718, Halley showed that
stars such as Arcturus and Sirius had shifted by about one degree
since Ptolemy's time.

By Copernicus's era the situation regarding the length of the
year and the precession had grown complicated. Inaccurate
observations (including some by Ptolemy, who had estimated
a precession period of 36 000 years) suggested that precession
might be nonuniform. Copernicus accepted that conclusion too
readily, trusting earlier observations more than warranted. To
explain the slow variations, some astronomers had posited
additional spheres with their own motions; others introduced
spurious devices. Copernicus rejected these accretions and
preferred to attribute the observed effects to motions of the
earth — a simpler conception than piling yet more spheres into
the heavens.

Writing long before Halley's discovery of stellar proper motions
and decades before Descartes raised serious doubts about the
stability of the stellar frame, Copernicus was confident that the
fixed stars provided the only appropriate ultimate frame of
reference, and he advised that one "must not heed Ptolemy in this
regard."

### 5.7. Copernicus's concept of motion

In an important respect, the Copernican revolution should be seen
as a culmination of ancient theoretical astronomy rather than its
overthrow. Hellenistic astronomy may have lacked an explicit
notion of physical causality, but it did hold that the
two-dimensional motions observed on the sky should be explained
by motions in three-dimensional space. That programme — to "save
the appearances" by assigning motions in space — distinguishes
Greek theoretical astronomy from Babylonian practice, and
Copernicus's insight is the crowning achievement of that
programme. The power of explaining observed phenomena by assuming
particular motions in space is the essence of the Copernican
revolution. In its ability to show how much can be achieved by
kinematic hypotheses, its importance rivals the introduction of
the epicycle–deferent scheme to which it is closely related.

Although Copernicus would have strongly rejected Osiander's
suggestion that the earth's mobility should be treated merely as
a convenient hypothesis rather than as literal, he would have had
little quarrel with Osiander's basic principle of theoretical
astronomy: adopt whatever suppositions enable motions to be
computed correctly from geometric principles for the future as
well as for the past. Now we will look more closely at how
Copernicus conceived motion and particularly at the role he
assigned to the sun.

Copernicus's thinking, though sometimes radical and clear when
necessary, often looks back more to Aristotle and Plato than
Ptolemy does; in some respects he seems more constrained than
Ptolemy. Geometry plays a more prominent role in its own right
throughout De Revolutionibus than in the Almagest: for Copernicus
geometry is almost a Platonic key to nature's essence, whereas
for Ptolemy it is primarily a practical tool. Plato is cited
frequently in De Revolutionibus; he is not invoked in the
Almagest.

This Platonic-mystical attachment to geometry helps explain
Copernicus's insistence that all observed celestial motions be
represented as superpositions of exact circles moving uniformly.
Consequently, he would have objected to the "whatever
suppositions" latitude implicit in Osiander's remark, seeing it
as excessive licence. That insistence on perfect circles also had
a historical accident to it: by holding fast to uniform circular
motion, he was led to one of the great discoveries of science
— the superiority of a heliocentric arrangement for simplifying
the description of planetary motion. It would have been too much
to expect Copernicus to see that his method for eliminating the
equant was logically distinct from that discovery.

Another decisive reason for Copernicus's adherence to the archaic
geometro-kinetic ideal of perfect uniform circles was his belief
that the stars are essentially fixed relative to one another. The
apparent immobility of the stars let him cover the abyss of
indeterminacy with a sheet of ice and trace exquisite geometric
patterns upon it. From his new vantage point he had no reason to
suspect that the Creator intended anything more complicated than
beautiful circles on that frozen surface. In that respect his
picture was not unreasonable: he was the first to see with
clarity the grandeur of the arena erected to stage the celestial
motions.

Thus, although Copernicus's work powerfully demonstrated the
utility of kinematic relativity — the idea that observed motions
can be re-described by changing the choice of moving frame — he
took only the first step toward the fuller relativity of motion.
That fuller step, which traces ultimately to ideas expressed by
Aristarchus and much later by Mach, goes further: not only are
position and motion revealed and defined by bodies in the
universe (the insight for which we owe much to Copernicus), but
they are actually determined by them. Realizing that there are no
truly stationary bodies in the universe is a further conceptual
leap that lay a century beyond Copernicus. He had no reason to
doubt the reality of a sphere of truly fixed stars; in his mind
that sphere had near-mystical status. This belief explains why
heliocentricity was, in his work, an important but not centrally
decisive feature.

It is significant that Copernicus begins his cosmology with the
description of the sphere of fixed stars: that is all he needs.
The space inside their rim is perfectly well defined. Within that
invisible space one can (by Copernicus's view, or by God's)
describe any circle one pleases; the stars around the edge give
the circle and its centre a definite, real place.

Such a circle can serve as the path of a moving planet, which is
assumed to travel uniformly around it, or as the path of a void
guide point that is itself the centre of an epicycle carried
around a deferent and on which the planet rides, again moving
uniformly about the epicycle. Everything is reduced to uniform
circular motion, and this motion is defined with respect to the
ultimate frame of reference. The existence of the fixed stars,
thought to be at rest relative to one another, allowed Copernicus
to be entirely satisfied with a purely geometrokinetic account of
motion. He changed the frame of reference but not the underlying
concept of motion.

Thus Copernicus affirmed the reality of motion while stressing
that it appears differently from different points of view; from
some viewpoints it may even seem to vanish. He went only halfway
toward a fully relative conception of motion: he retained a kind
of safety net. Motion still exists even if we do not observe it.
It is hard to accept a view with no fixed ground beneath our
feet. Ironically, post‑Copernican defenders of Aristotelian
cosmology, in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries,
pushed optical relativity to the opposite extreme. Just as
Copernicus used relativity to show the earth could move even if
it seemed at rest, they argued that the same principle could
equally well mean the earth is at rest while the rest of the
universe moves. They sought refuge in the impartiality of
relativity, failing to see that this doctrine had far more
disturbing implications than Copernicus’s actual scheme. In
Copernicus’s cosmology at least one knew where one stood; he was
closer in spirit to Aristotle and the reassuring idea that wine
belongs in a bottle or a candle in a lantern.

Which brings us back, in stages, to the sun. For Copernicus, the
task of accounting for planetary motion (with the earth included
among the planets) was to describe it — not to provide a causal
explanation — and to show that it consists of a superposition of
uniform circular motions. Everything else was subordinated to
that requirement: if you achieved uniform circular components,
the task was, in his view, complete. As he wrote proudly at the
end of the Commentariolus: "Then Mercury runs on seven circles in
all; Venus on five; the earth on three, and round it the moon on
four; finally Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn on five each. Altogether,
therefore, thirty-four circles suffice to explain the entire
structure of the universe and the entire ballet of the planets."

If Copernicus had any concept of motion beyond geometrokineticism
or mere ballet, it was mechanical. In his preface to the pope he
complained that philosophers had not understood the movements of
the world machine, created for our sake "by the best and most
systematic Artisan of all," with sufficient certainty, even
though they examined trifles of this world with great care.
Although he says little explicitly, it seems Copernicus did
believe the planets were carried by spheres of a world machine.
The meaning he intended for the Latin word orbis — which could
mean both sphere and circle — has been the subject of
controversy, with Rosen and Swerdlow taking opposite sides. The
debate began with Swerdlow’s suggestion that Copernicus may have
discovered the Tychonic system about the same time as his own
heliocentric account. Why, Swerdlow asked, did Copernicus choose
his system instead of the Tychonic one, which avoids the
dynamical and theological problems of a mobile earth? He
tentatively concluded that Copernicus was strongly influenced by
mechanical considerations tied to the idea that the planets were
carried on material spheres. In the Tychonic picture, Mars would
at times pass through the sphere of the sun, a difficulty
Swerdlow thought Copernicus could not accept, thus leading him to
the more daring heliocentric scheme with a moving earth.

I do not find Swerdlow persuasive on this point. It is true that
in Book 1 of De Revolutionibus Copernicus seems at one moment to
treat the Tychonic system as a possibility; however, he
immediately emphasizes the advantages of his own system in terms
that, to my mind, do not support Swerdlow’s mechanical
explanation. I am inclined to take Copernicus’s writings at face
value and to think he was mainly drawn by the internal harmony of
his model, as reflected in his qualitative anticipation of what
later became Kepler’s Third Law. In the Tychonic system the neat
ordering of the earth’s orbital period between those of Venus and
Mars is lost and some of the undesirable heterogeneity of the
Ptolemaic scheme is reintroduced. Like many commentators, I also
see no reason why Neoplatonic sympathies would not have inclined
Copernicus to place the sun in the ideal central position for the
planetary system. Swerdlow gives little weight to such motives.

Nevertheless, it is difficult to decide precisely what either
Copernicus or Ptolemy believed about the physical mechanisms
behind planetary motion. At minimum, Osiander’s preface to De
Revolutionibus — a statement reflecting views widely held at the
time — suggests considerable scepticism about specific hypotheses
concerning celestial motions and their mechanisms. Swerdlow
argues that the mechanical aspect is not prominent in either the
Almagest or De Revolutionibus because both works are primarily
concerned with mathematical description; he contends this does
not imply a lack of support for mechanical mechanisms. My
impression is that in both books the mathematical description is
primary: both authors concentrate first on finding what they
consider a satisfactory mathematical description — for Ptolemy
"satisfactory" meant simple, a point he emphasizes, while for
Copernicus it meant consonant with uniform circular motion — and
only after the mathematics is settled might one attempt, if
inclined, to construct a mechanical model that reproduces the
mathematics. This is what Ptolemy appears to have done in his
Planetary Hypotheses.

This insistence on mathematical description by Hipparchus,
Ptolemy, and Copernicus was decisive for the later discovery of
dynamics; thus their attitudes toward mechanical spheres are less
significant than their commitment to careful observation coupled
with rigorous mathematical formulation. That attitude alone
finally made dynamics possible, and it was rare before the late
seventeenth century. Still, Swerdlow is right to stress that the
decisive "destruction" of solid celestial spheres — prompted by
Brahe’s observations of the comet of 1577, which suggested it
passed through successive planetary spheres — stimulated Brahe
and, especially, Kepler to rethink what made the planets move.

Whether or not Copernicus truly believed in material spheres, his
system does evoke old threshing machines: wheels of many sizes
with drive transmitted from one part to another. The correlations
in his scheme are often bizarre, particularly for the inner
planets, which involve circles turning at twice the rate of the
earth’s annual motion. So one naturally asks: what controls it
all?

A modern reader approaching De Revolutionibus expects everything
to revolve around the sun as the linchpin. But the preface to the
pope already contains hints that this is not the case. For
example, Copernicus writes: "In the first book I set forth the
entire distribution of the spheres together with the motions
which I attribute to the earth, so that this book contains, as it
were, the general structure of the universe. Then in the
remaining books I correlate the motions of the other planets and
of all the spheres with the movement of the earth so that I may
thereby determine to what extent the motions and appearances of
the other planets and spheres can be saved if they are correlated
with the earth's motions."

Most revealing — and disorienting for the modern reader — is that
in the diagrams Copernicus uses to explain planetary motions the
sun is not shown, and its position is not easy to determine.
Everything is centred instead on the centre of the orbis magnus.
You search in vain for the engine that drives the Copernican
mechanism: it is not the sun. The planets move within the spatial
framework defined by the stars. Saturn moves slowest not because
it is farthest from the sun, but because it is closest to the
fixed stars — an echo of the ancient Aristotelian and medieval
idea that perfect rest most closely approaches the divine ideal.
The planets perform for our delectation; the sun does not cause
or govern their motion but serves as a lantern hung in the middle
to illuminate the dance and the temple that contains it. As
Copernicus puts it: "At rest, however, in the middle of
everything is the sun. For in this most beautiful temple, who
would place this lamp in another or better position than that
from which it can light up the whole thing at the same time? For,
the sun is not inappropriately called by some people the lantern
of the universe, its mind by others, and its ruler by still
others. Hermes the Thrice Greatest labels it a visible god, and
Sophocles’ Electra, the all-seeing."

In this Neoplatonic exaltation of the sun — a rare flourish in
a text otherwise as sober and restrained as the Almagest
— Copernicus allows himself one sentence that suggests a greater
role for the sun: "Thus indeed, as though seated on a royal
throne, the sun governs the family of planets revolving around
it." Yet despite Kepler’s generous remark that Copernicus, "when
he speculated," shared the view that the sun actually controlled
planetary motions, Copernicus’s book as a whole does not support
that claim. It demonstrates the earth’s mobility, not the sun’s
dominion.

### 5.8. Significance of Copernican revolution: second evaluation

Several commentators, including Kuhn and Koestler, tend to
emphasize the unrevolutionary and even archaic aspects of
Copernicus's system: although he eliminated one set of epicycles
he introduced another, and ended up with a model that was, on the
whole, as complicated as Ptolemy's and no more accurate. This
emphasis, however, does not sufficiently distinguish the two
parts of the planetary problem — the theories of the first and
second inequality. The Copernican revolution concerned the second
inequality; his conservatism applied to the first inequality,
where he made no positive contribution.

Viewed one way, what Copernicus achieved was small, almost
a pinprick. But he executed it extremely well. Concerning the
Earth's motion, De Revolutionibus conveys the central point with
clarity. The preface addressed to Pope Paul III is particularly
effective, even artful, in preparing the reader for a momentous
announcement. On the main claim Copernicus made, he indeed
delivered. He complained to the pope that his predecessors could
not "elicit or deduce from the eccentrics the principal
consideration, that is, the structure of the universe and the
true symmetry of its parts" (emphasis in the original). If you
had asked Ptolemy what the universe looks like from Saturn, he
would have had no sound theoretical answer. Ptolemy did not know
Saturn's distance; his solution of planetary motions suffered
from scale invariance and his trigonometry could not overcome
that. By extending trigonometry, Copernicus provided a tool that,
in his imagination, allowed one to travel through space: one
could deduce how the world appears from unvisited points at vast
distances from Earth. Historically, Copernicus accomplished this
task. He explained his insight in clear language for
contemporaries and, for specialists, supplied the mathematical
arguments to support his vision. For the first time in history,
people had a tolerably accurate picture of the solar system and
some grasp of the universe's vastness. Unlike the early Greek
hints of heliocentricity, Copernicus's picture rested on
substantive observation and theory (even if the main innovation
was largely the relocation of the linchpin).

The defects of Copernicus's system were confined to secondary
effects and stemmed from his peculiar refusal to accept the
equant at face value and his excessive reliance on others'
observations. This led him to reintroduce a remarkable profusion
of epicycles. Nevertheless, there is a clear criterion showing
how major his achievement was. We have already noted the far
greater coherence of the Copernican system in terms of gross
features. Kepler also pointed out that, in the Ptolemaic model,
the paths of the planets in geoastral space are never-ending
spirals that wander widely. Consider Kepler's drawing of Mars's
orbit, based on Brahe's observations from 1580 to 1596, produced
under the assumption that Earth is at rest — that is, the orbit
of Mars in geoastral space (the zodiacal signs appear around the
rim). Kepler describes the pattern as in figura panis
(quadragesimalis — in the shape of a Lenten bun, or Fastenbrezel
in German). The intricate pattern comes from superposing the
Ptolemaic epicycles on top of the deferent motion. The eternal
tangle of spirals arises from the noncommensurability of the two
motions: the Earth's orbital period bears no simple ratio to
those of the other planets. By contrast, the largest Copernican
epicycles all had periods that were simple multiples of the
dominant motion; consequently the Copernican orbits were
essentially fixed in helioastral space. The many corrections
Copernicus added only shifted the planets slightly about their
mean orbits. Those deviations were not especially striking in
magnitude; what seemed eccentric was the mechanism producing
them. The actual orbits produced were quite accurate and deviated
only slightly from the true fixed positions. This was
a tremendous gain in clarity.

In that sense, it is irrelevant to fault the remaining parts of
Copernicus's work or to criticize his theory of the first
inequality. He published all his work in one book, which tended
to blur the distinction between his revolutionary insight and his
conservative repairs. Copernicus himself partly contributed to
this confusion: he suggested that his real revolution — whose
magnitude was clear to him, though he could not have foreseen all
its consequences — had a significant bearing on his theory of the
first inequality and would help eliminate the unwanted equant.
But, as we have said, it had no effect on that problem.

One particular fascination of the Copernican revolution — which
was indeed a cataclysmic event — is that it rested entirely on an
already developed theory. It was not preceded by any major
observational discovery by Copernicus himself. It was a pure
reinterpretation of long-known data, and that is likely what gave
it so much dramatic impact. The history of physics offers only
one comparable event: Einstein's creation in 1905 of special
relativity. Like Copernicus's proposal, Einstein's was
a reinterpretation — in his case, of Maxwell's electrodynamics.
One deft conceptual move in each case overturned a world-view
held for millennia as an instinctive article of faith. Both
revolutions are appropriately tied to relativity in a kinematic
or Galilean sense. Planck was right to say that Einstein would
come to be regarded as the Copernicus of the twentieth century
for his 1905 paper; the comparison is apt.

Another fascinating aspect, which we will examine in the
following chapters, is the Copernican revolution's consequences
for the theory of motion, celestial and terrestrial. For
celestial motion it led to what may be called the second or
Keplerian stage of the Copernican revolution, which the next
chapter will treat. For terrestrial motion it undermined the
Aristotelian doctrine that natural terrestrial motions are
directed toward or away from the center of the Earth, assumed to
coincide with the universe's center. Here the diurnal rotation of
the Earth — which in De Revolutionibus is almost an incidental
remark compared with the astronomical importance of the annual
motion around the Sun — revealed its full dynamical implications.
In the chapters on Galileo and Huygens we will see how the mere
hints forced on Copernicus by his discovery, and anticipated to
some extent by Oresme, led to subsequent revolutions in the
science of motion as dramatic and surprising as the original
revolution. If Galileo's and Kepler's work can be seen as second
stages of the Copernican revolution, they still only partly
removed the Aristotelian division between heavens and Earth. In
their lifetimes Copernicus's proposal remained a flimsy arch
between two distinct parts of the universe. The final removal of
that gulf constitutes the third stage of the Copernican
revolution: at the philosophical level this readjustment was
largely Descartes's work; at the level of a usable dynamical
theory of motion it was Newton's.

Copernicus himself had far less idea of the full significance of
his revolution. This is not surprising: clear and essentially
correct ideas about geometry had crystallized in ancient times,
whereas the corresponding conceptual crystallization for motion
did not occur until well over a century after Copernicus's death.
Indeed, the Copernican revolution was the single most important
event in bringing about that crystallization of concepts of
motion.

As preparation for the next chapter, it is worth reviewing
exactly why Copernicus produced a theory in which the Earth was
mobile in a heliostatic sense rather than truly heliocentric in
the dynamical sense used here — that is, heliocentric in the
sense that the Sun actually controls planetary motions rather
than merely serving as a static center for describing them. The
oddity of Copernicus's revolution has already been hinted at. It
arises from the relative sizes of two solar effects: the
circularity of the planetary orbits and the small-eccentricity
corrections to zero-eccentricity behavior. The first effect is
much larger and more striking, and by itself was sufficient for
the Copernican revolution. Historically it was an accident that
Ptolemy discovered the equant while Copernicus discovered
heliocentricity. Judged by the magnitudes of the corresponding
effects, the two discoveries logically should have occurred the
other way round. In Chapter 3 it was suggested that the great
successes of the Ptolemaic system probably explain why Ptolemy
did not adopt heliocentric ideas: his system worked so well that
there seemed little left to do.

Another reason Ptolemy may not have discovered heliocentricity is
that the clear signal of zero-eccentricity behavior is obscured
by the small-eccentricity effects, which displace the centers of
the orbits from the Sun. Instead, those centers are scattered
around the Sun in a seemingly random way, some at considerable
distances (Saturn's center lies over halfway from the Sun to
Earth's orbit). Considering only the centers, it looks as if the
Sun just happens to be in the same region as the orbital centers,
and there is no apparent causal connection between the Sun's
position and the various centers. This aligns with the modern
understanding: the centers of planetary orbits are determined by
initial conditions, not by the law of gravity. The Sun's true
significance, however, is revealed by a subtler phenomenon — the
equant. Planets move nonuniformly in their orbits but, to first
order, they move uniformly about their equants. These equant
points are as unambiguously determined by observation as the
orbit centers (and it is nontrivial that such points exist). The
planets' recognition of the Sun's lordship appears because, for
each planet, the line joining the equant and the orbit center
passes through the center of the Sun; furthermore, an equant's
eccentricity is exactly twice that of the center. The
choreography is subtle: the Sun's influence is expressed in a way
not immediately visible.

Thus the irony is this. In the equant phenomenon, Ptolemy
actually uncovered a smaller manifestation of the Sun's dominion,
but wrapped in geocentric terms. Because it disturbed the older
geometrokinetic harmonies, Copernicus tried to eliminate this
hint of a new order. In doing so he stumbled on the more obvious
effect of the Sun's dominion: approximate circular orbits about
the Sun. Yet he was misled by the particular values of the
eccentricities. He could not accept the equant phenomenon and
therefore had no inclination to incorporate it into his new
heliocentric picture. Ptolemy's theories, which no one had
bettered, suggested a system organized about the center of the
orbis magnus; why should Copernicus contradict that if the
geometry still fit? As long as the geometrical relations matched
observations, Copernicus had no motive to improve on Ptolemy.

As Kepler remarked, "Copernicus merely set himself the task of
reproducing Ptolemy." It did not occur to him that the equant
might be a direct consequence of the Sun's presence and that one
should seek a specifically heliocentric explanation of it.
Instead, he turned to mechanical contrivances: cogwheels and cam
shafts.

Thus the old obsession with the equant narrowed his vision. The
astonished Copernicus — a student at Padua a century before
Galileo taught there — opened a door at night into a medieval
courtyard and found the courtiers dancing, with Mother Earth
among them. Dazzled by the spectacle and instinctively applying
the pedestrian rules of his outdated training, he failed to
perceive the new rhythms and effectively reduced the lord of the
dance to a mere lantern. What he achieved with one hand he almost
undid with the other — yet it would have been superhuman to have
done better. He performed his task, prompted by the right effect
but for the wrong reason. He opened a door into a new world; he
revealed enough that the door would never be closed again.
Aristarchus had probably only peeped through the keyhole.
