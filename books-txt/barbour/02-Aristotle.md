###  1. Brief review of the period up to Aristotle

The scientific revolution was a unique event created out of
the activity of two great periods.

- Period 1: lasted from 600 BC until the first centuries AD
- Period 2: began in the high Middle Ages.

- The first was almost entirely associated with Greek culture
  which became widely diffused by Alexander the Great (356-323
  BC). It is convenient to divide Greek history into two periods:
  before and after Alexander. The second period is generally
  referred to as the _Hellenistic age_ and will concern us in the
  next chapter.

The 'pre-Socratic' philosophers dominated the first two
centuries of the pre-Alexandrian period (600-400 BC). They
are noted for their bold philosophical speculations, but
unfortunately there is not much first-hand information about
them. The following notes are based on the Dictionary of
Philosophy.[^1] For fuller accounts see Barnes[^2] and Kirk,
Raven, and Schofield.[^3]

The best known figure of the early period was _Pythagoras_
(circa 572-497 BC). He founded a philosophy school and
religious brotherhood that flourished until the end of the
fourth century BC. Although the Pythagorean theorem is
associated with him on dubious grounds, his school did make
important discoveries in harmony and mathematics (irrational
numbers). Pythagoras is held by some[^4] to have been the
most important influence responsible for the scientific
revolution because of his emphasis on _geometry and numbers_.

The Pytagoreans held that all matter is literally composed
of numbers and that _numerical ratios underlie all sensuous_
phenomena. For them mathematical harmony was the sole
reality behind the visible universe. They had a great
influence on Plato and the early giants of the scientific
age: Copernicus, Kepler, and Galileo.

Independent of the Pythagoreans was the Milesian or _Ionian_
(Turkey) school of philosophy. This was developed in the
sixth century BC by Thales, Anaximander, and Anaximenes.
Thales reputedly predicted an eclipse of the sun in 585 BC.
The Milesians thought a _single elementary matter_
underlies all transformations of Nature:

* Thales declared this primordial matter to be water;
* Anaximenes said it was air;
* Anaximander identified it with 'the unbounded'.

The next major figure, a generation later, was
_Heraclitus_ (circa 536-470 BC) from Ephesus (Ionia,
Turkey). In opposition to the Milesians, he held that
nothing in the world persists. There is merely a _constant
and ceaseless flux_. Change is the only reality; the
appearance that things persist is an illusion created by the
ordered manner in which change takes place.

    He foreshadowed the modern realization that the laws of
    nature, describing _the way_ things change, are more
    important than what is actually changing.

The next step was the reaction to Heraclitus, which was
taken by _Parmenides_, who founded the Eleatic (of Elea,
Italy) school and whose life spanned the sixth and fifth
centuries BC.

* Whereas Heraclitus put all emphasis on change and becoming,
* Parmenides insisted on the supremacy of _being_.

However, his was not simply a return to the Milesians, for
he took his notion to its logical and paradoxical extreme.
He held there is only the one being without inner
differentiation and that _change and the apparent diversity
of things is an illusion_.

He was followed by Melissus (of Samos; active around 450
BC), who believed in a One that is eternal, motionless and
without change, and by Zeno of Elea (circa 490-430 BC),
whose famous paradoxes try to prove motion is an illusion.

The final phase of the pre-Socratic period is marked by
attempts to reconcile the opposing views of Heraclitus and
the Eleatics. _Empedocles_ of Agrigentum, Sicily (circa
490-430 BC) developed the idea that all individual things
are produced by mixing four elements:

1. earth
2. air
3. fire
4. water

The elements remain the same, things arise from their mixing
in different proportions. This seems a quite modern idea but
one cannot say the same of his idea that love and hate cause
motion and therefore bring about the mixing of the elements.

An alternative reconciliation of Heraclitus and the Eleatics
was attempted by the _atomists_ from Abdera (Thrace,
Greece):

* Leucippus (active around 450 BC)
* Democritus (circa 460-360 BC!).

Leucippus retained Parmenides' idea that there is just one
being, of itself completely homogeneous, but he supposed
that it was _broken up into infinitely many pieces, or
atoms_, of different shapes and sizes, and that their
combination gave rise to all the variety of individual
bodies. This atomic hypothesis was made famous by
Democritus. The main source of knowledge about ancient
atomism, especially in the Epicurian form, is the famous
poem of Roman poet Lucretius (circa 95-55 BC) _De Rerum
Natura_ (On the Nature of Things).

The atomic hypothesis is important in marking an early step
towards the concept of _absolute space_. The point is that
Leucippus not only broke up the homogeneous block of
Parmenides's being into atoms but also assumed that these
pieces of being are separated by that which is non-being or
void, i.e., empty space.

This introduction of _empty space_, as that in which the
atoms can move (mobility is evidently crucial for the atomic
hypothesis), was the outcome of a sophisticated
philosophical debate, which is well described by Bailey.[^5]

The void is not nearly as obvious as it might appear to the
modern mind. Leucippus defined the void, or emptiness, by
contrast with what is real and tangible. If we stretch out
our hand and feel something tangible, then that is matter;
but the lack of this positive sensation is also in its way
real. Thus, nothing (= no-thing) has a kind of reality too.
It should be said that the atomists developed only vague
spatial concepts, getting little further than the idea of
emptiness between the atoms. The reader is especially
recommended the early selections in Capek's 'The Concepts of
Space and Time'.[^6]  This vagueness of the atomists on
spatial concepts was an important stimulus to Aristotle.

The last of the pre-Socratics is _Anaxagoras_ of
Klazomenai (Ionia), who settled in middle age in Athens and
was active around 430 BC. He taught that there was an
_infinity of simple substances_, divisible into parts, as
in the atomic hypotheses. All becoming is due to their
combination and separation. To explain the motion of the
parts he assumed the existence of a 'soul-substance' that is
itself in motion and can set the normal substances in
motion. In contrast to the atomists, Anaxagoras accorded
_purpose_ an important role in nature. However in practice
he did not develop this idea very much. Aristotle took it
much further - _teleology_ was the supreme principle in
nature for him.

In philosophy, the century before Alexander was dominated by
Athens and its three great philosophers:

* Socrates (circa 470-399 BC)
* Plato (circa 427-347 BC)
* Aristotle (384-322 BC)

For the development of science, Plato and Aristotle were the
most important, since they shaped the general climate of
thought. The two men had very different attitudes to the
material world. For _Plato_ it was largely an illusion; he
distrusted the senses and held that true reality resided in
eternal immaterial forms. On the other hand, he greatly
emphasized the importance of mathematics, especially
geometry. In 387 BC he founded a school of mathematics and
philosophy in Athens, which became known as the Academy
(after of Plato's garden 'Akademos' where the school was
held). His works have been well preserved. The part of
Plato's philosophy distrustful of the material world
influenced the Christian world through the writings of St
Augustine (AD 354-430), and may have been a factor in the
decline of science during that period. In contrast, the
mathematical and geometrical aspect of his teaching was very
influential in the 16th and early 17th century.

_Aristotle_ was much more 'realistic' than Plato. He took
the observed world at face value and was more empirical. The
seeds of the scientific revolution was initiated in Western
Europe in the 13th century by the rediscovery of his works
(as lecture notes with later interpolations). It was his
cosmology and concepts of motion that provided the framework
of scientific thought up to the time of Galileo and this is
why a chapter needs to be devoted to his ideas, which have
a major bearing on the _absolute / relative debate_.
Indeed they amount to the first full-scale airing of this
question and there are many parallels between his writings
and discussions of the problem in the 17th century.

Like Plato, Aristotle founded a school of philosophy in
Athens, called the Lyceum, at which he taught from 335 BC
for twelve years. The school was also known as the
Peripatetic, apparently because Aristotle liked to lecture
in a covered walk, or peripatos. The 'Peripatetics'
dominated philosophy in European universities from 1300 to
about 1650. Aristotle is also known as the Stagirite, from
his birthplace in Stagira. His works constitute the oldest
extant treatises that are informed by an essentially
scientific spirit.

The period before Alexander left few solid results in
natural science. In general it was too speculative and
without empirical basis, apart from the study of the
_geometry_ of the natural world. Almost all of Aristotle's
output was purely qualitative and much was wrong. The
period's most permanent achievements were in mathematics,
especially geometry, which laid the foundations of later
advances in:

* astronomy beginning in the Hellenistic period
* in statics, also in the Hellenistic period, through the
  work of Archimedes (circa 287-212 BC)
* and much later, in dynamics in the early 17th century with
  Galileo (1564-1642)

Thus, the main importance of the first period of Greek
history was the beginning of rational enquiry into the world
and the creatiion of mathematics, which has proved to be the
_sine qua non_ of all advance in the study of motion.


### 2. Aristotle: the man and his vision

In the history of dynamics, Aristotle is often regarded as
retarding its development. He is generally compared
unfavourably with Plato and the pre-Socratics. This
judgement is too facile and is encouraged by the rise of
science in the 17th century which coincided with the
overthrow of Aristotelianism. However one could argue that
a qualitative Aristotelian phase had to precede any
quantitative development of dynamics.

There is one crude way of estimating Aristotle's
contribution. Newton postulated three laws of motion and
very little resembling these laws is found in
pre-Aristotelian authors. On the other hand, antecedents of
all three occur in Aristotle's main works relating to the
problems of space, time, and motion.

It is true they were all subordinated to his supreme
teleological principle that[^7]:

> 'God and nature create nothing that does not fulfil
> a purpose'

and this was completely abandoned by Newton (at least as
a dynamical principle). However Aristotle's lofty teleology
was not itself a hindrance to scientific progress, being far
removed from the real world. God could see the overall
pattern and determine the final causes of things, but the
nitty-gritty details were delegated to _efficient causes_,
and it was with these that Aristotle's principles, like
Newton's laws, were concerned.

Aristotle was the great systematizer and he arrived at the
end of a period of intense philosophical speculation. He
perceived that his predecessors had not come to terms with
_motion_, with the partial exception of Plato. They hadn't
even considered the meaning of crucial words such as place
and space. Thus their concepts of motion itself were almost
nonexistent.

Moreover, in the enthusiasm generated by the discovery of
geometry, the atomists and Plato _tried to explain
virtually everything by purely three-dimensional geometry_.
[like modern Minkowski space-time?!] For a variety of
reasons Aristotle rejected this approach, according to which
the majority of the phenomena associated with motion,
change, and transformation have an explanation _in terms of
shape_. Instead, he asserted that motion must be accepted
as a primary phenomenon, not reducible to three-dimensional
geometry. We will see how consequential this assertion was.
In this respect Aristotle was more progressive than his
predecessors and in many ways a pioneer of dynamics.

With the benefit of hindsight, we see his biggest mistake
was not appreciating the _quantitative aspect_ of motion.
Plato and the atomists had an exceptionally sharp
_three-dimensional_ vision. Aristotle was revolutionary in
having a _four-dimensional_ vision, but it was fuzzy and
qualitative. He could see a mountain range extending into
a further dimension and correctly discerned the valleys to
be explored, but there was no sharp focus. Indeed, there
seems to have been no awareness that such focus might prove
crucial and put things in a different light. In this
respect, Aristotle was indeed a step backward.

> The deepest and most remarkable secret of dynamics, the
> connection between _force and acceleration_ (rather than
> velocity), was entirely hidden from his hazy vision;

It finally came to light when Galileo insisted the world is
as sharp and precise in four dimensions as Plato had seen it
in three. Thus, there were two essential aspects that fused
in the discovery of dynamics

1. quantitative mathematical exactitude
2. and _four-dimensionality_ (time)

Plato recognised the former, but it was Aristotle who
pointed the way into the enigmatic fourth dimension and made
_Chronos the world's arbiter_.

Besides his dynamic cast of mind, Aristotle is remarkable
for anticipating several Machian principles. His physics was
founded largely on epistemological Machian ideas. However
his thinking was here again flawed; it derives from his
incomplete awareness of the quantitative aspect of
existence. Writing in the period before geometry was fully
formalized, and before the systematic use of trigonometry,
Aristotle developed a concept of space and position that was
_topological_; only through the neglect of the metrical
and trigonometrical properties of space could he construct
an apparently consistent cosmology.

From inadequate conceptions he nevertheless constructed
a vision of great beauty and harmony. His predecessors
described a world of exactitude populated with sharp-edged
atoms. The doctor from Stagira saw things with a human eye.
His was not the world of sharp edges and mathematical
triangles; position was not a point in Euclidean space or
a mathematical abstraction for him. Position was place, the
place of wine within the bottle, the baby within the womb.
His principle was: each thing to its proper place. This gave
his philosophy its wombic reassurance and also the sense
that fulfillment is possible.

It also explains how his overall conception could survive
for so long despite being internally deficient and
contradictory. It was steadily undermined (without
collapsing)

* from _within_ by the application of trigonometry to
  astronomical problems

* and from _without_ by the astronomical discoveries made by
  this use of trigonometry.

We will look at the reasons that led Aristotle to his
physical and cosmological conceptions but first, a brief
outline of the whole.

_Aristotelian cosmology_ is finite and onion-like. At its
centre is the spherical earth which Aristotle held to be at
rest. (He was well aware of the earth's sphericity and gave
the first estimates of its radius) Above the earth extends
a region of air and fire, which reaches to a sphere that
carries the moon. Beyond this sphere are further spheres
which carry the sun and the five naked-eye planets: Mercury,
Venus, Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn. Beyond that comes the
sphere carrying the stars, which Aristotle held to be fixed
and unchanging. Beyond this outermost sphere, the
_ouranos_, there is neither space nor motion nor even
time. In short there is nothing.

All the spheres _rotate_. The outermost sphere has the
fundamental rate of rotation corresponding to the daily
rotation of the earth (as Copernicus recognised). Because
its rotation seemed more fundamental and regular than the
other spheres, the outermost sphere acquired particular
significance. It rotates about fixed poles, corresponding to
the poles of the celestial sphere. Within the ouranos are
numerous other spheres (over fifty), only seven of which
actually carry celestial bodies. They are all concentric
with the centre of the earth and the ouranos but rotate at
different rates about different poles. Aristotle took these
spheres from Eudoxus and their purpose was to explain the
irregular motion of the planets, sun, and moon relative to
the stars.

The Aristotelian cosmos was divided into two distinct
regions:

* the _sublunary_ region below the sphere of the moon
* the _superlunary_ region of celestial spheres above it.

The 'laws of physics and motion' (to use an anachronism)
governing these two regions were completely different. The
superlunary spheres were made of a different material and
moved differently: always circularly with perfect
uniformity. Aristotle named this material _aither_ (ether),
deriving it from a Greek expression for 'run for ever'.

Ether was deemed  unchangeable, incorruptible and became
known as _quintessence_, the fifth (quint) essence or
element, to distinguish it from Empedocles' four elements
which composed the sublunary region. (earth, air, fire, and
water)

A characteristic feature of Aristotles scheme is the
doctrine of:

1. proper places
2. natural motions

The _proper place_ of the element 'earth' is the centre of
the universe. Therefore a falling stone is explained by the
natural striving of 'earth', the predominant element in
stone, to reach the centre of the universe, which coincides
with the centre of the earth. Similarly, the proper place of
fire is on the circular periphery of the world, which
explains its striving to fly upwards.

In contrast to the incorruptible heavens, the sublunary
region is the domain of corruption and generation.
Aristotle's physics tried to explain all changes taking
place in the sublunary region and motion was only one among
many changes. Aristotelian physics really bears little
resemblance to modern physics. Its overarching principle is
that everything happens for a specific (and basically good)
purpose. Nature never does anything in vain. Thus the stone
falls for a purpose, to reach its proper place. Only in its
proper place is it fully real, or actual. This helps explain
the Aristotelian notion (difficult for the modern mind) that
all change and nto just motion, is a process of passing from
_potentiality to actuality_.

Aristotelian physics contains little quantitative or
mathematical aspects. It is more concerned with establishing
the essence of things and their causes. The analysis is
mainly verbal and logical. Much attention is paid to the
various categories of existing things and Aristotle poses
questions like:

* what is matter?
* what is motion?
* what is space?
* what is time?

We are ask these questions today but seldom from the same
point of view as Aristotle. The same is true of our notion
of causality. Aristotelian enquiry is summarised by
Clagett:[^8]

> The four causes can be explained by analogy with something
> artificially produced. A bed is a bed because:

> 1. it is made of wood (material cause),
> 2. in a given shape (formal cause),
> 3. by a carpenter (efficient cause),
> 4. for the purpose of providing slumber (final cause)

It was probably this logico-verbal aspect of Aristotelianism
that most hindered the development of science. However the
medieval study of motion in the form of commentaries on
Aristotle almost made the breakthrough to quantitative
science. Aristotle was a practical and down-to-earth person
(unlike the ethereal Plato), and he would have found it
implausible that the quantitative study of motion could
explain the multifarious qualitative changes he saw around
him. Even in modern times, some great physicists, above all
Schrodinger,[^9]  insist modern physics is still
comprehending only a portion of reality.

Aristotles work closest to the spirit of modern dynamics is
'_On the Heavens_' (usually known as 'De Caelo'). Together
with his 'Physics', this is our main subject. In it _he
treats motion as an autonomous spontaneous phenomenon_.
Thus, the heavens spin forever without intervention, stones
fall to the centre of the universe as if drawn by a magnetic
force, and fire similarly seeks the periphery of the cosmos.
Not surprisingly, most of the quantitative studies leading
to modern dynamics developed out of the conceptions outlined
in 'De Caelo'. In fact, Galileo still retained Aristotle's
notion of _natural motions_; he merely treated them
mathematically and quantitatively.

This shows that the _mathematical treatment_ of motion is
what is essential rather than any underlying concept of it.
In his 'Metaphysics' which appears to represent his later
thinking,[^10] Aristotle developed a scheme where all motion
is produced by an _active agent_. There is little change
regarding the natural motion of the four sublunary elements,
since they are moved by internal striving to reach their
proper places. But other sublunary motions are _violent_
or enforced (because the corresponding motion is not one to
the proper place) and are caused either by an animal agent,
or ultimately by friction between the nonrotating sublunary
region and the rotating heavens. This friction transmits its
motion to the corruptible part of the universe and keeps it
in a perpetual state of unrest.

But the heavens themselves are inspired to move by the
divine and Unmoved Mover, which in Christian theology is
God. Therefore the state of _perfect rest_ is the highest
ideal, achieved only by the divine. The heavens represent
the next best approximation. By moving circularly with
perfect uniformity, the 'ouranos' is perpetually in the best
possible place, nearest the divine. By executing perfect
uniform circular motion the heavens are drawn to the divine,
as a lover to the loved one.

To achieve this aim, Aristotle felt the heavenly spheres
needed guidance and assistance from ethereal spirits. These
spirits, which are absent in De Caelo, figured prominently
in medieval philosophy and helped sustain the notion that
the cosmos is an _animated organic structure_. Kepler
(1571-1630) still took Aristotle's spirits as a serious
possibility, though his mature work inclined to mechanical
or dynamical explanations.

Thus all Aristotelian motion ultimately derived from the
unchanging divine. It is interesting that Aristotelian ideas
about the heavens sprang from a _human deficiency_
analogous to the absence of a clock for timing terrestrial
motions. In this case it was the limitations of human vision
and the brevity of human life compared to astronomical time
scales.

Human vision is marvellous for the earth but it failed to
reveal that the heavens are not that different from the
corruptible earth. With minimal artificial assistance, the
human eye crosses the resolution threshold and can see, as
Galileo did, mountain ranges on the moon and continually
changing spots on the sun. So much for the unchanging
perfection of all things heavenly.

All that was needed was again a factor of about 5 or 10,
this time in the power of vision, and the world would have
changed out of recognition. Similarly, had Aristotle's life
span been lengthened by a similar factor and he had watched
the stars intently, he would have established their relative
movements among themselves, which would again have destroyed
his scheme.

But for Aristotle the stars swept above his head, never
appearing to change though always seemingly in a state of
perfect uniform motion around the centre of the earth.
Transferring to the stars themselves the sense of awe
humanity feels before the sight of the heavens, Aristotle
made them divine and quintessential and said of his
vision[^11]:

> '... it is the only way we can give a consistent account
> and one which fits with our premonitions of divinity'.

This vision, constructed by the naked human eye and
speculating mind, held for nearly two millennia until it was
destroyed during those nights in Padua in 1609 when Galileo
turned his telescope to the heavens. Seen in historical
perspective, the world has only just recovered from the
shock of discovering it is not nested within the
quintessential spheres that set a limit on our mortal
existence as well as space and time.

There is a lesson here for us too. Like Aristotle our
present conceptions are determined by our ability to '_see_'
and just as strongly by our '_premonitions_' of how the
world must be. Let us now look in a little more detail at
how Aristotle's ideas were formed.



### 3. Pre-Aristotelian geometrism

The revolutionary nature of Aristotle's approach to
_motion_ can be illustrated by looking at Pythagoras,
Leucippus, Democritus and Plato.

As noted in Sec. 2.1, the Pythagoreans believed matter is
actually composed of numbers.[^12]  This idea stems from
their representation of squared and cubed numbers. Thus,
a square number such as 9 was represented by three rows of
three dots forming a square. The cube number 27 was then
formed by three such squares on top of each other. Our very
words 'square' and 'cube' for such numbers derive from the
Pythagoreans, according to which all bodies could be built
up this way.

It is interesting that another early step towards _absolute
space_ can be found in this Pythagorean construction: it is
the space, or void, _between the dots_ representing numbers.
Evidence for this remarkable notion is found in
Aristotle:[^13]

> The Pythagoreans too asserted the existence of the void
> and declared it enters the heavens out of the limitless
> breath - regarding the heavens as breathing the very
> vacancy - which vacancy [void] "distinguishes" natural
> objects, as constituting a kind of separation and division
> between things next to each other, its prime seat being in
> numbers, since it is this void that delimits their nature

Turning to atomic theory developed by Leucippus and
Democritus, a standard summary is:[^14]

> Nothing exists but atoms and empty space; everything else
> is opinion. Only in opinion does sweetness exist, only in
> opinion bitterness, in opinion hot, cold, colour; in truth
> there exists nothing but atoms and empty space.

> The atoms are infinite in number and of infinite variety
> in shape. ... The differences of all things derive from
> the differences of their atoms in number, shape, and
> arrangement; there is no qualitative difference between
> the atoms; the atoms have no 'inner state'.

If we compare this with Newton's theory of the microscopic
nature of matter, the similarity is striking. As Newton says
in his Opticks:[^15]

> It seems probable to me, that God in the Beginning formed
> Matter in solid, mass, hard, impenetrable, moveable
> Particles, of such Sizes and Figures, and with such other
> Properties, and in such Proportion to Space, as most
> conduced to the End for which he form'd them. ...

> And therefore, that Nature may be lasting, the Changes of
> corporeal Things are found only in the various Separations
> and Associations and Motions of these permanent Particles.

So far the standpoints are remarkably similar. However the
real gulf between the atomists and Newton lies in ideas
about motion and here Aristotle played a linking role.

The ancient atomists never developed precise ideas about how
their atoms move. They had only one clear idea: that they
_fall_ (even this may have been an addition of Epicurus,
see Sec. 2.5):[^14]

> 'In eternal falling through infinite space, the larger
> atoms, which fall faster, collide with the smaller; the
> sideward movements and vortices that then arise are the
> beginning of the building of worlds.

> Innumerable worlds arise and pass away, next to each other
> and one after another'

Notice how vague the picture becomes ('sidewards movements
and vortices') in addition to the standard Greek error that
larger bodies fall faster than smaller. In Lucretius's great
poem on atomism, nearly four centuries after Leucippus and
Democritus, the description of atomic motions is still
vague, though the poem is often precise in other ways. Thus:

* the _geometrical_ aspect of atomism was clear,
* the _motional_ aspect was vague.

By contrast, Newton's Opticks continues:

> It seems to me farther, that these Particles have not only
> a VIS INERTIAE, accompanied with such passive Laws of
> Motion as naturally result from that Force,

> but also that they are moved by certain ACTIVE PRINCIPLES,
> such as is that of gravity.'

The first steps towards the discovery of _Vis inertiae_
(force of inertia) and the active Principles were actually
taken by Aristotle. In any case we continue with the
geometrical slant of Plato's understanding of the world.

Whereas Leucippus and Democritus tried to explain all matter
transformations by atoms of differing shapes and sizes,
Plato had a more definite vision. In his scheme, (Timaeus
[^16]) material bodies are built from triangles - those
simplest plane figures. He used them to construct the
Platonic (or perfect) solids which were 'molecular' bodies
that constitute the four elements:

* the tetrahedron for fire,
* octahedron for air,
* icosahedron for water,
* the cube for earth.

Thus the greater density of earth is explained by the dense
packing of the cubes while the penetrating power of fire
comes from its sharp pyramidal tetrahedrons:[^18]

> Let us consider how it is we call fire 'hot' by noticing
> the way it acts on our bodies by dividing and cutting.

> That its property is one of sharpness we all, perceive;
> but as regards the thinness of its sides and the acuteness
> of its angles and the smallness of its particles and the
> rapidity of its motion - owing to all which properties
> fire is intense and keen and sharply cuts whatever it
> encounters,

> these properties we must explain by recalling the origin
> of its form, how it above all others is the one substance
> which so divides our bodies and MINCES them up as to
> produce naturally both that affectation which we call
> 'heat' and its very name.

Aside: Plato derived the Greek word for 'heat' from the word
for 'mince up'.[^19]

Although some passages in Plato appear almost modern and his
mathematical approach is closer to modern physics than
Aristotle's (organic, qualitative, and teleological), it is
a fact that the fundamental properties of matter such as
mass, weight, momentum, and energy are not explicable by
three-dimensional geometry.

Instead they are _properties of the four-dimensional world
in which TIME is included as an essential dimension_. It is
precisely here that Artistotle makes a significant departure
from Plato and pointed the way forward. However it is worth
reflecting on the contribution Plato made to the development
of physics.

The inscription above Platos Academy is well known:

> 'Let no man ignorant of geometry enter here'

There is no doubt Plato put great emphasis on geometry and
this was his supreme contribution to the development of
science. A striking feature in the history of physics is how
mathematical theories were developed without any direct
application to the world and only later were found to be
essential for the correct application of physical processes.
These applications often occurred decades after the
mathematics had been worked out. (or millienia later in the
case of conic sections; ellipses, parabolas, and
hyperbolas). Wigner has written a famous essay on this
subject.[^21]

Thus, the importance of Plato is manifest[^*2] but his
mechanico-geometrical models of the elements and their
dynamical behaviour proved sterile. It was in other
applications that his emphasis on geometrical relationships
bore fruit. The most important of these occurred about 150
years after Plato's death when Hellenistic astronomers used
_geometrokinetic models_ to explain planetary motions.
This represented the first breakthrough to the laws of
motion. Later in the 16th and 17th centuries, Plato's motto
was again taken up by Copernicus, Kepler and Galileo and
geometry was their indispensable tool.


Only using Aristotle's topological concept of position (sec.
2.5), it would have been impossible to understand processes
above the surface of the earth. But Plato's emphasis on
exactness[^*3] encouraged Kepler and Galileo to expect
precision in motion: both in spatial relationships and also
in time. It was only then that terrestrial dynamics
progressed beyond the embryonic form of Aristotle and could
be unified with the advanced art of celestial dynamics.

Even so, science advances in curious ways. Kepler was
initially fired by a dream as geometrical as anything of
Plato's: he conceived of the relative spacing of the planets
in terms of the nesting of the five Platonic solids. To the
end of his life he regarded his (misguided) Platonic theory
more highly than his correct determination of planetary
motions, a discovery which finally blew apart the
Aristotelian world-view he too inhabited.




### 4. Aristotle's natural motions

> 'If you can look into the seeds of time And say which
> grain will grow and which will not'
> Macbeth, Act I, Scene 3

It is a difficult to know where to enter Aristotle's
self-contained interlocking world. However one of his most
interesting (and fruitful) innovations was the doctrine of
_natural motions_. Quite why Aristotle put such emphasis
on motion is unclear. Perhaps he thought the purely
geometrical considerations of his predecessors could not
explain some simple phenomena relating to motion. An earlier
quotation demonstrated the impression free fall made on him.
A great part of Book III in _De Caelo_ anlayses whether
weight and lightness can be explained in geometrical terms.
Aristotle concludes they cannot. His doubt mave have come
from the multiple contradictory explanations his
predecessors gave for the phenomenon of weight. In addition,
many of the explanations were mere hand-waving with logical
gaps. For example:[^24]

> 'There are some, e.g. Pythagoreans, who construct nature
> out of numbers. But to construct the world of numbers
> leads them to the same difficulty, for natural bodies
> manifestly possess weight and lightness, whereas their
> combination of monads cannot produce bodies nor possess
> weight.'

He is also critical of the vague use of motion made by the
atomists:[^25]

> When therefore Leucippus and Democritus speak of the
> primary bodies as always moving in the infinite void, they
> should say by what motion they move and what is their
> natural motion.

> Each of the atoms may be forcibly moved by another, but
> each one must have natural motion also, from which the
> ENFORCED MOTION DIVERGES.

> Moreover the original movement cannot act by force, but
> ONLY NATURALLY. We shall go on to infinity if there is no
> first thing which imparts motion naturally, but always
> a prior one which moves because itself set in motion by
> force.

One can see a qualitative anticipation of Newtonian
dynamics, with the natural motions of Aristotle
corresponding to inertial motion and diverging enforced
motion corresponding to the force-induced deviations from
rectilinear inertial motions.

However, instead of a single Newtonian inertial motion,
Aristotle has _several natural motions_, none of which
correspond exactly to the Newtonian. Nevertheless, the
tendency is in the right direction and for the first time
there is a potentially complete concept of motion. It was
precisely this concept, with one significant alteration from
Copernicus, that Galileo developed so dramtically.

Besides this partial anticipation of Newtons scheme, we note
Aristotle's conviction that concepts belonging to dynamics
can only be defined in dynamical terms. This is interesting
in connection with the tortuous history of the _mass
concept_, which plays an important part in Mach's
Principle. Aristotle's discussion of weight and lightness
anticipates Mach's clarification of the mass concept. Recall
the atomists tried to _reduce weight to size_. Bodies are
heavier simply because they are larger:

    the dynamical property weight is explained
    geometrically in terms of extension.

For example, bodies containing invisible pores, i.e., voids,
will be lighter than ones of the same overall size
containing no such pores. However Aristotle's arguments and
those of the people he criticises are flawed by straight
errors of fact. What comes out clearly is Aristotle's belief
that all such attempts are misguided and that a different
approach is needed.

In the Timaeus, Plato conjectures that the essence of the
elements lies in the _three-dimensional structure_ of the
bodies which compose them. However Aristotle dismisses this
approach and promotes _characteristic motions_ (falling of
earth, ascending of fire) as the defining properties of the
elements. Instead of making their motions the consequences
of their geometrical properties, his standpoint is that by
definition, earth is that which falls:[^26]

> * 'Let "the heavy" be that whose nature it is to move
>   towards the centre,

> * "the light" that whose nature it is to move away from the
>   centre,

> * "heaviest" that which sinks below all other bodies whose
>   motion is downwards,

> * and "lightest" that which rises to the top of the bodies
>   whose motion is upwards'

This definition establishes the ordering: earth, water, air,
fire. It is almost an operational definition of how to
obtain these elements and anticipates the _mass
spectrometer_, in which a fundamental dynamical property is
used to separate different substances.

Aristotle regarded his definition as an important insight
and a significant advance. He does not look for any deeper
explanation of weight and falling unless it is in
metaphysics. His attitude was the scientist should note the
existence of the characteristic phenomena, not explain them.
In this he was a precursor to Mach. The extent to which
Aristotle regarded _motion_ as primary is evident:[^27]

> Of bodies, some are simple, and some are compounds of the
> simple. By "simple" I mean all bodies which contain
> a principle of natural motion.

Thus, the essence of a _simple body is its principle of
natural motion_. Precisely the same principle is used today
to define and distinguish elementary particles.

We must now clarify those much abused Aristotelian terms
_potentiality and actuality_. When Aristotle called
'weight' the unrealized potential of a body to fall, he was
identifying an important physical property which he
correctly perceived as primary. Modern science does the same
when it says a body is electrically charged if capable of
being accelerated in an electric field. Newton himself was
Aristotelian in his definition of inertia. He described the
faculty by which any body remains in its state of rest or
uniform motion as a _potentia_.

It is worth noting the modern word 'potential', as in
potential energy, was deliberately created[^28] in the
Aristotelian sense (existing in a latent or undeveloped
state). In modern science, the expression _vis potentialis_
was first used in 1750 by the Bernoullis and Euler. The
concept of _potential function_, with many uses in
mathematics and physics, was introduced by Green in 1828.
Finally, the expression _potential energy_ was introduced
in 1853 by Rankine in explicit contrast to _actual
energy_. Actual energy was renamed kinetic energy by
Thomson and Tait, which obscured Rankine's Aristotelian
contrast of potential and actual.

A remarkable passage from Ptolemy's _Almagest_ highlights
how right Aristotle was to study motion itself. Having
declared that the mathematical study of celestial motions is
the only prospect for scientific understanding as opposed to
mere 'guesswork', Ptolemy states this also applies to
motions on earth:[^29]

> 'As for physics, mathematics can make a significant
> contribution. For almost every attribute of material
> nature becomes apparent from the peculiarities of its
> motion from place to place.

> [Thus one can distinguish] the corruptible from the
> incorruptible by [whether it undergoes] motion in
> a straight line or in a circle,

> and heavy from light, and passive from active, by [whether
> it moves] towards the centre or away from the centre.'


This is so close to the spirit of Galileo one wonders why
the scientific revolution did not take off then and there.
Is the reason perhaps that:

    the pursuit of pure understanding was the passion of
    such a small band of people?

The problem with Aristotle's philosophy was it could be
developed in two directions:

* 'upwards' in the direction of metaphysics, theology, and
  teleology

* 'downwards' in the direction of quantitative investigation
  of the precise way _potentials were actualized_.

Not surprisingly, medieval philosophers concentrated on the
'upward' direction. Aristotle himself gave little
encouragement to quantitative development of his physics.
Moreover, his espousal of _plenism_ as opposed to atomism
(the view the world is a plenum rather than a combination of
matter and void), forced attention on mechanisms where
motion is communicated from one body to another rather than
on the quantitative study of motion in its own right.

In fact this was a relapse into geometrism. From an
embryonic science Aristotle's _motionics_ degenerated into
mumbo-jumbo until Galileo injected just enough Platonism
into the fourth dimension to reveal its remarkable
properties and spell the end of the Aristotelian world-view.

But perhaps even committed scientists would agree the detour
through Aquinas and Dante (see Chapter 4) was worth the
candle.


### 5. The corruptible and the quintessential

We have already seen certain similarities between Mach and
Aristotle. Basically they stem from the same instinctive
reaction to the perceived world. They regarded it as
a reliable source of knowledge, to be extracted by careful
observation. In this respect however, Mach was more
single-minded and empirical than Aristotle.

Parallels can be seen between Mach's thoughts on the meaning
of Newton's First Law and Aristotle's discussion of a defect
in the atomists' motion in the void. His recognition of it
was a major factor that helped determine his own rival
scheme. His point was that in an undifferentiated isotropic
void, the same everywhere and _in all directions_, it is
impossible to conceive of any definite motions at all.

He notes that terms such as 'up' and 'down', 'left' or
'right', are purely relative in a geometrical space:[^30]

> The comparison of mathematical figures illustrates the
> point. For such figures occupy no real positions of their
> own, but nevertheless acquire a right and left with
> reference to us, thus showing that their positions are
> merely those we mentally assign to them and are not
> intrinsically distinguished by anything in Nature.'

The final words, 'not intrinsically distinguished by
anything in Nature', are typical of relationists and similar
passages are found in Berkeley, Leibniz, and Mach and also
Newton and Kepler. Thus in a vacancy (void), it is
impossible to conceive of motion that takes place in
a definite direction (as happens in the case of falling
under gravity)[^31]

> 'since no preference can be given to one line of motion
> more than to another, inasmuch as the void, as such, is
> incapable of differentiation'

And even more explicitly:[^32]

> 'But how can there be any natural movement in the
> undifferentiated limitless void? For qua limitless it can
> have no top or bottom or middle, and qua vacancy it can
> have no differentiated directions of up and down (since
> the non-existent can no more be differentiated than
> "nothing" can, and the void is conceived as not being
> a thing, but as mere shortage)'

The parallel with Mach's Principle becomes almost complete
when Aristotle argues from the undoubted existence of
falling of bodies that they must be falling to a definite
place.

Aristotle's position is as follows. We observe several
striking examples of natural motions that quite clearly
distinguish certain directions: these are the falling of
heavy bodies, the ascent of fire, and the circular motion of
the heavens. For him it is inconceivable that these
directions should somehow crystallize spontaneously out of
the atomists' void. They must be motions tending to some
quite definite goal.

Now these directions are not like the purely relative ones
of mathematical space just discussed; for:[^33]

> These terms such as 'up' and 'down' and 'right' and
> 'left', I mean when thus applied to the trends of the
> elements are not merely relative to ourselves. For in this
> relative sense the terms have no constancy, but change
> their meaning according to our own position, as we turn
> this way or that; so that the same thing may be now to the
> 'right' and now to the 'left', now 'above' and now
> 'below', now in front and now behind; whereas in *nature
> each of these directions is distinct and stable
> independently of us*.

> 'Up' or 'above' always indicates the 'whither' to which
> things buoyant tend; and so too 'down' or 'below' always
> indicates the 'whither' to which weighty and earthy
> matters tend, and does not change with circumstance; and
> this shows that 'above' and 'below' not only indicate
> definite and distinct localities, directions and
> positions, but _also produce distinct effects_.

The italics emphasize the double parallel with Mach:

* the _epistemological_ (in a featureless space there are no
  markers to which motion can be referred)

* and the _physical_ (the idea that places not only act as
  markers but also exert a causal effect, serving as centres
  of attraction).

Of course, the parallel is not complete: the motions are not
the same and the role of the markers is also different. In
Aristotle, there are two distinguished places:

1. the centre of his universe, occupied by the earth,

2. the periphery of his 'onion', where the shells of
   quintessence spin.

The earth and the quintessence mark these distinguished
positions, and thereby define definite goals for motion, but
for Aristotle the attractive power resides in the places
themselves, _not in the matter_ that occupies the places.

In this respect Aristotle is unphysical. Mach identified the
masses of the universe as the actual 'attractors'. One
should really they are the 'governors of motion' (governor
is Latin for steersman), i.e. the masses literally steer
inertial motion according to Mach's idea. Furthermore Mach
assumed their 'attracting' or 'steering' power is
proportional to their masses, a physical concept barely
hinted at in Aristotle.

Before describing Aristotles solution to defining motion in
a cosmological context, we consider the perpetual falling of
the atomists, since it is an example of the _detachment
hypothesis_ (Sec. 1.6). It also shows how deeply the
stability of our human environment influences thoughts about
space.

There is controversy in the field of classical philosophy
over who introduced the notion of _perpetual falling_
(see, Barnes's comments on atomism[^2]). It was believed to
originate with Leucippus or Democritus, but possibly
Epicurus introduced it, reacting to Aristotle's criticism
that atoms were not given definite motions.[^34] Whatever
the truth, the idea resulted in a paradox, one which should
not have persisted after Aristotle pointed out the absence
of distinguished directions in a void.

The atomists, wishing to explain our stable earth,
conjectured that originally no world existed at all.
Instead, only an immense void existed through which the
atoms fell, some faster than others, their collisions
creating innumerable worlds, both 'next to each other and
one after another'. One such world was supposed to be ours.
The detachment hypothesis is as follows. The actual world
the atomists lived in had an _Up_ and a _Down_. There was:

1. a _dynamical_ definition of up and down, through falling

2. a purely _geometrical_ definition, through the surface of
   the earth

Although the two agreed to a high degree of accuracy, the
atomists who postulated the '_falling_' did not perceive
there could be a _causal connection between the two_.
Instead, they conceptually transfered the reference frame of
the earth onto the void. They removed the earth but left in
the void, the disembodied 'Up' and 'Down' that the
'earth-to-be-explained' had already defined for them. And
they explained the existence of earth by means of
a phenomenon we now know is caused by the very thing they
tried to explain i.e. the earth itself! Of course we can see
the fallacy so clearly because the atomists' 'world' has
long since been 'dismantled'.

The relevance of this to the Machian problem is highlighted
by Einstein, whose quote here follows the passage from the
Introduction (one wonders if Einstein knew his analogy did
in fact happen almost exactly as he imagined):[^35]

> The soundness of Mach's critique can be seen from the
> following analogy.

> Imagine a people constructing a mechanics who are confined
> to a tiny part of the earth's surface and cannot see the
> stars. They will ascribe special physical attributes to
> the vertical dimension of space (the direction of
> acceleration of falling bodies) and on the grounds of such
> a conceptual basis, will offer reasons why the earth is in
> most places horizontal. They might not be influenced by
> the argument that the geometrical properties of space are
> isotropic and that it is unsatisfactory to postulate basic
> physical laws with a preferred direction;

> They will be inclined (analogously to Newton) to assert
> the absoluteness of the vertical as something proved by
> experience and one simply has to come to terms with it.
> The preference of the vertical over all other spatial
> directions is precisely analogous to the preference given
> to inertial systems over other rigid co-ordination
> systems.

But now to continue with Aristotle's scheme. The spherical
universe was the solution to his problems, since in such
a universe distinguished places and motions are
unambiguously defined. The centre and surface of the sphere
are _distinguished places_, with circular motion around the
centre and linear motion towards and away from the centre
(downwards and upwards) being the _distinguished motions_.

Aristotle stresses that these are the only simple and hence
natural motions and constructs both his cosmology and
'chemistry' from them. As far as the sublunary world is
concerned, the basis of all terrestrial chemistry is built
from what goes 'up' (fire) and what goes 'down'
(earth):[^36]

> 'Every element has its proper motion, and the motion of
> a simple body is simple. But there is not an infinite
> number of simple motions, because the directions of
> movement are limited to two [up and down].'

It must be said that the intermediate elements, water and
air, must be introduced unnaturally into this scheme:[^37]

> the centre is contrary to the extremity, and the
> constantly falling body contrary to the rising. That there
> are two kinds of body, the heavy and the light, is thus
> conformable to reason, for the places are two, centre and
> extremity.

> But there is also the space between the two, which bears
> the opposite name in relation to each, for that which lies
> between the two is in a sense both extremity and centre.
> Owing to this there is something else heavy and light,
> namely, water and air.

In this respect Plato is more convincing with his perfect
solids. (Many Greek thinkers appeared to place great store
in contraries, and Aristotle often introduces them on shaky
grounds) This gives Aristotle his four terrestrial elements
but there is no point in detailing his theory of
transformation (generation and corruption). It is remarkable
how deeply he was _impressed by motion_ and how he
regarded motion as more significant than substance or shape.
This left an indelible mark on medieval and Renaissance
physics which eventually bore fruit.

More valuable for the evolution of dynamics was his theory
of quintessence (aither), the fifth element he postulated as
the substance of the celestial bodies and the spherical
shells that carry them.[^*4]  This was just an extension of
his theory of simple (natural) motions. Because Aristotle
always looked of intelligible concepts, he thought the
infinity of space cannot be comprehended. In contrast, the
circle which closes on itself is the paradigm of
intelligibility. Therefore circular motion about the centre
had two attractions for him:

1. it was self-contained (and therefore intelligible) and

2. if the periphery is regarded as a proper place, it has
   the wonderful property of keeping any substance that
   circles the periphery eternally in its proper place.

This was enough for Aristotle to postulate a fifth element
_aither_, and endow it with divine attributes. The following
is one of the passages which came to dominate centuries of
physical, philosophical and theological thought:[^40]


> From what has been said it is clear why, if our hypotheses
> are trusted, the primary body of all is eternal, suffering
> neither growth nor diminution, but is ageless, unalterable
> and impassive. I think that the argument bears out
> experience and is borne out by it.

> All men have a conception of gods, and all assign the
> highest place to the divine, both barbarians and Hellenes,
> as many as believe in gods, supposing obviously, that
> immortal is closely linked with immortal. It could not,
> they think, be otherwise.

> If then - and it is true - there is something divine, what
> we have said about the primary bodily substance is well
> said. The truth of it is also clear from the evidence of
> the senses, enough to warrant the assent of human faith;
> for throughout all past time, according to the records
> handed down from generation to generation, we find no
> trace of change either in the whole of the outermost
> heaven or in any one of its proper parts.

> It seems too that the name of this first body has been
> passed down to the present time by the ancients, who
> thought of it the same way as we do, for we cannot help
> believing that the same ideas recur to men not once nor
> twice but over and over again.

> Thus they, believing that the primary body was something
> different from earth and fire and air and water, gave the
> name aither to the uppermost region, choosing its title
> from the fact that it 'runs always' (aei \#eiv) and
> eternally. (Anaxagoras badly misapplies the word when he
> uses aither for fire)

The grandeur of such passages cannot be denied (though it is
interesting that the OED supports Anaxagoras with regard to
the etymology of aither) Part of Aristotle's popularity must
stem from the way he transfers the awe we feel before the
welkin onto the the firmament itself. Thus, we attribute to
quintessence all that we associate with our own moments of
heightened awareness. (In a notable passage from the
Dialogo,[^*5] Galileo points out that quintessence and the
unchanging celestial world would be excruciatingly dull)

But the history of dynamics is nothing if not ironic. On one
hand Aristotle put an unbridgeable gulf between the heavens
and the earth, but at the same time he attributed nearly all
the correct properties of inertial motion to the heavens, as
is clear from the following:[^42]

> The circular motion in question, being complete, embraces
> the incomplete and finite motions. Itself without
> beginning or end, continuing without ceasing for infinite
> time, it causes the beginning of some motions, and
> receives.the cessation of others . . .

> It suffers from none of the ills of a mortal body, and
> moreover . . . its motion involves no effort, for the
> reason that it needs no external force of compulsion,
> constraining it and preventing it from following
> a different motion which is natural to it. Any motion of
> that sort would involve effort, all the more in proportion
> as it is long-lasting.

It is not fortuituous that Aristotle ascribes the essential
properties of _inertial motion_ to the heavens, since the
observed diurnal motion of the heavens (which inspired the
above passage) is a consequence of the rotational, i.e.,
basically inertial, motion of the earth, while the proper
motion of the planets, sun, and moon is largely the result
of the inertial component of their motion. Indeed, the
Ptolemaic planetary system is an early expression of the law
of inertia although not normally interpreted that way. It is
incomplete but accurate in essentials.

There are three aspects of unadulterated inertial motion
that still evoke wonder, in ascending order:

1. the rectilinearity
2. the uniformity
3. the eternity

What made Galileo's introduction of inertia so breathtaking
and simultaneously amenable to mathematization was its
eternal uniformity. The revolutions of the heavens did
appear to persist for ever, but on the earth, in Aristotle's
words (echoed by the poets of all ages):[^43]

> 'time itself is destructive . . . things perish without
> anything being stirred, and it is a kind of perishing
> without apparent provocation that we especially attribute
> to time.'

Lost from view on the surface of the earth because of a host
of disturbing factors and the absence of a decent clock,
_the most fundamental law of nature_ (admittedly not yet
formulated in the Newtonian manner) finally forced its way
into Galileo's consciousness after Copernicus had proclaimed
the earth to be a planet. Locked in combat with the Church
and more or less forced to it by Copernicus's proposal of
the earth's mobility, Galileo pulled eternity down from the
heavens in an attempt to prove that the earth too could
move.

He realized the implication of the earth's mobility - that
through terrestrial perishing and decay there must after all
run a thread which persists for ever. He made us partakers
of eternity. (It is ironic that Hamlet called man the
quintessence of dust just as Galileo was taking our
'quintessential nature' seriously)

As we shall see, this was one of the decisive steps in the
eventual recognition of the _law of inertia_ and opened
the door to mathematization of motion (eternal uniformity
could be formulated precisely even before the development of
calculus). It also had the consequence that the law of
inertia was formulated in transcendental geometrokinetic
terms. _Mach's Principle is the attempt to recast the law in
physical terms, to shake off the residual geometrokineticism
which it inherited from Aristotelian metaphysics and ancient
astronomy._

One is left wondering if things had to take such
a roundabout route. Aristotle dismissed the myth of Atlas
because he said it arose from the mistaken belief that the
material of the heavens has weight and must therefore be
prevented from falling (according to his motion-based
physics and chemistry, only substances that fall have
weight). He went on:

> 'We must not then think in this way, nor must we say with
> Empedocles that it [the sky] has been kept up all this
> time by the cosmic whirl, i.e., by having imparted to it
> a motion swifter than that to which its own weight
> inclines it.'

Poor Empedocles. How near the truth he was. Two thousand
years later, Hooke would write to Newton, posing the problem
of the planets in strikingly similar terms.[^44]

This completers the review of the _five_ Aristotelian
elements and their natural motions. On earth these were
augmented by the concept of _violent motions_, enforced by
some agent (either an animated being or some body which
itself is kept in motion). It is ironic that the
quantitative description of such violent motion (which led
to Newton's Second Law) constituted the terrestrial physics
that Kepler learnt in Tubingen, and was imported by him into
the heavens to explain planetary motions just when Galileo
was importing celestial inertia into terrestrial physics to
understand earthly motions.

Ironic yes, but there is a logic in it too. Copernicus's
_mobile earth_ bridged the Aristotelian gulf between the
terrestrial and the celestial. Over half a century later,
two intrepid robbers each with a bag of booty, could be seen
crossing the void via that flimsy bridge. Kepler from the
earth to the heavens, Galileo from the heavens to the earth.
They exchanged a cordial word or two as they passed, but
each went resolutely on his own way.


### 6. The concept of place & the self-contained universe

The key to understanding Aristotle's cosmology is his
concept of place (topos). Aristotle presents the problems
associated with place and space very clearly in Book IV of
his Physics, from which it is worth quoting, particularly
because the problem of the nature of space figures
prominently in the debate about absolute and relative
motion. Aristotle opens as follows:[^45]

> The Natural Philosopher has to ask the same questions
> about 'place' as about the 'unlimited'; namely, whether
> such a thing exists at all, and (if so) after what fashion
> it exists, and how we are to define it. ...

> but we encounter many difficulties when we attempt to say
> what exactly the 'place' of a thing is. For according to
> the data from which we start we seem to reach different
> and inconsistent conclusions. Nor have my precursors laid
> anything down, or even formulated any problems, on this
> subject.

He sees the strongest argument for the reality of space in
the phenomenon of 'replacement':[^46]

> To begin with, then, the phenomenon of 'replacement' seems
> to prove the independent existence of the 'place' from
> which - as if from a vessel - water, for instance, has
> gone out, and into which air has come, and which some
> other body yet may occupy in its turn; for the place
> itself is thus revealed as something different from each
> and all of its changing contents.

> For 'that wherein' air is, is identical with 'that
> wherein' water was; so that the 'place' Or 'room' into
> which each substance came, or out of which it went, must
> all the time have been distinct from both of the
> substances alike.

Although as a plenist he does not believe in it, Aristotle
also mentions the argument from the void:[^47]  Further, the
thinkers who assert the existence of the 'void' agree with
all others in recognizing the reality of 'place', for the
'void' is supposed to be 'place without any thing in it.'

One might well from this that there must be such a thing as
'place' independent of all bodies, and that all bodies
cognizable by the senses occupy their several distinct
places. And this would justify Hesiod in giving primacy to
Chaos [ =the 'Gape'] where he says: 'First of all things was
Chaos, and next broad-bosomed Earth'; since before there
could be anything else 'room' must be provided for it to
occupy. For he accepted the general opinion that everything
must be somewhere and must have a place.

But Aristotle is not happy with the idea that void has
reality:[^48]  And if such a thing exists we might
contemplate it with wonder capable as it must be of existing
without anything else, whereas nothing else could exist
without it. Since 'place' is not destroyed when its contents
vanish. But then, if we grant that such a thing exists, the
question as to how it exists and what it really is must give
us pause. Is it some kind of corporeal bulk? Or has it some
other mode of existence?

His initial discussion of this , which is not particularly
illuminating, leads him to the conclusion that the 'mode of
existence' of space is extremely difficult to pin down, and
he concludes the chapter by remarking:[^49]

> 'after all, we are forced by these perplexities not only
> to ask what a "place" is, but also to reopen the question
> that appeared to be closed and ask whether there is such
> a thing as "place" at all'.

In coming to grips with this problem, Aristotle exhibits
a strongly posivitist frame of mind.[^*5]  He insists
instinctively thatthere must be something observable that
defines place. This leads him to his fundamental concept:
place, whatever may be its actual mode of existence, is
defined by a material container. His favourite example is
a vessel that may contain successively water, wine, or air.

Very characteristic is his assertion that the 'immediate
place' of a thing is the 'inner surface of the envelope
[i.e., container]'.[^51]  This is the rock on which
everything in his scheme stands or falls: unless there is
some defining container, all talk of place or space is
meaningless. This is why he needs a plenum.

However, this still leaves a number of possibilities for
what place actually is. We know a body occupies a place when
within a container. In answer to the question 'what is
place?' Aristotle then asserts:[^52]

> 'it must be either
>
> (i) the form or
>
> (ii) the matter of the body itself, or
>
> (iii) some kind of dimensional extension lying between the
> points of the containing surface, or
>
> (iv) the containing surface itself,. if there be no such
> "intervenient", apart from the bulk of the included body'

At least he can eliminate the first two possibilities. It is
clearly not the matter of the body itself (ii), since that
can be replaced (as we have seen). Nor can it be regarded as
the form of the body. For though 'that which embraces' may
suggest the moulding 'form' (since 'the limiting surfaces of
the embracing and the embraced coincide') and 'both the
"place" and the "form" are limits', they are not limits 'of
the same thing, for the form determines the thing itself,
but the place the body-continent'.

He finds possibility (iii), which Plato favoured by
advocating the identity of 'room' and 'matter', harder to
eliminate and admits:[^53]

> we see that what makes 'place' appear so mysterious and
> hard to grasp is its illusive suggestion now of matter and
> now of form, and the fact that while the continent is at
> rest the transferable content may change, for this
> suggests that there may be a dimensional something that
> stays there other than the entering and vacating quanta.

Air too is contributing to this last illusion since it looks
as if it were incorporeal - so that the 'place', instead of
being recognized as constituted solely by the adjacent
surface of the vessel, is held to be the dimensional
interval within the surface, conceived as 'vacancy'.

And he also says:[^54]

> 'it is no wonder that, when thus regarded - either as
> matter or as form, I mean - "place" should seem hard to
> grasp, especially as matter and form themselves stand at
> the very apex of speculative thought, and cannot well,
> either of them, be cognized as existing apart from the
> other'

In the face of these baffling questions, he falls back on
the _plenum_ and completely eliminates space as
a conceptual entity:[^55]

> But because the encircled content may be taken out and
> changed again and again, while the encircling continent
> remains unchanged - as when water passes out of a vessel,
> the imagination pictures a kind of dimensional entity left
> there, distinct from the body that has shifted away.

> But this is not so; for what really happens is that
> (instead of anything being left) some other body - it
> matters not what, so long as it is mobile and tangible
> -succeeds the vacating body without break and
> continuously.

There is no point in getting involved in the arguments for
or against the plenum. It was the subject of great
controversy both in the ancient world - in De Rerum Natura
Lucretius referred to the supporters of the plenum as
stolidi - and again in the seventeenth century. However,
this 'great debate' proved in the end to be an exercise in
shadow-boxing, the reason for which will be indicated
shortly.

For the purposes of the present discussion, let it be
accepted that for Aristotle the plenum was more or less an
article of faith. Having disposed of all the other
alternatives, Aristotle concludes that place is not only
defined by but actually is the inner surface of the
container: the envelope. The inner wall of the bottle is the
place of the wine, the water that touches its outside is the
place of the boat. However, this is only the immediate place
of the body. In another clear anticipation of Mach,
Aristotle puts the immediate place in a larger context: he
is careful not to fall victim to a detachment fallacy:[^56]

And so, too, a 'place' may be assigned to an object either
because it is its special and exclusive place, or because it
is 'common' to it and other things, or is the universal
place that includes the proper places of all things. I mean,
for instance, that you, at this moment, are in the universe
because you are in the air, which air is in the universe;
and in the air because on the earth; and in like manner on
the earth because on the special place which 'contains and
circumscribes you, and no other body'.

The hierarchical nature of place comes out explicitly in the
following passage, which completes Aristotle's account of
how we should understand place:[^57]

> And, from this point of view, if one thing is moving about
> inside another, which other is also in motion, as when
> a boat moves through the flowing water of a river, the
> water is related to the boat as a vessel-continent rather
> than as a placecontinent; and if we look for stability in
> 'place', then the river as a permanent and stable whole,
> rather than the flowing water in it at the moment, will be
> the boat's site. Thus whatever fixed environing surface we
> take our reckoning from will be the place.

So the centre of the universe and the inner surface of the
revolving heavens constitute the supreme 'below' and the
supreme 'above'; the former being absolutely stable, and the
latter constant in its position as a whole.

    He has found and defined the universal and ultimate
    frame of reference.

This might seem facile; for it appears that the outermost
heaven, the ouranos, should be regarded as 'placed' by the
void beyond it. In fact, the Aristotle problem presented by
the placing of the outermost sphere played a significant
role in the late medieval and Renaissance periods in
undermining the Aristotelian cosmos on the basis of purely
philosophical considerations.

Already in Plato's time Archytas, a Pythagorean, had posed
questions that we find recurring from the thirteenth to the
beginning of the seventeenth century (and, indeed, still put
by the layman to any relativist who attempts to explain the
concept of a closed finite world):[^58]

> If I am at the extremity of the heaven of the fixed stars,
> can I stretch outwards my hand or staff? It is absurd to
> suppose that I could not; and if I can, what is outside
> must be either body or space. We may then in the same way
> get to the outside of that again, and so on; and if there
> is always a new place to which the staff may be held out,
> this clearly involves extension without limit.

Equally famous is Lucretius's taunt:[^58]

> If for the moment all existing space be held to be
> bounded, supposing a man runs forward to its outside
> borders and stands on the utmost verge and then throws
> a winged javelin, do you choose that when hurled with
> vigorous force it shall fly to a distance, or do you
> decide that something can get in its way and stop it? for
> you must admit and adopt one of the two suppositions;
> either of which shuts you out from all escape and compels
> you to grant that the universe stretches without end.

Such criticisms of Aristotle's remarkable idea that there is
literally nothing beyond the outermost shell of his cosmos
strengthened and anticipated the even more destructive
evidence that was brought forward by astronomers and were an
important factor in preparing the human mind for the
transition from a closed world to an infinite universe.

With the benefit of hindsight, I do not suppose that
Aristotle's answer to the problem of the placing of the
outermost sphere will be found convincing by the reader. For
this, Aristotle would have had to anticipate non-Euclidean
geometry. However, an answer of sorts can be found in the
way Aristotle couples the problem of space so strongly to
that of motion. In fact what partially saves Aristotle's
universe from the objection that his ouranos must itself be
ultimately contained in the limitless void is that his
scheme is dynamically self-contained.

In fact, quintessence comes to the rescue. It is not so much
that Aristotle abolishes the problem of the 'place' of the
'supreme above', as that within his scheme the problem never
becomes acute since all substance at the 'supreme above'
moves circularly. The threat to the logical coherence of his
scheme from Archytas's question is simply ignored, but with
a semblance of logic and coherence since, in accordance with
Aristotle's own laws of motion, the outermost sphere spins
within itself and the laws of motion for the other elements
say explicitly that they have no cause to pass beyond the
ouranos; for all the places to which they strive are
contained within it.

Such thoughts are implicit in the following passages:[^59]

> It follows that if a body is encompassed by another body,
> external to it, it is 'in a place'; but if not, not. As to
> such an 'unplaced' body (be it water or anything else) its
> parts may be in motion, for they embrace each other, but
> as a whole it can be said to 'move' only in a special
> sense. For as a whole it cannot change its collective
> place. But it may have a motion of rotation, which motion
> is what constitutes the 'kind of place' with respect to
> which the rotating parts move. Now in the universe there
> are some parts which do not move up and down but do
> rotate; and others (such as are susceptible to
> condensation and rarefication) which can either rotate or
> move up and down . . . but the heavenly mass as has been
> said cannot change its place as a whole. Nor indeed has it
> a place to change, seeing that there is no body-continent
> embracing it. But after the fashion of its own motion it
> constitutes places for its own parts, since one part
> embraces another. . . .

> Heaven therefore 'rotates', but the universe has not
> a 'where', for to have a 'where' a thing must not only
> exist itself but must be embraced by something other than
> itself; and there is nothing other than the
> universe-and-the-sum-of-things, outside that sum, and
> therefore nothing to embrace it. . . . So earth is
> naturally surrounded and embraced by water, water by air,
> air by aether, aether by heaven, and heaven itself not at
> all.

It must be admitted that Aristotle's solution to the problem
of space and its mysterious nature has many attractions. By
defining _place_ as the envelope of the contained body and
insisting the world is a plenum, he was effectively able to
say that space does not exist at all and was able to reduce
everything to matter. Not only is matter everywhere, which
eliminates the embarrassment of the void, but by being
everywhere, matter is always available to define a universal
frame of reference as well as the immediate place. Moreover,
from an epistemological point of view, the diversity of
matter and its differentiation was welcome in lending
a certain precision to the concept of space - we can usually
say where one thing ends and another begins.

Where is the flaw in this self-contained whole? Undoubtedly
in Aristotle's _neglect of the metrical_, as opposed to
_topological_, properties of space. His concept of place
is at once fundamental but primitive.

* _Fundamental_ because it derives directly from the most
  basic of spatial concepts - contiguity, coincidence, and
  inclusion.

* _Primitive_ because it stops there and takes almost no
  account of the metrical properties of the world, which
  although secondary nevertheless have a very real practical
  existence and lead to the instinctive posing of questions
  like the one that Archytas put.

The defect with Aristotle's concept of place is that it
ignores a second and more convenient way of determining
position. This is the method in which position is determined
by using the metrical properties of Euclidean space, e.g. by
defining the position of a body by means of its distances
from certain _selected points of reference_. In this method,
the distances can be measured either directly, by rulers, or
indirectly, by means of trigonometry.

It is interesting to note that Aristotle, despite his
high-brow philosophy, also had a concept of a metric space
in the manner of Euclid buried deep in his psyche. For
metrical geometry does enter Aristotle's scheme in numerous
places, above all in the use he makes of straight lines and
circles to define natural motions. He also frequently talks
about distance without making the least attempt to show how
such a precise quantitative concept derives from his more
primitive topological concept of place.

But perhaps the most graphic evidence of his unconscious
belief in space is to be found in his firm belief that the
earth is at rest while the heavens spin.[^60]  For all that
one can observe is relative: there is absolutely no
objective criterion provided by anything in Aristotle's
concept of place that would enable one to say that it is the
heavens that spin rather than the earth. Indeed, in
Aristotelian rigour the notion that either is at rest
(nonrotating) is meaningless.

Aristotle is thus a classic example of the point made by
Maier and quoted in Chapter 1. Libe other philosophers, he was
capable of developing a sophisticated concept of place that
dispenses with space, but this broke down as soon as it
became necessary to treat problems involving motion. Here
instinctive man, with both feet on solid ground,
immediately comes to the fore and we are confronted with
what seems to us to be a schizophrenic failure to
link together the two aspects of geometry:

* the Aristotelian topological (and positivistic) concept of
  place and

* the Euclidean concept of space with its metrical notions
  adopted as true a priori - because self-evidently true!

Kuchar has pointed out that the coexistence of these two
concepts for two millennia, represented respectively by the
finite Aristotelian cosmos and infinite Euclidean space,
which were both developed with great precision in Greek
antiquity and lived cheek by jowl until the middle of the
seventeenth century, when the Euclidean concept finally
triumphed, is a most remarkable phenomenon. How were people
able to live for so long with such contradictory notions?

I think the answer must be that it takes the human mind an
extraordinary effort to piece together the disparate parts
of experience into a unified whole. Who knows, millennia
from now our descendants may smile at the way most modern
scientists live quite happily with a scientific picture of
the world devoid of all qualitative sensations, with which,
however, they are confronted in the most intense manner in
every instant of their conscious existence. I refer again to
Schrodinger's Mind and Matter.[^9]

One thing is suggested by the period from Aristotle to
Descartes - that clarity of philosophical argument and
mathematical intuition alone are not sufficient for
a fundamental change of concepts. Opposition to Aristotle
was frequently voiced, often with striking clarity, as for
example by John Philoponus in the sixth century AD:[^61]

> 'Place is not the adjacent part of the surrounding body.
> . . . It is a given interval, measurable in three
> dimensions; it is distinct from the bodies in it, and is,
> by its very nature, incorporeal. In other words, it is the
> dimensions alone, devoid of any body. Indeed, insofar as
> their matter is concerned, place and the void are
> essentially the same thing'

This concept of space is already extremely close to that of
Newton. Moreover, it reappeared numerous times in the Middle
Ages but never with enough strength to overthrow Aristotle.
(For a discussion of concepts of space in the Middle Ages
the reader is referred to Grant's Much Ado About
Nothing.[^62])

What was lacking, I suggest, was really hard and ineluctable
evidence, empirically based and expressed in mathematical
form, suggesting that there was something fundamentally
wrong with the Aristotelian scheme. _This evidence was
supplied by the astronomers_.

It is for this reason that this book traces how the
alternative metrical, above all trigonometrical, concept of
position steadily undermined the Aristotelian universe. Here
again the difference between the heavens and the earth is
most interesting. The point is that whereas on the earth
both methods, metrical and topological, can be used to
determine position, in the heavens the topological method
is with a very few exceptions entirely useless.

It can be used to determine the apparent position of
a planet on the heavens but fails to say how far away the it
is. It is interesting to note that in De Caelo Aristotle
only gives meagre information about the distances of the
planets and the size of his universe. In fact, he is content
to give an estimate of the size of the spherical earth and
to note the fact - established by the topological
observation of an occultation of Mars by the moon - that the
planets are further away than the moon. He does not even
attempt to estimate the distance to the moon.

We shall see shortly how quantitative study of the heavens,
first by the ancients and then by Copernicus and Kepler,
simply bypassed Aristotle's philosophy and step by step
reinstated geometry as an essentially quantitative science
of the real world.

This will also give us a key to understanding why one of the
greatest of all philosophical disputes, that between the
adherents of the void and plenum, generated a great deal of
smoke without materially affecting the actual development of
dynamics once it got into its stride. Because de facto
planets have no visible 'envelopes', Aristotle's concept of
position fails completely for them. Astronomers were forced
to use trigonometry and geometrical models to determine
planetary positions.

But this concentrated attention on trigonometric position
and change of position. Both were treated quantitatively of
necessity, since the raw data was gathered in quantitative
form and could only be evaluated this way. Thus, far more
effort was expended on establishing the precise motions of
the planets than on speculating about their origin and
whether the planets are moved by:

* the agency of a medium,
* by an active soul,
* or by an occult faculty such as gravitation.

This is true above all of Kepler, despite the fact that for
him physical speculation was a consuming passion and he
dabbled in all three explanations.

Thus Aristotle's physics lived on, a fleshy beast with no
clear contours, while astronomy, an austere science far
removed from the fleshpots of the world, was forced
willy-nilly to come to terms with the bony quantitative
reality of celestial phenomena. This may also explain why
Kepler, an astronomer by choice, made a more radical break
with Aristotle than Galileo, the astronomer by accident.

We cannot conclude without commenting on the remarkable
similarity of the closed universe constructed by Aristotle
and the one 2000 years later by Einstein. Both were
spatially spherical and infinite in both _temporal_
directions. (Einstein's _metrically closed_ universe was
intellectually more satisfying than the topologically closed
one of Aristotle based on inadequate concepts) Even more
striking is the fact that both were constructed for
essentially Machian reasons - _a desire to find a closed
circle of observable physical causes._

This point is of the greatest importance for the
understanding of Aristotle and the realization of what
ultimately defeated Einstein. In common with many Greek
thinkers, Aristotle abhorred the idea of the 'actual
infinite' As we have seen, he was always on the look out for
'intelligible concepts', ones that the mind could completely
grasp. He recoiled before the idea of an infinite universe,
and much of his De Caelo is devoted to proving that the
universe cannot be infinite.

The only form in which Aristotle could contemplate the
infinite was as a potentiality, for example, as the
potential possibility of infinite division of a finite piece
of matter into ever more and more pieces. Looking forward to
Vol. 2, it is worth noting that conscious rejection of the
infinite was probably one of the major factors that led
Aristotle to his closed finite cosmology in the first place,
whereas for Einstein realization of the danger posed by the
infinite came very late, in fact only after he had developed
his dynamical theory in its entirety.

As the story of the absolute/relative debate unfolds, we
shall see that the intervening discovery and systematic
elaboration of dynamics goes a long way towards explaining
the different perspectives of these two great men who
dominate the history of dynamics at its respective ends.

Another significant similarity between the cosmologies of
Aristotle and Einstein is both were blown apart by the
'beast' of dynamics. The Aristotelian vision of the
quintessential divine heavens was destroyed overnight, but
the Aristotelian universe itself lived for another half
century until Newton and his laws of motion burst that
cordon sanitaire which Aristotle had fended off the nagging
void. It was Newton's laws that let in infinite space, the
extension of the trigonometric solar system where Kepler had
uncovered ellipses, significant steps from the finite
Aristotelian circles to infinite conic sections stretching
forever into the void.

If Aristotle's spherical universe survived two millennia,
Einstein's barely lasted two months before De Sitter gave it
the coup de grace. Hamlet would have had as much pleasure
from the end of Einstein's universe as he had from the
demise of Guildenstern and Rosencrantz. Truly a case of the
'engineer hoist by his own petard'. Aristotle's universe was
blown apart by Newton's laws; Einstein's by Einstein's. We
shall come to all this anon.


### 7. Time in Aristotelian physics

In his discussion of time, Aristotle again exhibits reflexes
that can be called 'Machian' but, as in the case of space,
his positivistic philosophy exists alongside abstract
mathematical intuition.

This section introduces some remarkable empirical and
quantitative results about the nature of time discovered by
the Hellenistic astronomers. Innumerable phenomena give rise
to an awareness of the passage of time - the alternation of
day and night, the seasons, birth, growth and death.
Ariotti[^63]  makes the point that, apart from the Greeks,
the people living around the Mediterranean in antiquity:

> 'did not separate time from its contents . . . Time was
> not a neutral and abstract frame of reference. Time was
> its own contents.

> Events were not in time, they were time. Ancient Hebrew
> did not have a word for time, but for season, point in
> time, or eventful duration.'

This resulted in multiple times. Ariotti sees a significant
conceptual development in the fact that the Greek
theogonists and poets

> 'hypostatized time into a single entity, a cosmological
> principle or god: CHRONOS.'

In the great flowering of Greek literature, time plays an
all-mastering dominant role. For the emergence of the
science of dynamics, the transition from particular measures
of time to the concept of a universal and uniformly flowing
time was crucial. In this process, Plato and Aristotle mark
two important stages. Plato said that,[^64]

> 'with a view to the generation of Time, God brought the
> celestial bodies into existence 'for the determining and
> preserving of the numbers of Time'.

He seems to have come close to identifying the passage of
time with the motions of the heavenly bodies. On the other
hand, Plato regarded the world as a play of appearances and
didn't regard time as a concrete reality. There is also the
problem that the celestial bodies move at different apparent
speeds at different times. Which among them would define
a _unique time_ if such a thing even exists?

It was left to Aristotle and his commonsense attitude to
crystallize the concept of time that would endure until the
Middle Ages. Recall that for Aristotle motion was a primary
phenomenon. _However Aristotle did not conceive of motion as
taking place in time_. For him all changes are primary,
including the special case of motion. Aristotle, like the
medieval philosophers, was still closer to the primitive
concept that _time is not distinguished from its contents_,
unlike scientists of the post-Newtonian age.

As usual, Aristotle attempted to establish the nature of
time, a thing evidently as elusive as space. He is certain
of one thing:[^65]

> Time cannot be disconnected from change; for when we
> experience no changes of consciousness ...
> no time seems to have passed'

and again:

> 'Since, we are not aware of time when we do not
> distinguish any change ... it is clear that time cannot be
> disconnected from motion and change.'

As always, Aristotle tries to be concrete, as is evident in
his remark[^66]:

> 'when any particular thing changes or moves, the movement
> or change is in the moving or changing thing ITSELF or
> occurs only where that thing is.'

Some passages show that Aristotle would have preferred to
think in terms of concrete change and a succession of
states, or 'nows', dispensing entirely with an abstract
notion of time. However, he is prevented from this by two
things that in reality are but one:

1. the overwhelming sense of a passage of time that goes on
   _independently of any particular process_ of change one
   may be observing and

2. the fact that innumerable processes of change are going
   on _simultaneously_

For example, he immediately qualifies the comment just made
by saying[^66]

> Whereas "the passage of time" is current everywhere alike
> and is in relation with everything. And further, all
> changes may be faster or slower, but not so time; for fast
> and slow are defined by time, "faster" being more change
> in less time, and "slower" less in more.

The philosopher of the concrete reverts to the abstract with
the same instinct that told him the heavens truly rotate.
His conclusion is:[^67]

> 'Plainly, then, time is neither identical with movement
> nor capable of being separated from it.'

However, his tendency to the concrete reasserts itself when
he considers the question of measurement, which he thinks is
closely related to the true nature of time. To clarify his
ideas, suppose we imagine taking many snapshots of an object
moving against a fixed and unchanging background, on which
much detail can be seen. Say we obtain a hundred such
pictures. Because of the detail in the background, we could,
even if the snapshots were supplied in a mixed-up order,
easily put them in the correct order. There is a clear
ordering with respect to before and after. Aristotle
comments that[^68]

> 'the primary significance of before-and-afterness is the
> local one of "in front of" and "behind" . . .

> But there is also a before-and-after in time, in virtue of
> the dependence of time upon motion.'

He seeks to make this _before and after ordering_, derived
from spatial relationships through motion, correspond to
time itself:[^69]

> 'Motion, then, is the objective seat of before and
> afterness both in movement and in time.'

He can now almost lay his hands on time:

> When we determine a movement by defining its first and
> last limit, we also recognize a lapse of time; for when we
> are aware of the measuring of motion by a prior and
> posterior limit then we may say time has passed.

> And our determination consists in distinguishing between
> the initial limit and the final one, and seeing that what
> lies between them is distinct from both; for when we
> distinguish between the extremes and what is between them,
> and the mind pronounces the 'nows' to be two - an initial
> and a final one - it is then that we say that a certain
> time has passed;

> for that which is determined either way by a 'now' seems
> to be what we mean by time. And let this be accepted and
> laid down.

Although this seems definite enough, it is still not as
precise as we would wish. There is unfortunately a gap
between the principle and concrete practicality.

Let us first look at a few more explications of the general
principle. Aristotle says:[^70]

> 'When we perceive a distinct before and after, then we
> speak of time; for this is just what time is, the
> calculable measure or dimension of motion with respect to
> before-and-afterness'

The ability to count different stages in a process is
important:[^71]

> 'It is in virtue of the countableness of its
> before-and-afters that the "now" exists'

so that:[^72]

> 'time, then, is the dimension of movement in its
> before-and-afterness, and is continuous (because movement
> is so)'

Even more explicitly:

> To "before" and "after" are objectively involved in
> motion, and these, qua capable of numeration, constitute
> time.'

And finally:[^73]

> 'Time is the numeration of continuous movement.'

Taken literally, this suggests time is to be identified with
counting the snapshots we considered above, or perhaps with
the distance that the object has travelled ('the dimension
of movement'). There is a danger that we finish up with
a separate time for each motion; however, this is
a possibility Aristotle explicitly rejects in a passage that
makes his overall concept of time much clearer:[^74]

> But if we take one kind of change and say 'now' with
> respect to it, other kinds of change, each of which has
> a specifically different unit to be counted in, will be at
> a certain stage of their change at this same 'now'. Can
> each of them have a different time, and must there be more
> than one time running concurrently? No; for it is the same
> lapse of time that is counted by two 'news', everywhere at
> once, whatever the units of movement or change; whereas
> the one-and-sameness of the units is determined by their
> kind and not by their 'at-once-ness'; just as if there
> were dogs and horses, seven of each, the number would be
> the same, but the units numbered different. So, too, of
> all movement-changes determined simultaneously the time is
> the same; one may be quick and another slow, and one
> a change of place and the other of quality; the time,
> however, is the same, if the counting has reached the same
> number and been made simultaneously, whether of the
> qualitive modification or of the change of place. So the
> movements or changes are different and stand apart, but
> the time is the same everywhere, because the numeration,
> if made simultaneously and up to the same figure, is one
> and the same.

Because Aristotle's universe is finite, the ideas expressed
here can be illustrated particularly well by the snapshot
device. Suppose snapshots are taken of successive 'nows' of
the entire universe. Within the universe, innumerable
processes of general change as well as motion are taking
place. We could concentrate our attention on any one of
these, establish the succession of 'befores' and 'afters'
and count them.

According to Aristotle, this numeration is time. We can do
the same with any other process of change. But because all
processes take place simultaneously, there is a one-to-one
_correspondence_ between the 'nows' in the two parallel
trains of successive 'nows'. Although he does not say so
explicitly, such a notion of a universal succession of
all-embracing simultaneities in which all the different
processes of the universe 'run together' is clearly what
underlies the above passage.

At this point we must distinguish between mere succession
and the notion of _uniform motion_. It is here that
commonsense prejudice gets the better of the philosopher of
the concrete. By Aristotle's time the Greek mathematicians
had developed the concept of a uniform motion and used it in
kinematic geometry. It is obvious from the passages which
follow that Aristotle shared their belief in the existence
of uniform motion.

But if time is revealed by nothing else but motion and
change, where do we _find a criterion that tells us a motion
is uniform_? All that is agreed so far is that change and
motion provide the evidence for the lapse of time. That is
what Aristotle calls time. But uniform motion can only mean
that the motion results in equal displacements in equal
times. _But the times are derived from the motion, so we
get ourselves into a vicious circle._

In the case of a single motion, as in the first snapshot
example we considered, it is nonsense to use the
Aristotelian concept of time as constituted by changes that
occur while also attempting to say such a motion is uniform.
There is mere succession, nothing more or nothing less.

Aristotle in fact gets close to solving the problem of
uniform motion within the framework of a concrete philosophy
when he considers what it means to say that one motion is
quicker than another:[^75]

> What I mean by one change being quicker than another is
> that, of two homogeneous change movements (either both on
> a periphery, or both on a straight line if it is a local
> movement, and mutatis mutandis other kinds of change),
> that one is the quicker which reaches a certain determined
> stage or point in its course "before" the other reaches
> the point at the same distance from the starting-point in
> its course.

From this it is _one short step_ to an operational
definition of uniform motion, namely, one motion may be
uniform relative to another if the distance traversed in one
always stands in a given _fixed ratio_ to the distance
traversed in the other.

But Aristotle does not take this final step, which would
have made time _both concrete_ (reduced to motion and change
and not conceived as an abstract transcendent entity) _and
relative_. It would then have been an empirical matter to
establish if such mutually uniform motions are actually
realized in the world.

However he introduces the concept of uniform motion without
defining it in concrete terms:[^76]

> And not only do we measure the length of uniform movement
> by time, but also the length of time by uniform movement,
> since they MUTUALLY DETERMINE each other;

> For the time taken determines the length moved over (the
> time units corresponding to the space units), and the
> length moved over determines the time taken. And when we
> call time 'much' or 'little' we are estimating it in units
> of uniform motion, as we measure the 'number' of anything
> we count by the units we count it in.

> For example, the number of horses by taking one horse as
> our unit. For when we are told the number of horses, we
> know how many there are in the troop; and by counting how
> many there are, horse by horse, we know their number. And
> so too with time and uniform motion . . .

But the problem with this is that a horse may be recognized
without reference to anything else, whereas, as we have
seen, a _uniform motion is incapable of definition in
isolation_.

Aristotle's discussion reveals no evidence he was aware of
this vicious circle or that he could have avoided it by
a relative definition. This is another confirmation of
Maier's thesis. Abstract instinct takes over. The remainder
of his discussion is concerned solely with practicalities.
Given that uniform motions do exist, which may be used to
conveniently measure time? Circular motion is an obvious
candidate:[^77]

> And now, keeping locomotion and especially rotation in
> mind, note that everything is counted by some unit of like
> nature to itself - monads, monad by monad, for instance,
> and horses, horse by horse - and so likewise time by some
> finite unit of time.

> But as we have said, motion and time mutually determine
> each other quantitively; and that because the standard of
> time established by the motion we select is the quantitive
> measure both of that motion and of time.

> If, then, the standard once fixed measures all
> dimensionality of its own order, a uniform rotation will
> be the best standard, since it is easiest to count.

This is followed by a somewhat curious passage:[^78]

> Neither qualitive modification nor growth nor genesis has
> the kind of uniformity that rotation has; and so time is
> regarded as the rotation of ?THE SPHERE?, inasmuch as all
> other orders of motion are measured by it, and time itself
> is standardized by reference to it.

The translator provides no explanation of what Aristotle
means here by 'the sphere'. It does not seem that Aristotle
means the sphere of the fixed stars, though this would fit
in well with a passage in De Caelo where he says [^79]:

> 'the revolution of the heaven is the measure of all
> motions, because it alone is continuous, unvarying and
> eternal'.

In fact, intepretation of 'the sphere' as a generic sphere
would not be inconsistent with the idea that uniform
rotation is natural to all spheres (found at various points
in Aristotle's work cf. the footnote on p. 81). This is
certainly true of the celestial spheres, for a primary
property of the Eudoxan system of concentric spheres, which
Aristotle adopted and modified, is that all the spheres
rotate uniformly, though not about the same poles nor at the
same rate.

As we shall see, belief in the perfect uniformity and
circularity of celestial motions appears to have been an
article of faith from the period of Plato and Aristotle
through to Ptolemy about five hundred years later.

We conclude that Aristotle believed the rotation of the
heavens defined and measured the uniform flow of time both
practically and as a metaphysical principle. In adopting
this position he betrayed his philosophical instincts. It
seems the intuition of Plato and the geometers held too
strong a grip on his mind.

Even so, by attempting to concretely define time, Aristotle
and the medievals who followed him,[^80] developed a concept
more modern than that of Newtonian absolute time, as we
shall see in Chapters 3 and 12.

[^\*4]  It is interesting that Aristotle did not postulate
the crystal spheres to carry the stars and planets because
he found it inherently impossible to believe they could move
of their own accord. The significant thing for him was the
manner in which they moved.[^38]

The argument was as follows: first, the sun and the moon
were evidently spherical. From this he concludes all
celestial bodies must be so. Now in the case of the moon it
could actually be seen that it neither rotated on its axis
nor rolled - but these were the only two effective (natural)
motions of which terrestrial spheres were observed to be
capable. Extrapolating from the moon to all the celestial
bodies, Aristotle concluded that they must all move by
virtue of being fixed on spherical shells, since otherwise
they would have to be endowed with wings or flippers to swim
through the ether. One sees here how plenism dominated his
thinking. One of the more entertaining passages in Kepler's
Astronomia Nova is his quite serious discussion of whether
the planets have flippers.
