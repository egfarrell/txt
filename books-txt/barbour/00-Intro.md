# 1. About this Book

This book was originally published in 1989 as "Absolute or
Relative Motion? Volume 1, The Discovery of Dynamics". I had
hoped it would be followed by a 2nd volume titled "_The Frame of
the World_". The two volumes would have given a comprehensive
account of the _absolute / relative_ debate.

This is closely related to Ernst Mach's idea (Mach's principle)
that inertia stems from the combined effect of the universe and
not from the straitjacket imposed by Newton's absolute space. The
_foundation of dynamics_ is at stake and with it our deepest
questions about the cosmos:

    * does the universe exist within an invisible _absolute
      framework_, as Newton argued,

    * or is it a holistic and self-contained _relational system_,
      as proposed by Leibniz and others?

Closely related are questions of the nature of time and
motion, and how these issues relate to Einstein's theories
of SR and GR.

To date I have failed to complete the 2nd volume (though for
encouraging reasons). Following discussions with my publishers,
it was decided to retitle the already published book 'The
Discovery of Dynamics' and issue it as a paperback. This is
appropriate, as the book mainly details how dynamics came to be
discovered and is not just as a monograph on the
absolute/relative debate.

Except for this Preface, the book is reissued exactly as
originally printed. I find no reason to undertake significant
revisions, and this has helped keep the cost down. It has the
disadvantage that the reader will encounter references to the now
defunct Volume 2. However, _a substitute of sorts_ is to hand, as
I explain later, so I hope the reader will not find this irksome.

The remainder of this Preface is divided into two parts:

    * In _Section 2_, I respond to points raised by reviewers and
      mention some new studies by authors that complement The
      Discovery of Dynamics.

    * In _Section 3_, I explain why its companion volume has not
      yet appeared, and draw attention to published material that
      covers much of its planned content.

# 2. Response to Reviewers and Additional Comments

Overall the reception of this book on first publication was
encouraging. However, several reviewers felt its value was
diminished by being written from an exclusively modern
perspective. Historians distrust "Whig history," that is, history
under the assumption that humanity through the centuries has
necessarily been progressing toward the great ideals of liberal
democracy and enlightenment. My most severe critic was the late
Eric Aiton, who wrote [in Band 672 (1989) of Zentralblatt fur
Mathematik und ihre Grenzgebiete]) that "the author's arguments
are flawed, as a result of a deep-rooted anachronism. Everything
is judged in relation to what the author regards as the correct
theory: that is, the explanation accepted today ... The idea of
a linear progression towards the modern world view ... has long
been abandoned by historians of science."

Now I am sure there is some truth in what Aiton says, and am
happy to let the reader judge whether the "everything" is fair.
However, I feel Aiton failed to grasp that the book had several
aspects, only one of which is history of science. And I am not
sure Aiton's own position is secure. Many modern historians of
science seem to believe there are no criteria of good science.

There are criteria of good science. Working scientists mostly
want books about real science and why it works. You get that in
particular from the study of Newton. Descartes's great importance
for Newton was his embryonic dynamical scheme and almost perfect
formulation of the law of inertia. That is where the real
interest for science lies...

I have set out my own position on these matters in the essay
"Reflections on the aims, methods and criteria of the history of
science" in Contemporary Physics, 38, 161-166 (1997), which
includes a review of S. Chandrasekhar's Newton's Principia for
the Common Reader. I concluded with the words:

> "I remain convinced that the history of science represents
> a rich organic development in which success has reinforced
> success and that the primary task of the historians should
> be to tell that great story as it is. We need more work on
> a broad canvas."

This is my apologia for the present work. Since the original
publication of 'The Discovery of Dynamics' in 1989 I have done
little new work on this period. However, I do regret not citing
the remarkable essay by Clifford Truesdell in the Archive for
History of Exact Sciences, where he outlined "_A Program toward
Rediscovering the Rational Mechanics of the Age of Reason_."

He believes that a whole age of the development of rational
mechanics had been lost from sight through the towering
prominence of Newton's Principia (1687), and Lagrange's
Mechanique Analytique (1788). In his essay he makes some
interesting and provocative comments on the Principia.

Some I feel are off the mark, especially the claim: "It is
small oversimplification to say that ... the Principia is
a retrospective work which selects, marshals, and formalizes
the achievements of the century before it." I cannot see
how Truesdell can say this of a work that introduces the
clear notion of force and with total clarity foresees the
next 300 years of physics and expresses it in 92 words:

> "I wish we could derive the rest of the phenomena of
> Nature by the same kind of reasoning from mechanical
> principles, for I ... suspect that they may all depend
> upon certain forces by which the particles of bodies, by
> some causes hitherto unknown, ... cohere in regular
> figures. These forces being unknown, philosophers have
> hitherto attempted the search of Nature in vain; but
> I hope the principles here laid down will afford some
> light either to this or some truer method of philosophy."

That is hardly a retrospective sentiment. But Truesdell's
essay is a fine read and a worthy tribute to his hero Euler.

Another quite different matter concerns the moon, which
might have figured more prominently. Among the motions of
the celestial objects that move in the sky relative to the
background of the fixed stars, those of the moon are by far
the most obvious yet at the same time the most baffling. In
fact, some argue that the attempts to understand these
motions created most of dynamics and many of the most
powerful methods of modern mathematical physics.

There is much truth in this view, but I still feel it was
observations and interpretations of the simpler motions of
the sun and the planets which unleashed the Copernican
Revolution, led Kepler and Galileo to their discoveries, and
finally provided the most telling clues for Newton. Happily
I can recommend two excellent accounts of lunar studies,
both by Martin Gutzwiller:

    1. The part devoted to the moon in his 'Chaos in Classical
       and Quantum Mechanics' (Springer-Verlag, 1990).

    2. His review article on the lunar three-body problem in
       Reviews of Modern Physics (70,589,1998).

I'd also like to draw the reader's attention to a useful
book and two interesting articles:

    1. The Key to Newton's Dynamics: The Kepler Probe by J. B.
       Brackenridge (1995),

    2. "Hooke, Orbital Motion, and Newton's Principia" (American
       Journal of Physics, 62, No. 4, 1994) (Nauenberg)

    3. "Newton's Early Computational Method for Dynamics"
       (Nauenberg) (Archive for History of Exact Sciences, 46,
       No. 3, 1994).

Nauenberg, like the great Russian mathematician V. I. Arnold
[whose book _Huygens and Barrow, Newton and Hooke_ (1990) is
recommended], shares my belief that Hooke's contribution to
universal gravitation has not been fully recognized.
Nauenberg's 2nd paper seems a genuine contribution to
understanding the development of Newton's dynamical methods.
The paper is recommended to those who like a bit of good
detective work coupled with a fine feeling for dynamics.

I would also like to quote from an otherwise positive review
by Bruce Brackenridge (Isis 82, No. 3, 1991):

> [I]n the discussion of the Principia and its immediate
> development, there is not one reference to Whiteside's
> monumental eight-volume ... The Mathematical Papers of Isaac
> Newton (CUP, 1967-1981). ...

> It is so extensive, in fact, that it is often a work more
> referenced than read. Instead, Barbour seems to rely upon the
> works of John Herivel and Westfall, and his references to
> Whiteside are limited to a few published articles."

Brackenridge is right; I only looked at small parts of
Whiteside's great work and did not use them. Partly due to
lack of time, but mainly because I was only attempting to
capture the main developments, and I felt here it was more
appropriate to consider Whiteside's papers (though
I actually disagree with one of his conclusions, as the
reader will see). However, the lack of prominent mention of
the _Mathematical Papers_ was inexcusable. My apologies to
Whiteside, who has done Newton scholarship a magnificent
service.

Now a brief word about Chandrasekhar's book on the
Principia. It has become a best-seller and made the public
even more aware of Newton's achievements. There are many
fine things in the book, but even Homer nods occasionally
and there are also some misrepresentations. More seriously,
I feel Chandrasekhar misread the historical development.

I had some interaction with him in Oxford while he wrote the
book. One of the main problems was Chandrasekhar's unshakeable
conviction that Newton had a complete notion of universal
gravitation by 1666. I asked him (to no avail) to read Curtis
Wilson's fascinating paper (See [^57] in ./10_newton_I.md), which
to me shows this cannot be true. Chandra was curiously blind to
the story of Newton's discoveries and I think this distorts his
account of the Principia and even dehumanizes Newton. I have
written more about this in my essay review above.

# 3. Why Volume 2 Did Not Appear?

### Reason 1:
The first is tucked away in the footnote on page 5,
where I comment:

> "the time aspect of the problem of the relativity of
> motion has been curiously neglected."

I predicted time would play a large role in the 2nd volume and it
has certainly played a large role in my life since then. There
are 2 fundamental issues regarding time in classical physics:

    1. _Duration_: What is 'duration' - what is the theoretical
       justification for saying that a second today is the same
       as a second yesterday (or immediately after the Big Bang?)

    2. _Simultaneity_: How is one to define simultaneity at
       spatially separated points? Einstein's brilliant answer to
       this question led to the creation of both relativity
       theories, but for some reason he ignored the first
       question.

Surprisingly, there _is_ a beautiful _theory of duration_ hidden
within the mathematics of GR. In fact, time and duration arise
within GR from an arena in which there is no time at all. For
a variety of reasons Einstein was unaware of this and the same is
true of many working in relativity.

The _timeless basis_ of GR has profound implications, especially
for attempts to make it compatible with quantum mechanics and
create a quantum theory of the universe. It is possible that
quantum cosmology will be static and time will not play a role in
the foundations of physics.

This conviction grew on me in the years immediately following
'The Discovery of Dynamics'. It resulted in several papers (see
platonia.com), and my book "The End of Time: The Next Revolution
in Physics". A large part of that book is also concerned with the
absolute/relative debate, and much of its material would
otherwise have appeared in volume 2.

### Reason 2:

The second thing deflecting me from volume 2 was an invitation
from Herbert Pfister to organize a conference devoted to Mach's
principle. This took place in 1993 and published as Volume 6 of
the Einstein Studies series: "Mach's Principle: From Newton's
Bucket to Quantum Gravity". This book is regarded as a standard
source for studies of Mach's principle. It too is a substitute
for my 2nd volume, with the advantage of contributions from many
other authors.

### Reason 3:

The final reason for the absence of volume 2 is related to
ongoing work. In the 'Introduction to Volumes 1 and 2' I mention
how Bertotti and myself showed Mach's principle could be
implemented in point particle mechanics by my '_best matching_'
technique. It enables one to construct a dynamical theory where
only the _separations_ between bodies play a role.

In contrast to Newtonian theory, it is impossible for any
imagined overall _rotation_ of the universe to play a role.
[Harvey Brown talks about this too - he says this is a prediction
of relational theories!] This is why a theory based on _best
matching_ implements Mach's principle. Bertotti and I showed that
best matching is one of the two key properties of the deep
structure of GR when treated as a theory of the _evolution of 3D
geometry_ (as opposed to a 4D spacetime treated as a 'block').
The other key property of GR is its timeless basis. (as already
mentioned)

### My note: 2 key properties of GR

    1. timeless basis - from which theory of evolution arises

    2. best matching - when GR formulated as evolution of _3D_
       geometry

All this is satisfactory from the Machian viewpoint, but it would
be desirable to go further and create a dynamical theory in which
_the overall size of the universe_ has no meaning.

Theoretical physicists have long dreamt of creating
a _scale-invariant dynamics_. From 1996 to 1998 I took a step
towards a generalization of best matching. Then I had to complete
_The End of Time_ and only returned to the problem in 1999, when
I formulated a _scale invariant generalization_ of the Machian
particle dynamics that Bertotti and I had created years earlier.

Because of these developments I have not yet found time to
publish this theory ["shape dynamics"??]. The most important
aspect of the new theory is it showed how a similar
generalization of GR could be attempted.

At this point I collaborated with Niall O'Murchadha. Niall has
great expertise in _3D conformal geometry_, exactly what was
needed to mould best matching into a form appropriate for
a modern gravitational theory. He added a crucial ingredient and
soon we had the outlines of _conformal gravity_. This putative
new gravitational theory is nevertheless similar to GR, and may
give the same predictions as GR in the domains where it has been
well tested.

However deviations from Einstein's theory are expected in domains
where it remains to be tested. This work led to many surprises
and is being rapidly developed. It could transform our
understanding of both _relativity_ and _gauge theory_, which
describes the interaction of various forms of matter that exist.
At least this work demonstrate how far Machian ideas can be
applied to describe the universe.

It would be premature to summarise the absolute/relative debate
until full clarity on these developments is achieved. So this is
the 3rd reason the volume hasn't appeared. I hope what finally
does appear will be more valuable than anything I contemplated
back in 1989.

#  0.2 Preface to the Hardback edition

At its deepest level, our understanding of the physical world is
based mainly on the theory of _dynamical systems_ and the many
examples of such systems found in Nature.

These range from hydrogen atoms, complicated nuclear systems and
everyday technology, to systems such as star clusters and
galaxies, and even the universe itself. Many characteristic
features of dynamical systems first appeared with Newtons
Principia (1687). In a sense the Principia marks the discovery of
dynamics.

This book explains how Newton made his discovery. Three quarters
of the book deal with _preparatory work_ in astronomy and the
mathematical study of terrestrial motions which made Newton's
work possible. The final quarter describes Newton's own
discoveries, his synthesis into a workable scheme of dynamics,
and his introduction of "universal gravitation".

However this book is more than a history of dynamics. It attempts
to situate this discovery in light of currently _unresolved
questions_ involving space, time, and motion. It is about
continuing the ancient search for the foundation, or _frame_, of
the world. This aspect of the work, to be continued in volume
2 (The Frame of the World) covering the period from Newton to
Einstein, is explained at some length in the Introduction.

In the remainder of the Preface I'd like to address the
curious reader who has to read this far. My original
intention was to write a specialist work, targeting
scientists in the narrow field of relativity and the
philosophy of science. However, I became aware of the
relevance of this material to a wider readership. The scope
of the book was extended accordingly.

Working scientists generally know little about the
historical origins of their discipline. Nevertheless they
appreciate anything they can learn on the subject,
particularly as they grow older and a sense of the mystery
of the world grows on them. Per Eliot in East Coker:

> The world becomes stranger, the pattern more complicated.

In addition to the original select audience, I hope
scientists across many disciplines will find this book
interesting. With the explosion books on the fundamental
questions of science I have attempted to make the work
accessible to interested laypeople by opening chapter 1 with
a _review of Newtonian dynamics_. The mathematics required
is minimal - basic trigonometry, vector analysis and algebra
and a little on the geometry of triangles, circles and
ellipses (explained where necessary).

One of the attractions of the history of astronomy and
dynamics is the number of non-trivial results that can be
understood with simple mathematics. Though some passages in
the book are technical (and a few refer to advanced
developments) the book could be read by motivated 18-year
old pupils thinking of specialising in physics, mathematics
or astronomy at university.

This brings me to the readers I am most keen to attract
- students of these disciplines at universities. Above all
I would like to awaken their interest in both the
'_knowledge of the celestial things_' (Kepler) and the ways
they were discovered. I would like to give them a stimulus
as they study, to question received wisdom and think for
themselves. It is only such people who, combining
_historical awareness with radical innovation_, will
advance our understanding of these fascinating matters.

# Introduction

> 'How differently the clouds move across that lofty,
> limitless sky!'

The words of Prince Andrei Bolkonsky as he drifts into
consciousness at Austerlitz, express the spirit of this
book. It is about how _concepts of motion_ have changed in
the past and could change in the future. It is a fascinating
subject; because each change in our conception of _motion_
results in a change of our deepest conceptions of reality.
Each change opens the door to a new world.

If a stone is thrown at the stars with sufficient force it
will travel through the universe forever - or at least until
the end of time. Relative to the observable matter in the
universe, such a stone follows a definite path with great
accuracy, but we _do not know what determines the path_.
Is it space, or matter, or some _combination of the two_, or
what? We see the undoubted effect but cannot put our finger
on the cause. This _puzzle_ is the central subject of this
study.

By its ubiquity, motion doesn't strike us as particularly
marvellous or mysterious. But the seemingly simple is
complex and subtle. The discovery of the _law of inertia_
in the 17th century showed that all motions we observe are
merely fleeting sections of immense journeys through the
universe. Everything is caught up in _some huge flux_, of
which the motions we observe are but small details.

Our little local motions have a deceptive appearance of
simplicity because they are seen against the background of
the (relatively) stable earth and even more stable starry
heavens. However we have now learnt enough to realise that
motion is subject to the dominion of something far greater
than the earth or the few thousand stars that could be seen
before the telescope was discovered:

>   We sense the throb of the pulse; but we cannot locate
>   the heart.

Confronted with a restless universe apparently stretching to
infinity but also with the undoubted _existence of inertial
motion_, Newton identified _absolute space & time_ as the
framework of all motion. He asserted that undisturbed bodies
move along straight lines with uniform speed. However
Newton's concepts of absolute space & time were severely
criticized by Huygens, Leibniz, and Berkeley. Their
questions:

1. If space is _invisible_ - how can one say how a body
   moves relative to space?

2. If space is _nothing physical_ - how can it influence
   actual motions of physical bodies?

But their objections were gradually forgotten, until
rediscovered in the the 19th century by Ernst Mach.[^s1]
Although Mach was a reluctant theorizer, he proposed one of
the most radical ideas in science:

> He suggested inertial motion on Earth and in the solar
> system is causally determined by the _totality_ of matter
> in the universe in accordance with some definite but as
> yet _unknown_ law.

It is worth highlighting the _new element_ in Mach's
proposal. Newton accepted that motion could only be
_observed_ relative to other bodies. Nevertheless Newton
held that motion _actually_ takes place in absolute space
and time, irrespective of the presence of bodies in the
universe.

Thus, a solitary body would still have a motion even if no
other bodies existed in the universe. In contrast, Mach
asserted that motion does not exist except as a _change of
position_ relative to other bodies and the law which governs
changes in relative position must be expressed directly in
these same _relative terms_. He thought such a law would
lead to objective and observable differences from Newton's
laws.

Mach expressed his ideas in a book on the history of
mechanics[^1] which had a considerable influence on 20th
century physics. From 1907 to 1918 Einstein worked with
feverish enthusiasm to discover the mysterious law which
Mach postulated. He coined the expression Mack's
Principle[^2] for the conjecture that:

> the inertial properties of local matter are determined by
> the overall matter distribution in the universe

and was convinced that his general theory of relativity[^3],
would give full expression to the principle.

The outcome was a decided paradox. Einstein was forced to
conclude that although matter in the universe did influence
inertia within the framework of his theory, his theory was
_unable_ to demonstrate that inertia is _completely_
determined by matter.

After one attempt[^4] to save the situation (which itself
had an ironic consequence - it laid the foundations of
cosmology), Einstein reluctantly concluded[^5] that his
attempt had _failed_.

Despite this failure, GR was recognized as a supreme
achievement. More recently there have been impressive
experimental confirmations of the theory[^6] and today few
doubt its essential correctness, though many believe that GR
is only one aspect of a more comprehensive theory that
embraces _all the forces_ of nature.

The present status of Mach's Principle could be described as
_confused_. It is said there are as many Mach's Principles as
physicists. Years ago it was the subject of lively discussion,
and Machian theories were advanced by Sciama,[^7]  Hoyle and
Narlikar,[^8]  Brans & Dicke,[^9] and Treder.[^10]  Much of this
work has been reviewed by Raine,[^11] who gives a bibliography.
These theories aroused interest, though none achieved widespread
acceptance.

Another development was a reinterpretation of GR by
Wheeler[^12], who argued Einstein did actually give
expression to Mach's ideas. In his view, Machian ideas only
make sense in a _closed_ (ie. finite) universe. Wheeler
contends that in a closed universe Einstein's theory is
perfectly Machian, though maybe not quite in the way
Einstein envisaged and certainly not the way Mach did.
Wheeler has only been partially successful in persuading
other relativists of his interpretation.

In recent years the question has passed out of vogue, partly
because it seemed so _difficult_ to make progress or even
pinpoint the nature of the problem, more probably because of
exciting new theories that offer prospects for the
unification of all the forces of nature (gauge theories,
supersymmetry, GUTs and superstrings). It would seem that
now is not the best time to write a monograph on the
subject. However I would argue the opposite.

Firstly the problem has _not been solved_. It is only in
abeyance and remains the central _enigma of motion_; it will
surely come to the fore again. For a start, if the grand
unified theory is discovered - which some believe is just
over the horizon, we must see how it stands vis-a-vis this
great problem of the _foundations of dynamics_, indeed our
very concept of the universe, space, and time.

Secondly, the problem of unifying Quantum Theory and GR is
among the most baffling in Physics. This problem is termed
the _quantization of GR_, which means that gravitational
effects, like all physical phenomena, should be subject to
the laws of quantum theory. Although much is obscure, it is
clear that _general covariance_ - that most fundamental
property of Einstein's theory - presents the biggest
obstacle to its quantization.[^13]  But _general covariance_
was precisely the property Einstein invoked to implement
Mach's Principle.[^3]

>  Therefore there is a most intimate connection between
>  Mach's Principle (as perceived by Einstein), and the
>  obstacles to quantization.

Our attitude to the quantization problem must at the very
least be influenced by Machian considerations.[^14]

A third reason for reviewng the history of Mach's Principle
is precisely because little is happening in the field at
present. Even the great work on the quantization of GR has
flagged in recent years, as seen in the more reflective
nature of the 3rd Oxford conference on quantum gravity
(March 1984)[^15].  The calm between storms is a good time
to take stock. Moreover, the lack of progress in both fields
may well have a common origin, as has just been argued.

Let us look back on what has been achieved, see if any _weak
spots_ are visible, but above all identify the core problem
and agree on its precise nature. This is the task I set
myself here. A few words on the origins of the book may be
appropriate, as they supply the 4th reason for its writing.

My interest in the problem dates to 1963 and was stimulated
by reading Bondi's book _Cosmology_[^16]. This led to
a reading of Mach's _Science of Mechanics_[^17] which made
a deep impression. Mach's arguments for the relativity of
both space _and also time_[^s2] appeared to be undeniable.
If neither time nor space exist, it would seem the entire
theory of motion must be recreated ab initio.

However, careful reading of Mach and the Einstein's GR
papers led me to a surprising conclusion: although Einstein
professed great admiration for Mach and claimed to be
solving the problem of inertia as outlined by Mach, he
actually had a significantly different understanding (or
misunderstanding!) of the problem.

Above all, Einstein appeared to confuse _two quite distinct_
uses of the word _inertia_. When Mach spoke of the problem
of inertia, he was referring exclusively to Newton's _First
Law of Motion_: that bodies subject to no forces move
through absolute space with a uniform rectilinear motion.
Mach insisted that this law was _epistemological nonsense_
and a physical implausibility. It made statements incapable
of objective verification, and such statements could not
provide true knowledge of Nature (epistemology is the study
of the grounds of knowledge).

He called for a proper _operational_ definition of the
phenomenon and a physical and causal explanation in terms of
something real, ie. observable (at least in principle).
Though he criticized the other use of the word _inertia_, to
describe the _inertial mass_ which appears in the 2nd Law of
Motion, Mach had no criticism of the concept itself. He felt
it was unproblematic, especially if using his own
operational definition of mass instead of Newton's circular
one. (Mach's clarification of Newton's concept of inertial
mass is discussed in the final chapter)

In contrast, examination of Einstein's papers reveals he
used the word "inertia" indiscriminately to describe _both_
phenomena and seemed to regard _both_ as requiring
physical explanation. A typical example of Einstein's
interpretation (or misinterpretation) of Mach is the
following 1913 statement:[^19]:

> '... one must require that _inertial resistance_ be due to
> the relative acceleration of a body (with respect to other
> bodies).

> We must require that the _inert resistance_ of a body
> increase solely because in its neighbourhood there are
> unaccelerated inert masses ....'

(Note: the allegedly Machian variation in inertial mass
which Einstein believed he built into GR has since been
shown to have no physical reality and is a coordinate
effect)

Much of the confusion surrounding Mach's Principle stems
from this curious interpretation. It is surprising that this
_fundamental shift by Einstein_ from Mach's original
position has never been pointed out. Moreover, I argue that
by regarding the concept of inertial mass as unproblematic,
Mach was right and Einstein wrong (on this point at least).
This is a first point that must be made.

A second point is that, in developing GR, Einstein did not
make a frontal attack on the Machian problem of finding
a _dynamical explanation for the law of inertia_. Einstein
himself commented[^20] that the simplest way of realizing
the aim of relativity theory would be to formulate the laws
of motion directly and _ab initio_ in terms of relative
distances and relative velocities - nothing else should
appear in the theory. However he decided this route would be
'impracticable'.

In his view, the history of science had demonstrated the
impossibility of dispensing with coordinate systems. He
therefore adopted an indirect approach and was guided more
by gut intuition it seems, than a clear formulation of
principles that would lead to the realization of his aims.

This was in striking contrast to his approach to the other
aims of GR - the creation of a gravitational field theory
consistent with the principles of special relativity and the
inclusion of geometry as an integral part of dynamics. (This
was made possible by Riemann's 1854 demonstration that
Euclidean geometry was just one of many possible geometries
the world could possess). Here his approach was crystal
clear, entirely logical, and could not fail to achieve its
aim if pushed with sufficient vigour - as it was.

When I read Einstein's papers (many not yet published in
English), I was struck by the fact he was attacking three
problems:

1. _Mach_: the origin of inertia,
2. _Maxwell_: the relativisation of Newtonian gravity (????)
3. _Minkowski_: include geometry in a dynamical framework

However he was working on foundations not equally developed.
The Machian concept of inertia as interaction with distant
matter in the universe was just a vague hunch, whereas
special relativity[^21] was already a highly developed
theory. Above all, it had been cast into a beautifully clear
form by Minkowski[^22] by his creation of the space-time
concept. This concept was tailor-made for variable
Riemannian geometry, which was already a highly developed
discipline and perfectly suited to Einstein needs.

In addition Maxwell's electrodynamics, _translated into
relativistic form_, gave Einstein clear hints of the way to
proceed. (see Jurgen Renn talk!)

Einstein's carriage was being drawn by a Machian donkey and
a Minkowskian/Maxwellian stallion. It's hardly surprising
the stallion won out! I concluded one should go back to
first principles in the Machian problem and attempt the
route Einstein said was impracticable. In particular, the
problem might not be insuperable if it was attacked by
a _nonrelativistic_ approach[^s3] as a first approximation.

After all Mach had identified and formulated the problem of
inertia in the pre-relativistic world. Is it possible to solve
the non-relativistic Machian problem? If this could be cracked,
then one would have definite theoretical models to attack the
full relativistic problem.

Creating simple Machian models could show:

1. the possibility of an actual Machian origin to
    inertial _motion_

2. the kind of effects and structures one could expect
    in a realistic relativistic theory.

It was important to establish the plausibility of the idea
and get a feeling for its consequences. My work culminated
in the formulation of a framework for constructing theories
that are Machian of necessity, ie. by virtue of the basic
principles they are constructed on.

A publication in Nature[^23] outlining the basic principles
and a simple model led to a collaboration with Bruno
Bertotti. Our aims were to fully develop the nonrelativistic
theory and then incorporate special relativity in a Machian
manner.

The outcome of this work[^24] was not what we expected. It
began as an attempt to find an alternative to GR, which we
felt was not truly Machian, but our final conclusion was
that Einstein's theory was actually _much more Machian_ than
we had believed. It was Machian in fact precisely in the
sense required by our general principles!

We will not justify this conclusion here, to which we were
led mainly by the intervention of Karel Kuchar. The
necessary explanations will be in Volume 2. The point of
mentioning this work is the explanation it provides

1. for the writing of this book at this time and
2. for the overall view adopted in this study

which is broadly in line with Wheeler's position.[^12]  This
position is that GR is a true implementation of many of
Mach's ideas but this has been obscured by historical
reasons and by Einstein's attempt to find an allegedly
Machian explanation of inertial _mass_ (instead of
concentrating on the _real problem_, the origin of
Newton's First Law).

In broad terms, the grounds for asserting the 'Machianity'
of GR are as follows.

Mach's ideas came at a time when _field concepts_ still
hadn't supplanted the Newtonian concepts of material bodies
acting via long-range forces. But by the time Einstein
attacked Mach's problem he was working in a climate of
Maxwellian field-theoretic concepts. Many people, including
Einstein in his later years[^25], believe transitioning from
a matter-dominated world to one where fields are primary
makes the _original Machian idea obsolete_.

Let us look at this, for it will take us to the heart of the
problem. First we consider a Newtonian situation, where
material bodies constitute the entire content of the
universe.

Imagine an infinite Euclidean space which itself is
invisible. For simplicity assume it only has two dimensions.
Imagine this space populated with `n` visible material
bodies which are assumed to be so small as to be points.
Assume the relative distances between the `n` objects are
well defined and satisfy all the geometrical relationships
for such distances in a Euclidean framework. Suppose we
observe that the relative configuration (mutual separations)
of the bodies changes. From what we observe, is it possible
to deduce that any particular body has _a definite motion
in space_? This immediately raises the "fundamental problem
of motion".
![Snapshot Figures]($MY_IMAGES/physics/discovery_dynamics/I_1.jpg)


Suppose we take a 'snapshot' of the instantaneous
configuration of the bodies at some moment. It will show
a pattern of dots; their positions indicated by the crosses
in Fig. I.1. A little later we take a second 'snapshot'; the
new relative positions could now be as in Fig. I.2, in which
the positions are indicated by '0' symbols. The relative
positions are not quite the same. On the basis of this
information - and there is nothing else to use - what can we
say about motions of the individual bodies?

Here we confront the _invisibility_ of space. Suppose we
take the second snapshot and place it on top of the first,
obtaining Fig. I.3. We could then say the motions of the
individual bodies are represented by the little arrows. But
this is clearly arbitrary; we could just as well have placed
the second snapshot as in Fig. I.4 and then the arrows
showing the displacements would be quite different.

It appears from this simple example that the notion of
a body having a quite definite motion is untenable. In
particular, it seems absurd to say that a body moves through
space along a straight line.

> Key Point: Although Mach never expressed himself precisely
> in these terms, here we have the essence of the
> epistemological problem concerning him. Motion of one body
> can only be observed relative to others, and if all bodies
> are simultaneously in motion we face an acute problem if
> we want to assign a definite motion to any particular
> body.

Because everything is in motion, all _connection_ between
one 'time slice' (one snapshot) and the next is broken. We
cannot say where any of the bodies 'have gone'. If motion is
something directly observable, then it is transparently
relative. We must now comment on the different uses of the
word 'relativity'.[^*3]

We shall see that _confusion of meanings_ often contributed
to obscuring the absolute/relative debate. The two meanings
of inertia have already been mentioned.

* The relativity of motion as manifested in the
    two-snapshot illustration is normally called:
    _kinematic_ (or optical) relativity.

* It must be distinguished from: _Galilean relativity_,
    mentioned in Sec. 1.2 and examined in the second half
    of this book

* and also from the word _relativity_ and 'relativistic'
    in Einstein's special theory of relativity (which is
    closely related to Galilean relativity).

The connection between kinematic relativity and 'relativity'
in Einstein's GR will occupy us through Vol. 2. Throughout
this study, the expression 'relativity' of motion will be
used in the _kinematic_, ie. Machian, sense.

Now consider the situation in _field theory_; we will see it
is essentially _the same_. Imagine a pattern of field
_intensities_ in two dimensions and once again suppose
a snapshot is taken of them.

Later the pattern of intensities has changed everywhere by
a certain amount. We take a second snapshot. Now the aim of
a dynamical field theory, is say how the intensity at each
point of space changes with the passage of time. But again
we confront the invisibility of space. Given our two
snapshots, the only way we can determine how much the
intensity has changed is by comparing one pattern of
intensities with the other.

But how should one snapshot to be _placed with respect_ to
the other?? We lack all objective criteria for making any
definite placing of one snapshot relative to the next but
for every particular placing we choose we obtain in
principle _different changes in intensity_. Just like the
material particles, the universal change taking place
between both snapshots simultaneously _severs_ all
connection between both _time slices_.

Thus the Machian problem exists just as acutely in _both_
scenarios. This isn't altered by Einstein considering an
even harder situation where distant simultaneity is not
defined and the geometry of space can change as well.

Note the Machian problem above has no relationship to the
masses of bodies; the mass concept, ie. _inertial mass does
not even exist in the field scenario_. Thats why I disagree
with Einstein's summary late in his life:[^25]

> Mach conjectures that *inertia* would have to depend upon the
> *interaction of the masses* in a truly rational theory,
> precisely as was true for Newton's other forces, was
> a conception which I considered for a long time the correct one
> in principle

> However Mach implicitly presupposes that the basic theory
> should be of the general Newtonian type: masses and their
> interaction as the original concepts. Such a solution does
> not fit into a consistent field theory, as will be
> immediately recognized.

Einstein's comment 'as will be immediately recognized'
belies the fact that for a long time he achieved no such
recognition. Therefore there seems grounds for believing
Einstein was confused on this subject. Moreover, a further
quotation in Chapter 2 will show that as late as 1949
Einstein was still loath to abandon Mach.

Let me outline what _might_ have happened to the Machian
problem. If Mach been a more speculative theorizer, he might
have solved the problem in the 1870s in his original
Newtonian scenario. He might have found dynamical laws which
overcame the _severed connections_ between successive time
slices. It would of course involve the masses of the
universe, since it would have solved the Machian problem in
the Newtonian context. It would also have used _absolute
simultaneity_.

But such a theory might have been a template for overcoming
the analogous problem in field theory and also the more
ambitious theory involving variable geometry and the absence
of absolute simultaneity that Einstein created with GR.

Of course in later developments, _material bodies_ could not
play such a decisive role as in the hypothetical Machian
theory, because the very concept of material bodies is
superseded by these later developments.

But the commonality of the problem of motion outlined above,
equally acute in the both scenarios, suggests its solution
would in essence _not depend upon any particular theory of
the *contents* of the universe_. The problem doesn't arises
from the specific contents of the universe, but because the
contents are _constantly changing_.

Of course Mach didn't develop any such theory and Einstein's
theory was created without any such preparatory work. Many
people are convinced GR bears little resemblance to the type
of theory Mach advocated. In one sense they are correct, but
only because the concept of the world's contents has changed
out of all recognition.

The part played by _field_ concepts in transforming the
context of the Machian problem is highly significant even
though the underlying Machian problem, the _core problem_,
is still basically the same.

This is where the work that Bertotti and I did during the
1970s has value. I believe we succeeded in finding the
solution to the Machian problem in its original Machian
context subject to the _crucial assumption that the universe
consists of a finite number of bodies_ moving in either an
infinite or finite space.

It was while extending this idea which solved the original
Machian problem first to field theory and then to a variable
geometry that we realized the basic principles we were using
were essentially the same principles underlying GR.[^s4]

A word of explanation to relativists: the principles
referred to here are those discovered by "ADM" Arnowitt,
Deser and Misner[^28] (spacetime foliation into spacelike
surfaces labeled by their time coordinate, allows definition
of Hamiltonian) and discussed in connection with Mach's
Principle by Wheeler.[^29]

The 'Machian principles', developed by Bertotti and myself
quite independently of this earlier work in GR, were shown
by Kuchar to be in essence the same principles as used in
GR, though at a lower level of sophistication.

It is worth pointing out here that GR is extremely special
in the way it fulfils the Machian criteria just mentioned.
The general scheme could therefore be used to look for other
Machian alternatives to GR.

It is in this sense I assert GR is basically Machian: its
dynamical laws are _based on a principle_ (to be explained
in Vol. 2... what principle?) that enables one to overcome
the lack of connection between successive time slices of
a universe whose _total contents_ (whatever they may be) are
constantly changing.

These are my grounds for believing the time is ripe for
a discussion on the history of the absolute/relative debate.
This study aims to trace the evolution of the major strands
of Dynamics which terminate in GR.

The original intention was to treat the span from Newton to
Einstein, following the history of the absolute or relative
debate but inserting the work just described to supply an
link between:
* the Newtonian world-view of Mach's ideas and
* the relativistic field-theoretic framework Einstein used
  for GR.

However, it seemed appropriate to examine the grounds that
led Newton to formulate absolute space and time in the first
place. This led to an examination of Galileo's writings. But
Newton is truly a watershed: forward - there is no stopping
until Einstein (and no doubt we shall go further); backward
- well there is no logical stopping place before Aristotle
or a little earlier.

This historical research greatly added to my appreciation of
the problem and convinces me the historical perspective is
the correct one. There are few subjects with a more
absorbing history than the _theory of motion_.

Few problems have given rise to such profound reflections
and speculations. And few have brought such a rich harvest
of success. No one can survey the whole field from the
pre-Socratics through to GUT theories without being struck
by the sophistication achieved or by the extraordinarily
roundabout ways some key ideas have been established. For
this reason alone the _historical approach_ is recommended.

There are other reasons too. One is that it demonstrates the
extent Newton put himself out on a limb regarding absolute
space and time. His position was exceptional compared to
what came both before and after. There is a case for arguing
that absolute space and time were accidental products of the
history, which is an intriguing story in its own right. It
relates to Galileo's dispute with the Inquisition over
earth's motion and the effect this had on Descartes.
_Newton's Scholium_ on absolute space and time was actually
a polemic against Descartes.

Equally relevant is the fact that _'Machian' ideas_ were
prevalent long before Mach (and even Newton) and actually
played a role in the discovery of dynamics.

There were strongly Machian strands in Aristotle's thinking
(whom Copernicus followed in this respect) and above all
Kepler. Indeed, Kepler had an interesting pre-Newtonian form
of Mach's Principle, and it played a part in his work. Just
as the Machian stimulus can take credit for the psychic
motivation leading to GR, essentially the same idea can take
credit for Keplers discovery of the laws of planetary
motion.

Recognition of the _relativity of motion_ also played a role
in the acceptance of the Copernican proposal of a _two-fold
motion_ of the earth (daily around the polar axis and
annually around the sun). Several features of Newtonian
dynamics actually stem from Galileo's reflections on the
relativity of motion and the consequences of Copernicus's
proposal.

Finally, a deep conviction of the relativity of motion
allowed _Huygens_ to find the correct laws of _elastic
collisions_  which was another important development in
dynamics. There is a sense in which all these advances are
partly due to 'Machian' principles (admittedly of differing
degrees of purity).

These 'Machian' successes are worth emphasizing, since some
like to dismiss it as idle philosophy, exasperated by
endless discussions on Mach's Principle in GR. In fact, the
Machian ideal is ultimately indistinguishable from one of
the highest aspirations of the natural scientist:

> to show that the world we observe has an essentially
> *rational* structure where observable effects have
> observable *causes* and everything fits together into
> a coherent whole.

As Einstein[^30] put it:

> 'the chain of cause and effect is closed'.

This ideal has often been the driving force in discovering
major laws of nature, and no doubt it will do so again. Its
force is not spent.

A further argument for a historical presentation is that
throughout the development of dynamics there runs a common
thread, the _core problem_, as I call it.

Its root is to define unambiguously and uniquely _what one
means by motion_ or more generally change. This in a context
where everything else that could be used as a reference to
define that change _is itself changing_.

We are thus faced with the problem of quantifying and
mathematizing something that seems incapable of unique
definition. As we have seen, this problem reappears in
different guises and must be attacked in each period within
the framework of prevailing conceptions and level of
knowledge. It is quite clear that an historical approach is
helpful for such a constantly changing problem.

A final argument for the historical approach is that it
permits the presentation of some ideas thrown up during the
debate on motion but _not yet incorporated_ into any theory.

A _genuine seeker after truth_ must be open to the
possibility that our world-view may need major adjustment.
The recent experience of _Yang-Mills_ (gauge) theory shows
how a powerful concept can lie dormant until some further
element is added, and only then is progress possible.

It was the same with the Copernican revolution. Copernicus's
original proposal had a modest effect on astronomy until
Kepler added a new (and strongly Machian) dimension.

For this reason I felt this book should not only report the
ideas that were successfully incorporated into physical
theories but also other ideas which seem to have _unrealized
possibilities_; in particular, they might have a bearing on
the difficulties of scientific theories and sense
perception[^s4].

For this reason some fundamental philosophical questions are
examined, in particular the basic divide in the 17th and
18th centuries between _materialism_ and _idealism_ (in
which mind and perceptions are primary).

Broadly speaking, the materialists (or realists) were
identified with the absolute Newtonian approach to motion,
the idealists with the relational Machian approach, though
there were exceptions, above all Einstein. Closely related
to this divide is the tension between the concrete and the
abstract.

No apology should be made for this inclusion of unresolved
philosophical disputes. GR itself is one of the most
dramatic examples of the transmutation by genius and
empirical facts of initially abstract philosophical ideas
into a concrete physical theory of both motion and geometry
which is amenable to testing to an amazing degree.

The clouds are no doubt the same as they were in the 17th
century, that heroic age of science, but indeed how
differently they are now perceived to move. I rest my case
for a historical and philosophical presentation and hope
that airing these questions will help clarify the problem of
the _origin of inertia_. I also want to encourage the reader
to think afresh about these matters and ask questions as
fundamental as those posed by Copernicus, Kepler, Newton,
Mach and Einstein.

The inclusion of the pre-Newtonian history and the fact that
Newton marks a clear dividing line make it convenient to
divide the study into two volumes.

This first volumne treats the _pre-Newtonian origin of
dynamics_, its formulation by Newton, and the nineteenth
century clarification of certain conceptual aspects of
dynamics left in an obscure form by Newton. It is firstly
a study of how the prevailing concepts of space, time, and
motion influenced the final structure of Newtonian dynamics
at each of the intermediate stages that led to the Newtonian
synthesis. But this also makes it a history of the
_discovery of dynamics_ itself.

To ensure that volume 1 can be read as such a history in its
own right, the scope of the book has been extended and
contains material not strictly essential to the
absolute/relative debate, particularly in astronomy.
However, almost all aspects of the discovery of dynamics
have some bearing on the debate.

The main stress is on _conceptual developments_. For this
reason, I hope it will interest working scientists by
casting light on the origins of their discipline, and will
also appeal to those interested in historical and
philosophical questions.

I also hope 'The Discovery of Dynamics' will fill a gap in
the historical literature. The ground it covers has been
extremely well trodden, but it seems there is a gap between
the books that treat the general history of ideas, such as:

* Kuhn's: The Copernican Revolution[^31] and
* Koyre's: From the Closed World to the Infinite
  Universe[^32]

and books by professional historians that treat specialized
subjects:

* eg. Jammer's three books on force, mass, and space[^33]
* and Westfall's The Concept of Force in Newton's
  Physics[^34]

I don't think any book quite covers the same field,
certainly not examining the discovery of dynamics alongside
the evolution of the absolute and relative debate. By
restricting the focus to dynamics, I was able to concentrate
on the development of key concepts, which undoubtedly has
a unique fascination.

Indeed, it seems to me that the discovery of dynamics rests
on a _dozen key events_, almost all associated with the
precise mathematical formulation of empirical facts about
observed motions. We will clearly identify these pivotal
events, show how the insights were achieved, and put them in
the proper perspective.

In fact I identified thirteen insights, divided into:

* 6 associated with _celestial_ motions,
* 6 associated with _terrestrial_ motions,
* and the odd 13th, making the baker's dozen, being the
  _mathematical insight_ behind the Copernican revolution,
  which linked together for the first time the theory of
  celestial and terrestrial motions and was therefore a key
  event in the emergence of dynamics.

These _thirteen insights_, do not include the discovery of
the geometry of the _three-dimensional_ world nor the final
synthesis of all the elements by Newton which created
dynamics, which can be regarded as the geometry of the
_four-dimensional_ world.

It is in this respect the present study differs strongly
from 'history of ideas' approaches, which often avoid
technical details.

However it is through technical details that the _electric
current_ of discovery runs. If we do not follow the current,
we will not understand what happens and why.

To use another image: the technical details are like the
_hinges of a door_ opening onto a new vista. The change in
view they facilitate seems out of all proportion to what
they represent. But their immense strength comes from the
fact that like hinges, they are mounted on a secure support:
successful mathematical descriptions of observed phenomena.
And we cannot understand why the door swings until we see
how the hinges work.

This is the reason ancient astronomy is examined, for it
provided the foundation of much of what followed. In fact,
Chaps. 3, 5, 6 represent a self-contained history of
astronomy up to the discovery of Keplers laws, though the
material has been written with an eye to its significance
for the subsequent interpretation of Kepler's discoveries.

If volume 1 is straightforward for the reader familiar with
Newtonian dynamics, then volume 2 which culminates in the
creation of GR, will get progressively tougher. I fear that
my readers will fall away chapter by chapter as we approach
the Holy Grail. Alas there is little that can be done about
this.

By no stretch of the imagination is GR anything but
sophisticated, a fact which doesn't detract from its
conceptual beauty. By concentrating on the bare essentials
of the conceptual thread, I hope to keep the reader with me
as long as possible. There will be no gratuitous
mathematics, but neither will it be shirked when needed to
present the essential arguments. All I can say to the reader
is, keep going as long as you can. It's worth it to get even
an inkling of the denouement which that _unsurpassed genius
Einstein_ has left us as our best understanding of the
central problem of motion: a problem that already vexed the
greatest minds about 2,500 years ago.

So then, let us start. But first, the reader embarking on
a journey from Aristotle to Einstein may want to know the
conclusion at the end of this trek. If Frodo is on a journey
to Mordor[^35] will there at least be a positive outcome? In
a word: _Is motion absolute or relative_?

No definite answer can be given. In one sense motion is
unquestionably relative, since no experimentalist can
measure a motion that is not relative. It will be shown that
the real distinction is in the _structure and predictive
power_ of the theory used to describe motion.

When applied to a _finite universe_, Newton's theory is
found to have less predictive power than a Machian theory.
In fact Newtonian theory can be _recast in purely relative
terms_, but is found to have an ungainly and somewhat
arbitrary structure and also is less predictive than
theories with a Machian structure.[^36]

The positive conclusion of this study, reached at the end of
Volume 2, is that GR suffers from no such defect. At least
regarding its _basic structure_, GR's design is almost as
predictive as one could imagine: it is a 'ne plus ultra'
though there are some worrying technical details in the
mathematics.

(For relativists: this refers to the fact that Wheeler's
'_thin-sandwich_' conjecture doesnt seem to be an
appropriate way of approaching the initial-value problem in
GR[^37]. There are also uncertainties related to the part
played by quantum theory in the whole absolute / relative
question, to say nothing of the putative successor theories
to GR. Finally there remains the difficulties mentioned in
[^s4])

(Barbour 2012: GR _just fails_ to be fully machian. FXQi
research proposal)

So much can be promised to the traveller who persists to the
end. But there looms a larger difficulty: _the possible
infinity of the universe_. If the world is truly infinite,
then the chain of cause and effect can never be closed. New
influences can always 'swim in' from across our most distant
horizon and it would not appear to be possible under such
circumstances to close the circle. Even Frodo had to set out
again into the unknown once his mission to Mordor was
accomplished. Whether that is our fate remains to be seen.

    The _time_ aspect of the relativity of motion problem
    has been curiously neglected. In fact, it will be argued
    in Vol. 2 that there are _two Mach's Principles_:

    1. First: relating to the problem of _space_ & the
       relativity of motion;

    2. Second: relating to the relativity of _time_.[^18]

    What is normally called Mach's Principle is identified
    with the first of these. The Second Mach's Principle
    will play a large role in Vol. 2.

[^s3]:
    Note the word 'relativity' in SR does not have the same
    meaning as Mach's assertion of the relativity of motion.
    The significance of SR for the Machian problem is that
    it introduced a brand new problem regarding the nature
    of time, since it showed the naive concept of absolute
    simultaneity (a unique 'now' throughout the entire
    universe), must be radically revised.

    By a non-relativistic formulation of the Machian
    problem, I mean a formulation in which the _concept of
    simultaneity_ is still allowed.

[^s4]:
    If GR is Machian in this basic sense, it must be
    admitted that it is partially Machian in another sense.
    Mach had an extremely radical philosophy; he asserted
    the sole task of science was to establish correlations
    between directly observed phenomena. In his youth, an
    age still dominated by Newtonian material bodies, it was
    much easier to assume everything of dynamical
    significance (ie. all masses in the universe) be
    presented directly to the human senses.

    However, the revolutionary creation of field theory
    during Mach's lifetime led to a mathematical description
    of phenomena where the basic concepts are further
    removed from sense perception. It is true that these
    developments present severe interpretational problems
    and make GR un-Machian if judged as a theory which
    correlates sense perceptions directly.

    Short of a drastic revision of ideas, in which mind and
    perception are taken as primary and matter secondary, as
    advocated by Schrodinger in _What is Life_? and _Mind
    and Matter_[^26] and also by Wigner[^27] I do not see
    how this situation can be easily changed.
