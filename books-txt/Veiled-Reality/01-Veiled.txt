### 1 Philosophy and Physics

- This introductory chapter could equally be titled "Philosophy
  for Physicists." Physicists are often put off by the way many
  contemporary philosophers treat seemingly obvious notions, such
  as that of an "object." When most philosophers hear "object,"
  typically they immediately picture a subject for whom that
  thing is an object. Physicists, with rare exceptions, do not
  habitually think in those terms. 

- This difference is a frequent source of misunderstanding: to
  the average philosopher it is so obvious that "object" implies
  "subject" that they often do not even mention it, let alone
  explain why it is obvious.

Where do such ingrained philosophical conceptions come from?
Largely from the great classics of philosophy. Therefore
a physicist seeking philosophical perspectives on quantum theory
should not confine themselves to contemporary philosophy texts.
To grasp many issues deeply enough to discuss them, one must
consult the primary sources. For that reason several sections
below describe and critically analyze — from a physicist's point
of view — aspects of Hume's and Kant's work that are relevant to
the interpretational problems considered in this book. When, in
the final chapters, we try to assemble the pieces of information
quantum mechanics gives us and weigh the different
interpretations theorists have proposed, this background will
prove useful.

This first chapter also surveys a few broad questions raised by
quantum theory and introduces general notions that are helpful
when attempting to interpret it.

### 1.1 A Reminder of Some Basic Tenets in General Philosophy

In physics circles one often hears the motto "Remember that
physics is an experimental science." At first glance this appears
straightforward, but it admits two different readings.

One reading is that, ultimately, only through the testimony of
our senses can we learn partial truths about what exists "out
there" independently of us. In this view physics is a science
about reality, and human experience is the final criterion for
distinguishing true statements about reality from false ones. The
alternative reading is that, taking for granted that reliable
knowledge comes only from experience, we must push this logic to
the end and conceive of physics as a science about human
experience; anything beyond experience becomes "metaphysics."

The first interpretation is the commonsense one and was the
implicit stance of early empiricists. For example, in Locke's
distinction between primary (position, shape) and secondary
(color, taste) qualities, primary qualities are taken to be
properties of objects, while secondary qualities are treated as
merely human responses to the object's influence. This view
sounds reasonable and remains the default for many scientists;
for brevity I will call it "conventional realism," or more simply
"realism" (though, as will be seen at the end of the book, there
exist coherent conceptions also labelled "realism" that depart
from this common view).

The second interpretation — that physics is about human
experience rather than about independent reality — initially
attracts some physicists (mainly theorists), since physics does
center on what we observe and do. But enthusiasm wanes once it is
noticed that this second reading must also encompass the
so-called primary qualities. That would mean that the positions
of planets, the reading of a pointer on a dial, or the placement
of a table relative to walls are elements only of our experience,
without a one-to-one relationship with anything beyond that
experience. Many physicists find this unintelligible and even
absurd. Nevertheless, this second interpretation aligns with
a longstanding philosophical doctrine that has been defended by
many notable thinkers. Moreover, if accepted wholeheartedly it
dissolves some of physics' deepest problems. For these reasons it
cannot be dismissed and must be taken seriously — which forces us
to take seriously the philosophical doctrine tied to it.

We first need a clear label for that doctrine. Common terms such
as "empiricism," "positivism," and "phenomenalism" are ambiguous:
they do not make explicit whether the doctrine endorses the first
or the second reading of "physics is experimental." Even after
reading substantial works by empiricist philosophers it is often
unclear which sense an author means. Professional philosophers
can justify this ambiguity: many basic questions are best
approached indirectly via more tractable epistemological problems
rather than by blunt definitions. That approach can be productive
in general philosophy. However, quantum mechanics raises sharp,
concrete problems that force us to "take sides" and remove the
ambiguity. For our purposes we therefore need a generic name for
the family of philosophical theories that reject conventional
realism in favor of the second interpretation — namely that
physics is fundamentally a science about human experience.

One suitable name is "idealism." The term has been used for
several doctrines, some now obsolete and some hostile to science;
for this reason many philosophers of science who would accept the
second interpretation nevertheless avoid the label. To avoid
unnecessary terminological resistance I will sometimes use the
neutral coinage "phenomenism." In this chapter I will use both
terms in a broad sense to mean any doctrine emphasizing that our
perceptual, practical, and explanatory faculties shape the
concepts we use to describe experience — with the corollaries
that we've no direct access to reality "as such," we only know
phenomena in the philosophical sense, and therefore even
so-called primary qualities (including relative positions) are
human modes of apprehension. These views motivate a substantial
part of contemporary philosophical research, and this is one of
the reasons we will study them in some detail.

This broad definition leaves room for distinctions. I will call
"radical idealists" (or radical phenomenists) those who assert
that existence has no meaning except insofar as it can be
referred to experience. I will use "moderate idealism" (or
"moderate phenomenism") for thinkers who do not go that far or
who remain agnostic on that point. Readers, especially
scientists, should note that as defined here idealism is not an
esoteric doctrine. Indeed a number of prominent scientists
— Helmholtz, Poincaré, Schrödinger, and others — were clearly
idealists in this broad sense. That is another reason to analyze
these doctrines carefully.

### 1.2 "For" and "Against" Idealism

Philosophers historically developed idealism. Protagoras declared
"Man is the measure of all things" in the fourth century B.C.
Descartes used a similar idea as an intermediate step in his
arguments. Berkeley formulated the first fully elaborated
idealist doctrine: his doctrine was rather radical, though it
retained God as an independently existing being; his motto "esse
est percipi" ("to be is to be perceived") captures part of his
view. Kant is usually considered a moderate idealist: he accepted
the idea of things-in-themselves existing independently of
knowledge, but he claimed we cannot have genuine knowledge of
them. Kant argued that space, time, and causality are not
independently existing entities but modes of our sensibility and
understanding. Later followers of Kant often adopted more radical
positions and rejected the notion of things-in-themselves.

A full pedagogical account of Berkeley, Kant, and the neo- and
post-Kantians is beyond our scope and can be found in standard
philosophy texts; here we assume familiarity with the main lines.
More relevant for scientists are two complementary questions: (a)
what reasons and arguments led these philosophers to idealism,
and (b) how did they, or how could they have, responded to
apparently decisive objections?

To keep the analysis concise, consider Kant's standpoint.
Following Berkeley, Kant began from a plausible observation: any
specific knowledge of an external object is obtained through the
senses, hence is indirect and potentially questionable (think of
the broken-stick experiment). What we know directly and certainly
is only our ideas (a term that then covered sensory data).
Concerning space, Kant insisted that the most secure fact about
space is that we need the notion to order our sense-data;
phenomena are produced by this ordering.

This observation has far-reaching consequences. For instance, it
can support a simple argument for the ideality of space.
Regardless of whether things-in-themselves are in space, we are
obliged, as Kant emphasized, to represent them spatially. From
that alone, the hypothesis that things-in-themselves are truly in
space seems redundant: the spatiality we ascribe to them could be
a mode of representation rather than an attribute of the
things-in-themselves.

An immediate counterargument to this point is that the hypothesis
that things-in-themselves are in space — that is, that we
perceive them approximately as they are — explains a great many
facts simply and straightforwardly, so it is attractive. Kant
would likely have replied that to "explain" is to find causes,
and in this context that means explaining the detailed causes of
phenomena. But, following Hume, the notion of causality cannot be
derived from experience or pure reason: for Kant, causality is an
a priori mode of understanding, a structure "in" us rather than
"in" things-in-themselves. Consequently (a) giving up the view
that things-in-themselves are known and in space does not require
abandoning causality among phenomena, and (b) conversely, the
causal patterns we observe among phenomena are not evidence that
things-in-themselves are known and are in space.

Hume's critical analysis of causality plays a central role in
this line of thought, as Kant himself acknowledged. Therefore, to
evaluate idealism — and Kant's version in particular — we must
examine Hume's arguments in some detail. That is the topic of the
next section. Meanwhile, a few comments on the discussion so far
are in order.

1. When Kant speaks of space he means Euclidean space, and when
   he speaks of time he means Newtonian absolute time. His claim
   that these concepts are a priori modes of sensibility implies
   that physics must be formulated within those concepts. Since
   Einstein's relativity this is known to be false; that fact
   therefore warrants reservations about the particulars of
   Kant's system.

2. A similar remark applies to Kant's conception of causality.
   Kant identified causality with determinism in the spirit of
   Leibniz's principle of sufficient reason and in line with
   common views of his time. Modern physical theories have
   undermined that identification, so Kant's treating causality
   as an a priori element of understanding — and thus as a mold
   for physics — requires skepticism about the details of his
   position.

3. Kant lived in a period when geometry and Newtonian mechanics
   were thought to have achieved certainty. Popper pointed out
   a contradiction between that confidence and Hume's skepticism
   about causality, since Hume had shown that the notion of
   causality cannot be rationally justified. According to Popper,
   Kant preferred to eliminate the difficulty by taking causality
   as an a priori structure, thereby removing the need to justify
   it. Popper's view is that later developments in physics
   vindicated Hume's skepticism and exposed the certainty Kant
   attributed to science as illusory. There is truth in this
   critique. Nonetheless, for anyone who accepts Hume's analysis
   but still believes that science can reveal "how reality really
   is," Kant's problem remains relevant. Also, Popper's remarks
   do not invalidate the Kant-like argument for the ideality of
   space outlined above, since that argument does not rely on
   certainty.

To summarize: aspects of idealism — particularly elements of
Kantianism — are now obsolete, including Kant's main motivations.
But the realist conviction that primary qualities are possessed
by objects and that space (or space-time) is an independently
existing arena does not seem to be established purely by logic or
by empirical evidence. If that conviction is grounded at all, the
grounding is subtler and less obvious than typically assumed; its
extent and character must be analyzed and debated.

### 1.3 Discussing the Scope of Hume's Analysis

Nowadays, everyone accepts the relativity of space and time in
the Einsteinian sense. Still, in relativity theory space-time and
events are not "relative" to human knowledge: they are usually
conceived as existing independently of human observers, much as
nineteenth-century physicists thought space and objects existed
in some absolute sense. This is worth noting because some
writers, hoping to make the idealist claim about the
nonindependence of space and time less counterintuitive, suggest
substituting the term "relativity" for "ideality." That helps
insofar as it prevents conflating moderate or radical idealist
insights with specific obsolete Kantian doctrines. But it does
not go far enough. The core claim of modern philosophical views
linked to idealism is, in Kantian fashion, that the existence of
space-time, events, and indeed existence itself is logically
secondary to knowledge. No euphemism can erase the considerable
rupture this standpoint implies with deeply ingrained ways of
thinking.

Therefore we must ask, independently of terminology, whether and
to what extent this rupture is justified. Since the historical
arguments for idealism rely heavily on Hume's analysis of
causality, we must examine Hume's argument carefully. His
analysis proceeds in several steps, which we now describe and
discuss.

Description

Hume begins by observing that in many cases our conviction about
the reality of a fact, or the presence of something, rests on
inferences based on cause-and-effect relations. For example,
hearing a human voice in a dark room leads us to infer that
somebody is present; finding a watch on a desert island leads us
to infer that someone was there before.

His second step is to note that such cause-effect relations are
learned exclusively from experience. Experience, not reasoning,
teaches us that human voices are heard only when humans are
present (Gramophones and transistors were not known in Hume's
time) and that watches do not grow like trees or grass. Hume
stresses that this empirical origin of causal knowledge applies
even in cases where we might imagine pure reason could discover
the effects of a cause. Consider a moving billiard ball: we might
think that by reason alone we could predict the outcome of its
collision with a stationary ball. But a priori it is just as
conceivable that after the collision both balls might be at rest
or that the moving ball might bounce back. Only past experience
tells us which process actually occurs.

Hume's third and central step is to ask why we've confidence
that past experience allows us to infer a cause-effect relation
and apply it to predict future events — in modern terms, to
justify induction. He concludes that no satisfactory
justification exists. Cause-effect relations cannot be
established by pure reason; and if we try to justify induction by
appealing to the fact that it worked in past instances, we fall
into circularity: that appeal itself is inductive and presupposes
what we are attempting to prove. In short, there is no
noncircular justification of induction.

Kant and his successors turned Hume's negative result into an
argument for the ideality of causality and, by extension, for
moderate idealism: if causality is an a priori element of
understanding that structures experience, then Hume's difficulty
disappears. But for the overall picture to be coherent, phenomena
(the causes and effects) themselves must be regarded as mental
rather than physical; otherwise it would be unclear how an
essentially mental causal structure can legitimately be applied
to phenomena.

Discussion

Hume's argument is fundamental for how we interpret the world and
scientific results. One observation is that Hume argues as
a philosopher (and a very good one), not primarily as
a scientist. His typical examples are of the type "bread
nourishes," "spring makes fruit trees blossom," and so on. These
examples make it easy to emphasize the purely empirical character
of causal relations. Even when he mentions billiard balls, where
Newtonian mechanics gives general laws, he does not highlight
that the same mechanical laws account for many distinct
cause-effect regularities. Can that fact undermine the general
force of Hume's argument?

This is subtle. Simply pointing to the existence of physical laws
does not remove the motivation behind Hume's skepticism about
induction. Hume would reply that such laws were themselves
inferred from finite experimental evidence, and that invoking
their universality presupposes the validity of induction, so we
again face circularity.

That response is epistemological and does not appeal to ontology
(what really exists). At first glance it looks immune to
a realist reply. But one might try to avoid circularity by
postulating the universality of the structures of reality that
give rise to observed laws. This postulate can be defended on the
basis of maximal simplicity: it is simpler to assume
once-and-for-all that reality has general structures reflected in
our universal laws (Maxwell's equations, etc.) than to posit, for
every particular prediction, a separate assumption that induction
will work in that case. Many scientists find this simplicity
argument persuasive.

Yet this defense depends on the notion of simplicity, which is
human-centered and not a fully adequate basis for such general
claims. To make the argument succeed one would have to assume
a kind of preestablished harmony between reality and the human
mind, so that the real structures of reality appear simple to us.
That postulate is bold and cannot be justified by the success of
science without reintroducing circularity. Many philosophers
therefore find it arbitrary and metaphysical, though scientists
often accept it pragmatically.

A reasonable middle position is this: accept that we lack a fully
rational, assumption-free justification of our firm belief that
physical laws will hold tomorrow, and be modest about the
epistemic status of that belief. But this failure of rational
justification does not force us to abandon the belief, because it
does not falsify it; the postulate of stable, simple structures
remains self-consistent. If one adopts that view, then radical
idealism, moderate idealism, and conventional realism are all
a priori admissible doctrines; philosophy alone cannot fully
decide their relative merits. In later chapters we will examine
whether physics itself can provide further guidance.

### 1.4 Existence and Intersubjectivity

One reason physicists must engage with the philosophical problems
sketched above is that many physicists who study the foundations
of contemporary physics (for example, when writing a book)
implicitly adopt a phenomenist standpoint. The very nature of
foundational problems tends to lead them toward positions akin to
moderate or radical idealism. Some physicists may be surprised or
displeased by this observation, but it is undeniable. This
tendency becomes especially clear when authors explain that
quantum concepts, unlike classical concepts, cannot be taken as
directly describing an independent reality. That is true, but
does it mean quantum concepts describe reality indirectly? If so,
in what sense?

Some authors begin by noting that although no single quantum
concept fully captures the elements of reality it probes, each
concept provides reliable information that, combined with others,
can build increasingly faithful pictures of reality. Up to that
point they do not contradict idealism. But those who adhere to
the Copenhagen "orthodoxy" cannot stop there.

---[chunk-boundary]---

They must—and do—add that, according to the basic idea of
complementarity, quantum reality cannot be completely described
by a single picture. A full description requires a duality of
mutually contradictory (but “complementary”) pictures. We must
therefore ask: is this completed conception still compatible with
realism? Clearly not. Although this extra specification is
necessary for exactness, it plainly undermines the “realistic
message” of the earlier account. If the best description we can
give of reality requires us to use two mutually contradictory
pictures at once, we cannot legitimately claim to have described
“reality as it really is.” As will be explained in more detail
below, the kind of “reality” contemporary physics describes under
the orthodox Copenhagen interpretation is only an “effective” or
“empirical” reality — essentially the set of phenomena in the
Kantian sense.

These circumstances force physicists to address the two main
objections that realists typically bring against any form of
idealism (radical or moderate) and consider conclusive.
Nonrealist philosophers often dismiss these objections as naive,
but scientists generally expect a fair treatment. Let us
therefore examine the two principal objections: those about (past
and present) existence, and about intersubjective agreement.

Existence

Put bluntly, the objection runs: phenomenism and related
philosophical doctrines essentially adopt the core argument of
idealism and define phenomena as mental events made up of
sensations together with a priori forms that the mind applies to
those sensations (Kant’s phrase was “undetermined objects of
empirical intuition”). This makes the concept of phenomenon
anthropocentric. That is absurd, since, as everyone knows, Earth,
the stars, and the rest of the universe — with all the phenomena
that occur in them — already existed at times when no human
beings or other living organisms existed.

Idealists and their allies reply that this objection is flawed
because it covertly treats time as an externally given, absolute
parameter, which contradicts the basic ideas of the philosophical
doctrine in question. That reply is cogent. Still, the objection
is worth raising because it highlights that phenomenism and
similar positions must be accepted in their entirety, including
their most disconcerting consequences, if they are to be
consistent. From a Kantian standpoint, to assert that the
Universe existed before mankind simply means that we've the
conceptual possibility of organizing our collective experience by
describing it that way. The assertion has no meaning beyond this
organisation of experience, and understood in this way it is not
inconsistent with the anthropocentric character of phenomena.

The realist’s complaint can also be stated more sharply: idealism
makes humans the cause of everything we observe — events,
objects, tables, quasars, and so on — and it is absurd to suppose
that humans somehow generate stars. The idealist answers that the
realist is illegitimately using an absolute notion of cause to
criticize a theory whose basic premise is precisely that this
notion must be revised. A principle of (declared or hidden)
idealism is that causal relations hold between phenomena, and
phenomena themselves are compounds of sensations and a priori
structural forms into which sensations are cast. To apply the
cause–effect concept to a relation between two elements when one
of them (the human being, conceived as an unpersonal
epistemological subject) is not a phenomenon is to use the causal
concept beyond its domain of validity; this is faulty reasoning.

Still more generally, the realist may insist that we must not
confuse things with our perceptions of them — that things “really
exist” independently of us. The idealist responds, following
a critical analysis of the notion of meaning, that if all our
knowledge truly derives from perception, then saying that things
“really exist” has no further meaning: if we've perceptions of
things, then whether or not the things exist independent of
perception makes no difference; hence “to be perceived” is
equivalent to “to exist.” Heisenberg expresses this point
succinctly when describing Berkeley’s standpoint: “If really our
whole knowledge is derived from perception, to say that the
things ‘really exist’ has no meaning; for if we've ‘perceptions
of things’ it makes no difference whether the thus perceived
things exist or not. Hence ‘to be perceived’ is equivalent to ‘to
exist.’” Whatever our final judgment on the existence problem,
these arguments clearly have weight.

Intersubjective Agreement

“Intersubjective agreement” is a broad expression. It can mean
agreement about moral values, logical rules, mathematical
theorems, and so on. Here I restrict the term to agreement about
contingent observational facts: for example, if there is one
teapot on the table around which we sit, we all agree that there
is one teapot (neither zero nor two nor three, but precisely
one). From now on that is how the phrase will be used.

Intersubjective agreement is an empirical fact (in normal
circumstances; we set aside cases such as intoxicated observers).
The question is: does its existence argue in favor of realism,
i.e., does it provide a valid objection against idealism and its
variants?

The immediate answer appears to be yes. If Alice and Bob both
report that at noon they saw no teapot on the table, that at five
o’clock they saw one, and that at six o’clock they again saw
none, then the assumption that at noon there really was no
teapot, at five there really was one, and that it was really
removed between five and six, seems the best explanation for this
remarkable correlated sequence. If, on the other hand, the
idealist insists that the statement “the teapot really exists”
has no meaning beyond describing how Alice (and similarly Bob)
organizes her sensations, then this must also be true for claims
about whether the teapot “is really there” at particular times.
The fact that Alice and Bob always report the same sensations
then becomes puzzling — a kind of continually repeated miracle.
The realist is therefore entitled to press the idealist: what
explanation can the idealist offer that is as simple as the
realist’s commonsense account?

Surprisingly, few idealist philosophers have addressed this
challenge about contingent intersubjective agreement; the
literature tends to focus on agreement about noncontingent facts
such as mathematics. One can try to reconstruct the possible
defenses idealists might mount. Two lines of response suggest
themselves: (a) a general psychological remark that humans
habitually seek explanations even when none are required, and (b)
a more technical claim that the realist’s alleged explanation is
not a genuine scientific explanation. Both responses ought to
unsettle a physicist, so it is worth examining them in some
detail.

Response (a) can be illustrated by Cassirer’s 1910 discussion of
the atom concept. By 1910, several experiments already pointed
convincingly to the reality of atoms. Cassirer noted how we speak
of the number of atoms in a volume of gas and attribute similar
heat capacities to atoms of simple substances. He accepted that
the concept of an atom has a “logical function” of relating
various series of measured data — a “mediating concept” — but he
refused to regard the claim that atoms really exist as
a meaningful scientific proposition, calling it “metaphysical.”
That position was striking in 1910, when classical physics was at
its height and there was no sign that quantum questions would
later trouble the simple classical picture of particles existing
“out there.” Why would Cassirer reject the straightforward
inference that atoms probably exist because their existence
explains the data? His implicit answer is that physicists tend to
crave explanations that are ultimately metaphysical and therefore
illegitimate. Quine’s remark that theoretical entities of physics
(electrons, say) are epistemologically on a par with Homer’s gods
— convenient fictions introduced to explain empirical facts
— points in the same skeptical direction.

Response (b) is related and sheds further light on (a).
Reichenbach argued that to “explain” a fact is to link it with
some general law. For example, we explain a dog’s barking by
invoking the general law that dogs commonly bark at strangers, or
explain fossil seashells on a mountainside by invoking geological
processes and the general law that sea animals do not live on
land. If one accepts this criterion, the realist’s explanation
that Alice and Bob both saw the teapot “because the teapot is
really there” is not an explanation: it appeals to a metaphysical
notion of reality rather than linking the observations to
a general law.

A closely related criticism was voiced by Erwin Schrödinger, who
explicitly examined what he called the “arithmetical problem”
— the puzzle that arises because of the multiplicity of minds.
Schrödinger points out that the realist’s account assumes
a similarity between Alice’s mental picture and an external real
world R, and similarly between Bob’s mental picture and R. But
R is not observed. For the realist’s account to succeed one would
have to postulate a causal relation between R and Alice’s (and
Bob’s) mental pictures; this uses the causal concept outside its
well-established domain of validity, since R is not a phenomenon.
Thus Schrödinger argues that the realist’s purported explanation
lacks the necessary empirical anchoring.

One may therefore conclude that the insistence of idealists and
related philosophers on restricting causality to phenomena is
decisive in their arguments. Critics may charge that this
restriction is an arbitrary or overly strict rule that prevents
idealists from appreciating straightforward, commonsense
arguments. That criticism would be stronger if every instance of
intersubjective agreement admitted a simple realist explanation;
but that is not the case. Quantum mechanics provides clear
counterexamples.

Consider a case in conventional quantum mechanics: Alice and Bob
perform sequential position measurements on the same electron,
each using their own instrument, with Bob measuring immediately
after Alice. Suppose the electron’s wave function prior to
Alice’s measurement is not a delta function (the generic
situation). Quantum mechanics unambiguously predicts that, when
they later compare notes, Alice and Bob will report the same
position. But before Alice’s measurement the formalism tells us
the electron did not have a definite position, and so it was not
really at that place prior to measurement. Thus the obvious
realist “teapot-like” explanation of intersubjective agreement
fails in this quantum case.

This example illustrates that our commonsense objections to
idealists should not be taken at face value. That said, the
idealist and related philosophical positions do face a genuine
problem about intersubjective agreement, and their defenders have
often treated the issue too lightly. We will examine it further
in later chapters in the light of quantum theory. Meanwhile, the
difficulty provides some reason to be wary of accepting radical
or even moderate idealism uncritically. There are also
non-intuitive arguments against idealism. For instance, when Kant
notes that because our minds necessarily conceive things as being
in space, this does not prove they are “really” in space, one can
reply that perhaps the mind adopted the spatial conception
because objects actually are in space; through adaptive
evolution, the mind settled on the representation that best
tracks reality. The matter therefore deserves careful reflection
and will be taken up again after a systematic study of current
physical theories.

### 1.5 A Note About Scientific Revolutions

Earlier in this chapter, we looked at why so many philosophers
are uneasy with the idea of an ontology—that is, any claim about
what “really exists.” That skepticism runs deep in the history of
philosophy, as we’ve seen. But it’s not only ancient ideas that
fuel it. Some modern philosophers trace this mistrust to
something else entirely: the history of scientific revolutions.

The French philosopher Gaston Bachelard was probably the first to
talk seriously about this, using the phrase “epistemological
breakings.” Later, Thomas Kuhn and others picked up the idea and
developed it further. Their work inspired a few overblown
claims—like the idea that science is mostly arbitrary—but the
central point still holds. Every so often, science, especially
physics, has advanced by completely rethinking its foundations.
The shift from Newton’s idea of gravity as a force to Einstein’s
concept of curved space-time is a textbook example. Seen in this
light, it’s understandable why some philosophers doubt that
science will ever uncover the ultimate truth about “how things
really are.”

That’s basically their argument. And to be fair, their skepticism
does line up with some of the themes explored in this book. But
I don’t think it makes those explorations redundant. Most
physicists—myself included—don’t believe past scientific
revolutions prove that we’ll never reach a deeper understanding
of reality. The reason is simple: physics has changed enormously.
In scale, resources, and sophistication, it’s almost
unrecognizable compared to its early stages. It has grown into
a mature science, supported by a vast community of researchers
and unprecedented levels of technical and financial investment.

The famous examples of “scientific revolutions,” on the other
hand, mostly come from the early days of physics, when the field
was still taking shape. So projecting those past upheavals into
the future is risky. Back then, physics was fragmented into
separate, loosely connected areas, and a theory could succeed
even if its underlying ideas were off the mark—Newton’s “force of
gravity” being a case in point. But today, physics is highly
integrated. Its different branches fit together in ways that make
such deep, structural mistakes much harder to imagine.

There’s a good analogy here. When Columbus set out on his voyage,
his success rested partly on a serious mistake—he thought the
Earth was much smaller than it is and believed he could reach
China by sailing west. That kind of lucky miscalculation could
only happen because geography was still young. Today, with our
detailed maps and global data, no one could make that kind of
error and still stumble upon a “new world.” Physicists argue that
the same holds true for their own field: it’s too mature, too
interconnected, for that kind of dramatic conceptual blunder to
happen again.

This mindset—often unspoken but widely shared among
scientists—probably explains why so many physicists and
astrophysicists aren’t persuaded by the “scientific revolutions”
argument that philosophers find so compelling. When someone like
Stephen Hawking talks about one day discovering “God’s
blueprints” of the universe, he’s assuming, implicitly, that
physics has reached a point of maturity where its foundations are
solid enough to make such a quest meaningful.



### 1.6 Some Questions Raised by Physics

So far we've discussed general philosophical issues. In this
section I introduce some more specific questions and concepts
that are still philosophical but particularly relevant for
physicists and for the physical analyses in this book.

### Weak and Strong Objectivity
    - $MY_NOTES/physics/metaphysics/strong-weak-objectivity.txt

- That scientific statements are objective is a truism, but it
  conceals that “objectivity” is not a single obvious notion. 
    - In science the word is used in at least two distinct
      senses, and it's important to clarify this before we
      proceed.

Some scientific statements can be interpreted as directly
reporting attributes of the things under study. 
    - Examples include “Two oppositely charged bodies attract
      each other in such and such a way,” 
    - or “The Earth’s magnetic field is such-and-such,”
    - and, more generally, the basic laws of classical
      nonrelativistic physics (except statistical
      thermodynamics). 

- Such statements have a form that allows them to be read as
  descriptions of _how things really are_; 
    - I call these strongly objective statements.

- But not all scientific statements are strongly objective. Many
  statements in statistical thermodynamics explicitly refer to
  what is possible to observe (for example via coarse graining)
  or to what kinds of systems can be prepared. 
    - Similarly, conventional textbook QM often expresses its
      basic laws by referring to what will be observed in
      specified circumstances. 
    - These statements are objective in the sense that their
      truth is the same for everyone; but their form makes it
      impossible to interpret them straightforwardly as
      descriptions of how things actually are. I call such
      statements weakly objective.

A few remarks about these definitions:

1. Any strongly objective statement can trivially be rephrased
   into a weakly objective form. Instead of asserting that two
   oppositely charged bodies attract, one could say that, upon
   measurement, they will be found to attract. The converse is
   not generally true. While some weakly objective statements can
   be converted to strongly objective ones, doing so for the
   basic statements of statistical thermodynamics is certainly
   nontrivial and, by most specialists’ judgment, impossible. We
   will encounter a comparable situation in quantum mechanics.

2. The distinction between strong and weak objectivity has
   nothing to do with truth versus falsehood. A statement such as
   “Bodies carrying the same electric charge attract each other”
   is a false strongly objective claim. Nor does the distinction
   coincide with materialism versus spiritualism, despite
   occasional confusion by commentators.

3. Weak objectivity of concepts: statements that are not fully
   strongly objective are sometimes used to define concepts or to
   assign meanings to familiar terms. Bohr’s use of the concept
   “measuring instrument” illustrates this. As will be recalled
   in Chapter 11 and later, Bohr’s usage makes an instrument
   a set of objects defined by reference not to their material
   structure but to how they are used (a pointer or a movable
   diaphragm is part of the instrument only if it is used to gain
   information). Saying how an object is used is a statement that
   refers to human operations and hence cannot be strongly
   objective in the sense above. Within a well-specified
   experiment, however, there is no ambiguity about what is
   called “the instrument”; the specification is the same for
   everyone and is objective in that limited sense. In general,
   I call weakly objective those concepts defined by references
   to human operations, and strongly objective those whose
   definition does not involve such references. This distinction
   will be adequate for present purposes; we will examine it in
   greater depth later.

4. A strictness condition for realism: the distinction between
   strong and weak objectivity bears on the difference between
   conventional realism and phenomenism (idealism). By
   “conventional realism” I mean the view — not to be confused
   with Poincaré’s “conventionalism”; Poincaré’s position will be
   called “structural realism” in this book — that the ultimate
   attributes of “things as they really are” can in principle
   (perhaps asymptotically) be known by humans, although the
   concepts used may be very different from everyday notions.
   Those who accept conventional realism and insist on
   consistency should require that all basic physical laws be
   expressible in strongly objective terms. John Bell emphasized
   this point in his discussion of “beables.” Some physicists
   seeking “objective” interpretations of quantum mechanics
   nevertheless base their proposals on concepts like
   “measurement” or “preparation procedure.” The distinction
   between strong and weak objectivity shows such proposals to be
   problematic: if by “objective” one means “weakly objective,”
   then conventional quantum mechanics is already objective and
   there is nothing new to seek; if one means “strongly
   objective,” then proposals built on weakly objective concepts
   fail. More generally, for an interpretation to be truly
   “realistic” it must be grounded on notions and arguments that
   do not fundamentally refer to human operations or limitations;
   in particular it must satisfy the “truth criterion” introduced
   below. Schemata that do not meet this requirement should be
   rejected by anyone committed to conventional realism and
   strict consistency. 

5. Phenomenists, as defined earlier, should not be equated with
   doctrinaire operationalists who insist on weakly objective
   statements only. Kant willingly accepted Newtonian physics
   formulated in strongly objective terms; he interpreted those
   statements as referring to phenomena (what I called empirical
   or effective reality). It is striking that so much of physics,
   and nearly all commonsense factual knowledge, can be expressed
   in strongly objective form while nevertheless relying on
   concepts whose definitions refer to human abilities. Kant
   might have argued that the possibility of formulating the
   basic laws of physics as strongly objective statements is
   a necessary condition for science itself. If so, the example
   of conventional quantum mechanics shows that explanation is
   too sweeping and that a more refined account, informed by
   physics, is needed. Contemporary physicists have investigated
   this issue and their work will be discussed in later chapters.

Taking these remarks into account, it follows that when
a theory’s basic laws include weakly objective statements or
concepts that cannot be straightforwardly replaced by strongly
objective ones, phenomenists and conventional realists must view
the theory differently. Phenomenists can consistently accept the
theory as it stands and regard attempts to make it “more”
strongly objective as meaningless. Conventional realists, by
contrast, cannot be satisfied: they must either change the theory
or revise their philosophical standpoint.

A Truth Criterion

We all hold certain general ideas we consider immediately and
obviously valid because they are tautologies or definitions. One
such idea is that a statement that entails at least one
observable consequence that is found false cannot be correct.

Epistemologists have refined this simple idea. Duhem argued
persuasively that truly “crucial” experiments do not exist in the
actual practice of science, and many others have elaborated the
conditions under which a theory is eventually regarded as
falsified.

---[chunk-boundary]---

Duhem and his followers argued that the consequences of a theory
T are usually derived only with the help of some explicit or
implicit simplifying assumptions A. When a predicted consequence
is falsified, it often seems more reasonable to modify some of
the A’s than to abandon T itself. The same point applies here:
observable consequences of a statement are necessarily derived
using a theory—call it T again—and simplifying assumptions A. If
trouble arises, one can sometimes change those A’s in a way that
preserves both T and the statement.

Such considerations are well founded and supported by many
historical examples in science. Here, however, I want to discuss
the correctness and applicability of the criterion sketched below
under the working assumption that both the theory T and all
assumptions A that might enter the picture are strictly true.
T is assumed to be a physical, not a purely mathematical, theory
(we will specialize to quantum mechanics). More precisely, we
assume T grants the meaningfulness of contingent factual
statements such as “At such-and-such a space-time point
such-and-such a physical quantity has such-and-such a value,” and
allows one to derive observable consequences from them. For
short, call such a theory a “type Ty” theory. The truth criterion
we've in mind can then be stated precisely as follows.

Truth criterion. A factual statement can only be true if all the
observable consequences derived from it by means of a “type Ty”
theory, considered as valid, are true.

Despite its seemingly tautological character, this truth
criterion merits comment. Its validity depends on some tacit
assumptions that, reasonable as they appear, are better made
explicit. One of these assumptions concerns the phrase “all
observable consequences,” which implicitly invokes a notion of
counterfactuality that normally refers to free choice. For
a trivial example, if the considered theory is electromagnetism
and the statement being tested is that a particular piece of iron
is magnetic, the theory implies several observable consequences
whose tests require different apparatuses. To test the attraction
the piece exerts on other iron bodies, an experimenter might
place small iron filings near it; to test the induced current it
is predicted to create, the experimenter might pass it through
a coil; and so on. The truth criterion says that if any of these
tests fails, the body is not magnetic. Using the criterion
therefore implies that the experimenter has the freedom to choose
whether to perform a measurement and which measurement to perform
by selecting the appropriate instrument. In the quantum case the
necessity of free choice is even clearer, since in some instances
two different measurements cannot be combined into a single
measurement even in principle. The notion of free choice is, at
least in our elementary conceptions, foreign to the realm of
physics proper, so it is not absurd that some contemporary
theoretical developments ignore the truth criterion as formulated
here.

A related reason to question the truth criterion is that it
implicitly assumes a qualitative difference between past and
future: the past is fixed once and for all, while the future, as
the experimenter’s freedom shows, is open. Such a qualitative
distinction is alien to the basic laws of physics, so again it is
not surprising that some basic theories reject it and thus evade
the conditions imposed by the truth criterion.

On the other hand, denying that we cannot alter the past would
imply that whatever “really exists” is drastically different from
the network of physical phenomena, since there is a sense in
which we cannot change the phenomena of the past (consider the
well-known paradoxes associated with changing the past).
Likewise, denying that the choice of what measurement we will
perform in an hour is presently open seems to conflict with
a basic conception of empirical science. For these reasons the
truth criterion is, as a rule, implicitly accepted by most
scientists, whether realists or phenomenists. However—and this
brings us back to an earlier point—realists and phenomenists are
not bound by it in exactly the same way. The difference concerns
whether the criterion should include measurements that are
possible in principle (there is no law of physics forbidding them
and the sequence of operations can be precisely stated) but
cannot be done in practice because they are enormously
complicated (for example, they would produce a sudden,
appreciable increase in the entropy of the whole Earth). For
future reference, call the observables that such measurements
would measure “sensitive observables.” What is the logical
status, within the truth criterion, of such sensitive
observables?

For a conventional realist there is no doubt: the truth criterion
must treat sensitive observables on a par with practically
measurable ones, since excluding them would introduce
a human-centered element into the formulation of the laws
governing the world. As we will see, this obligation in turn
forces the conventional realist to dispute the validity of some
otherwise attractive quantum measurement theories. By contrast,
a phenomenist is not necessarily bound by such a strict
consistency requirement. For a phenomenist, all contingent
factual statements, including statements about measurement
outcomes, are elements in an ordering of human experience; she
may reasonably consider that some consistent way should exist to
exclude sensitive observables from those we must consider when
applying the truth criterion. In later chapters we will
investigate whether and how such an idea can be implemented.

What Is “Measurement”?

In ordinary language the word “measurement” has a straightforward
meaning. We take it that a table has some definite length and
width, and we perform measurements to find those values. This
conception is rooted in realism: the table is taken to have
a definite length even if nobody knows it; that is considered
both meaningful and true. Empirically, this idea is supported by
the fact that repeated measurements of the length yield the same
value within experimental error.

For very small systems this repeatability often fails. It can
happen, for example, that two successive measurements of the
velocity of a freely moving small ball give different outcomes.
Realism offers an explanation: any measurement requires
a measuring apparatus that must interact temporarily with the
system, and the first measurement may disturb the ball’s motion
and change its velocity. Even in this purely classical case,
a question arises about what the measurement outcome corresponds
to: the “initial” velocity just before the interaction with the
apparatus, or the “final” velocity just after?

In quantum mechanics the situation is even more subtle. There are
serious reasons to question the realist assumption that the
measured quantity has a definite (even if unknown) value at all
times. Nevertheless, one expects the measurement outcome to bear
some relation to reality, and most descriptions of quantum
mechanics—textbooks as well as articles—assume one of the two
possibilities above. Surprisingly, different authors take
different views even at this elementary level. Most recognize
that, as we will see in Chapters 3 and 4, conventional textbook
quantum mechanics naturally leads to the view that the
measurement outcome refers to the final value. Not everyone is
aware of this, and some critics reject conventional textbook
quantum mechanics for that reason. They consider it obvious, or
at least most natural, that the measurement outcome should refer
to the initial value (before the apparatus “disturbed” it), and
they construct quantum theories so as to fulfill that
requirement. This conception can be traced to Bohr’s
interpretation of quantum mechanics, in contrast with the
conventional textbook view inspired by John von Neumann. Recent
examples of approaches of this type are the theories of Griffiths
and of Omnès (see Chapter 11).

There is an escape from this dilemma: adopt a radically
operationalist standpoint and assert that the measurement outcome
is nothing more than what is read on the instrument’s scale.
Theorists often take this stance implicitly for its convenience,
and some feel it neatly separates physics from the vague problems
of philosophy. Indeed it does, but at the cost—often
unrecognized—of committing to a specific philosophical doctrine,
operationalism, and of leaving unexplored the acute problems that
quantum physics raises about the relationship between knowledge
and the world.

One of the main reasons questions about measurement are crucial
in quantum physics is that both Niels Bohr’s approach and the
conventional textbook approach use the notion of measurement to
state the “basic laws” of the theory, even if they do so
differently. Given this, it is not surprising that realist
physicists concerned with consistency have expressed
dissatisfaction with the state of quantum mechanics; one of John
Bell’s last articles was titled “Against Measurement.”

develop this topic in a didactic and pedestrian way, since to
understand the possible implications of this science for
admissible views of the world, no intermediate step can safely be
skipped.
