# 14. Open Realism

Two preliminary remarks about the chapter title are in order.

First, the term "realism" is more philosophical than scientific.
Although many would describe physics as "the science of physical
reality," words like 'realism' and 'reality' belong primarily to
philosophy. This chapter therefore necessarily blends
philosophical reflection with the scientific material developed
in the preceding chapters. It will explicitly combine elements of
both disciplines.

Second, to make the line of argument easier to follow, the
discussion is organized in four steps. 
    - `Section 14.1`: overall structure of the argument. 
    - `Sections 14.2–14.6`: explain the main features of each
      element in the chain of reasoning. 
    - `Sections 14.7–14.11` provide complementary details that
      aren't essential to the main argument but that philosophers
      like to request for strictness. 
    - Finally, `Section 14.12` draws conclusions.

### 14.1 Structure of Argumentation

The chapter answers three questions in succession:

1. What are the main riddles?  
    - We identify two: `weak objectivity` and `nonseparability`.
      These concepts do not arise in classical physics; their
      appearance in quantum theory is precisely what makes the
      predictive rules work extremely well while making
      interpretation in terms of an "external reality"
      problematic. This leads to the next question.

2. Do we need a reality?  
    - This is the philosophical core of the chapter. 
    - Our answer is yes, which leads to the final question.

3. Is this reality intelligible?  
    - To address this we assess the credibility of physicist's
      claims who assert they can describe how things "really are"
      without contradicting quantum predictions. 
    - Such claims typically rest either on alleged realistic
      explanations that recover classical features of macroscopic
      objects (including instrument pointers) or on ontological
      reformulations of QM. After examining both families of
      proposals, we conclude their credibility is low.

- From this we infer that reality is, most likely, either
  unknowable or veiled. Further analysis supports the conclusion
  that it is veiled.

### 14.2 Main Riddles, a Schematic Review

    - cf: $MY_NOTES/physics/metaphysics/strong-weak-objectivity.txt

Let's summarize a few established points.

`Weak objectivity`  
    - All statements in physics are objective in some sense, but
      there are two distinct types: strong and weak objectivity
      (see Section 1.6). 
    - Strongly objective statements are those that a conventional
      realist would interpret as describing features of an
      external reality that exist independently of our knowledge
      orexistence. 
    - Except for some statements in statistical thermodynamics,
      the basic assertions of classical physics are strongly
      objective. It was once reasonable to expect that future
      theories would also be expressible in strongly objective
      terms. That expectation failed. Some axioms of standard QM
      (see Chapter 3) refer essentially to measurements — to the
      notion of measurement irrespective of who performs it.
      Should such statements be called "subjective"? 
    - No, because they are valid for everyone. Thus there is
      a category of statements that are neither strongly
      objective NOR merely subjective: 
        - these are the weakly objective statements introduced in
          Chapter 1, where a more detailed analysis of the
          distinction was sketched. 

    - From a philosophical standpoint that analysis should be
      deepened; this is done in Section 14.7. That section also
      examines whether more elaborate forms of quantum theory or
      of measurement theory (e.g, the approaches in Chapters
      10–12) can restore strong objectivity; the answer given
      there is negative.

`Nonseparability`  
    - Classical physics was built on the powerful
notion of analysis — not only mathematical analysis but
conceptual and physical analysis: systems can be decomposed into
parts, and knowing the parts and the forces among them gives
complete knowledge of the whole. This was called "complete
divisibility by thought" in Section 8.1. Schrödinger was among
the first to point out that this idea clashes with quantum
mechanics.

In the elementary many-body formalism, the many-body wave
function is central. Even if one begins with a product of
approximately localized one-particle wave functions, Schrödinger
time evolution typically produces a wave function that is no
longer a product. Individual particles then cease to have
individual wave functions and cannot in general be assigned
definite spatial regions. Schrödinger called this entanglement.

Some tried to save divisibility by switching to density matrices
(state operators). While each subsystem may have its own reduced
density matrix, knowing all one-particle density matrices does
not determine the state of the composite system: reduced density
matrices omit information about inter-particle correlations,
which are experimentally observable. Thus divisibility by thought
is not restored.

This is qualitatively different from previous scientific
experience; as Weyl reportedly said, quantum mechanics is the
first holistic theory that really works.

The measurement problem — explaining what happens during
a measurement when instruments are treated as ordinary physical
systems — is essentially a special case of entanglement or
nondivisibility by thought (see Chapter 10). Ontologically
interpretable theories (Chapter 13) do not remove nondivisibility
by thought; Bell's theorems show they merely shift the puzzle
into violations of local causality. The convenient generic term
"nonseparability" covers the riddle in either form.

In short: weak objectivity and nonseparability are central
quantum features that clash with classical intuitions and force
us to revise them. These issues are true enigmas only if one
takes seriously the notion of an "external reality." They do not
impair the operational power of the theory (nondivisibility
cannot be used to send signals; see Section 8.2). Recognizing
these features naturally raises the question whether we need an
external, or better, an independent, reality.

### 14.3 Do We Need an "External" Reality?

Doubts about the need for an external reality trace back to
Berkeley's idealism and earlier to Descartes. From such
beginnings grew the idea that Being, things-in-themselves, and
Reality per se are redundant concepts: only human experience
provides meaningful reality. This "radical idealism" or "radical
phenomenism" underlies many modern philosophical doctrines
— phenomenalism, positivism, antirealism, internal realism
— which, despite differences of tone, share a basic affinity with
radical idealism.

A concise statement of the argument for radical idealism runs as
follows. Much of what we perceive depends on our sense organs, so
what we perceive immediately is a mental image, not the external
object itself. Therefore, we ought to attribute meaning only to
operationally defined entities; "existence" has meaning only in
reference to experience. Concepts such as space, time, and
causality are not structural elements of a reality-in-itself but
a priori modes of our sensibility and understanding (Kant).

Some physicists have flirted with such views — for example, the
young Einstein and Heisenberg — though many, like Einstein later
in life, moved to a more realist stance, and Heisenberg
introduced the notion of potentia. This evolution suggests the
radical-idealist, neo-Kantian approach is not fully satisfactory.
Several strong objections exist, some of which were sketched
earlier; fuller analysis appears in Sections 14.8–14.10. Briefly,
salient objections include:

1. Kantian or neo-Kantian insistence that physical laws must be
   framed in visualizable concepts may have obstructed the
   development of atomic theory. For instance, Schrödinger's
   insistence on wave-picture visualizability likely arose from
   such tendencies.

2. We sometimes construct elegant rational theories that
   experiments falsify. That experimental refusal to accept
   a theory suggests there is something more than merely "us"; it
   points to an external constraint.

3. Radical approaches place knowledge and experience logically
   prior to existence. But "knowledge" seems to presuppose
   someone or something that knows; making knowledge prior to any
   knower strains meaningfulness.

4. Intersubjective agreement across different observers is hard
   to explain without referring to something existing outside us.

These arguments require careful scrutiny; some analysis was given
in Chapter 1 and further development appears in Sections 14.8 and
14.9. Anticipating those sections, the overall outcome is broadly
favorable to the view that it is hard to dispense entirely with
a notion of reality prior to knowledge. We adopt the label open
realism for this stance.

Open realism: There is "something" — whether atoms, events, God,
Platonic ideas, or otherwise is left unspecified — whose
existence does not depend on the human mind.

Some will find open realism plausible; others will call it
a postulate. For the purposes of the subsequent argument, this is
not crucial. Importantly, unlike many conventional realists, we
do not postulate anything about the nature of what exists: we do
not assert it is knowable or unknowable. The question is left
open and is to be decided by factual and scientific
investigation.

To avoid prematurely suggesting properties of this "what exists,"
the neutral term "Independent Reality" will be used. This means
simply that something exists independently of us, not that we
cannot act upon it.

### 14.4 Is Independent Reality Atomizable?

By "atomism" here we mean the view that independent reality
consists of numerous simple, primitive entities. The question is
whether Being is dispersed into a multitude of such elements.

Elementary and popular presentations of physics might suggest an
affirmative answer. However, the material in the preceding
chapters points the other way: relativistic quantum physics, and
in particular quantum field theory, indicates that atomism in
this sense is basically wrong.

This may be surprising even to practitioners, since high-energy
physics is often couched in an implicitly atomistic language and
uses tools — notably Feynman diagrams — that evoke particles as
if they were entities existing on their own. But Feynman diagrams
are diagrammatic representations of calculation procedures that
yield probabilities for measurement outcomes in specified
circumstances. If we seek conceptual guidance about the
underlying ontology, we must look to the structure of quantum
field theory itself.

A philosophically striking feature of quantum field theory is
that it replaces the ill-defined notion of "creation" by the
well-defined notion of "state change." The theory treats fields
associated with particle types as primary and individualized
particles as secondary. If one searches for a mathematical object
corresponding to the basic "stuff," the candidate is the state
vector in Fock space. There is not a multitude of such
fundamental elements; conceptually, there is just one global
state vector. In that sense the theory is as far from atomism as
possible.

One might object that even if the basic formal object is unique,
it nevertheless materializes into many particles, each of which
could be called real. But quantum field theory inherits the basic
axioms of standard quantum mechanics, including (a) the
correspondence between operators and observables and (b) the
rules that associate eigenvalues with outcomes of possible or
actual measurements rather than with preexisting attributes of
systems (see Rules 5 and 6 in Chapter 3). In quantum field
theory, the "number of particles of a given type in a given
state" is an observable represented by an operator constructed
from field operators. Consistency then requires treating particle
number, like other observables, as the possible outcome of
a measurement, not as an intrinsic attribute of some underlying
reality. In other words, the contingent existence of a particle
is not fundamentally different from a dynamical property — it is
an eigenvalue of an observable, akin to the position of a quantum
particle that, before measurement, is represented by an extended
wave function. Thus there is a significant mismatch between the
atomism defined above and conventional quantum field theory.

Neither the pictorial "zigzag" account of pair
creation-annihilation nor the path-integral technique overturns
this conclusion. Both are computational tools internal to quantum
field theory that gain efficiency by using evocative,
pseudo-realist imagery; these images should not be taken
literally. For example, in the path-integral method we sum
probability amplitudes, not probabilities, and whether we sum
amplitudes or probabilities depends on whether we can, by
measurement, obtain "which-path" information — a criterion that
is typically only weakly objective.

### 14.5 Is Independent Reality Knowable?

This question should be addressed in light of physics. Can we
reasonably hope to construct a theory that is both ontologically
interpretable and scientifically convincing? A look at current
proposals suggests that the answer is no.

1. Two approaches are sometimes misread as ontological. One is
   a flawed interpretation of Bohr that identifies his view with
   "macro-objectivism," the idea that macroscopic objects obey
   classical physics and thus admit a straightforward ontological
   interpretation. This reading fails because Bohr, in his
   discussions (for example, involving movable diaphragms),
   places parts of an experimental setup on the instrument side
   or on the object side according to their role in the
   experiment, not according to their intrinsic physical
   complexity. Bohr introduced a logical distinction between
   instruments and quantum objects; that distinction is not one
   of mere scale or complexity. As Bohr himself emphasized, his
   description has "a perfectly objective character in the sense
   that no explicit reference is made to any individual
   observer." That captures what we call weak objectivity: Bohr
   aimed at weak, not strong, objectivity. Thus Bohr's approach
   does not reconcile quantum mechanics with realism. More formal
   reconstructions of Bohr's ideas, such as Omnès's, share this
   feature.

The other approach often misinterpreted as ontological is the
environment-induced decoherence program of Joos, Zeh, and others
(Chapter 12). Macroscopic bodies have extremely dense spectra and
cannot be treated as isolated; environment degrees of freedom
cause decoherence. While the relevant environment variables and
correlations that would contradict attributing definite classical
properties to macroscopic bodies are practically unmeasurable, in
principle they can be measured — correlations between outcomes of
distant measurements could, in principle, be recorded and
compared later. That reasoning (via the Schrödinger-cat argument;
see Section 10.6) rules out an ontological interpretation in
which objects always possess well-defined dynamical properties.
Joos's own summary — "the appearance of a classical world"
— captures well the epistemological status of the theory.

2. Some genuinely ontological but unconventional approaches
   exist: pilot-wave theory, hidden-variable theories (e.g., de
   Broglie–Bohm and others), and spontaneous-reduction theories
   (GRW, CSL). These are important because they restore strong
   objectivity. But that strength is also a liability: in these
   theories the counterpart of nondivisibility by thought is
   nonlocality (Bell's theorems). Although neither
   nondivisibility nor nonlocality allows superluminal signaling
   (see Section 8.2), nonlocality is more troublesome in
   ontological theories than in weakly objective formulations. If
   a theory posits strong objectivity, then for consistency one
   should also treat relativity as a strongly objective theory;
   that would require that no influence of any kind propagate
   faster than light, a requirement difficult to reconcile with
   nonlocality (see the remark near the end of Section 16.1).
   Thus the very feature that makes these ontological theories
   attractive is also the source of their main weakness.

This does not amount to declaring these theories falsified, but
it lowers the confidence one is inclined to place in them. There
are several competing such theories, experimental discrimination
among them appears very difficult, and extending them to quantum
field theory (with creation and annihilation) forces major
revisions of their ontological assignments because particles can
no longer be the basic stuff. The most reasonable conjecture is
that constructing a scientifically well-established ontological
theory is probably out of reach and that physics should focus on
describing and synthesizing phenomena.

### 14.6 Is Independent Reality in Space and Time?

Whether independent reality is embedded in space and time (or
space-time) is a momentous question: the answer shapes our entire
apprehension of the world and our place in it. Unsurprisingly, it
is also difficult. Saying that physics describes phenomena in
a more or less Kantian sense does not by itself answer whether
independent reality occupies ordinary space and time; and, as
noted in Section 1.1, there are scientific reasons to reject
Kantianism in full.

One clear point is that current science — especially special and
general relativity — does not incline us to think that
independent reality lies in ordinary flat space-time. Still,
relativity alone cannot settle deeper questions such as whether
independent reality resides in a distorted space-time or whether
it evolves in some cosmic time. There are impressive arguments
both for and against such possibilities.

---[chunk-boundary]---

Unquestionably, contemporary astrophysics proceeds on the tacit
assumption that the answer is "yes": that stars, galaxies, and
similar objects exist independently of our knowledge of them and
are localized in space and time roughly as we observe them.
People often point out that denying this amounts to claiming we
create stars and galaxies, and deem this absurd because, for
example, the Earth existed before humans did. As noted in Section
1.4, idealists have a ready reply to this objection, but that
reply relies on central tenets of idealism; accepting it
consistently requires embracing some of idealism's more
unsettling features, which makes many of us hesitate. On the
other hand, phenomena such as nonseparability and the weak
objectivity characteristic of the most productive versions of
quantum theory provide strong arguments against embedding reality
straightforwardly in space-time or cosmic time. Those arguments
are powerful enough to make proponents of embedding reconsider
their position. Here I will explore whether one can counter the
argument for embedding while remaining within open realism, that
is, without adopting radical idealism.

To begin, let us run a simple "first-year philosophy" comparison
between two concepts: the "direction of the Moon in the sky" and
the "taste of a fruit." Realists typically see a qualitative
difference. Our perception of the Moon's direction is taken to
report directly an objective fact: the Moon really is in that
direction. By contrast, the taste of a fruit is seen as the
result of a complex interaction of objective external facts
(which molecules are present) with subjective or intersubjective
facts about our sensory apparatus and brain. But it is
theoretically conceivable that the difference is more apparent
than real: that both perceptions arise from complex processing by
our sensory system and brain of "remote" factual elements
belonging to an independent reality very different from what
appears to us. Such a hypothesis would be arbitrary—and, to many
scientists, not worth considering—if a straightforward, strongly
objective interpretation of contemporary physics were available.
The preceding chapters have shown that no such straightforward
interpretation is available. Thus it is conceivable that the
"direction in which the Moon lies" is related to the underlying
reality no more directly than the taste of a fruit is, and so is
in just as much a product of our perceptual and conceptual
apparatus. Indeed, in ontologically interpretable theories such
as Bohm's [7], the explicit order—the order we observe in
astronomy, daily life, and in laboratories—can be thoroughly
different from the underlying implicate order, rather than
a faithful reflection of it.

Pursuing how open realism might be reconciled with the idea that
independent reality is not embedded in space and time, recall the
two roles that mathematical symbols (p, E, B, r, etc.) play in
classical physics, as discussed in Section 10.8. One role (Role
a) is to write down the general laws—Maxwell's equations, for
example—that are taken as candidates for describing the world's
general structure. The other role (Role b) is to denote the
particular values those quantities have in a given instance. In
quantum mechanics, Heisenberg-picture operators perform Role
a only; Role b is not played by any algorithm that carries
a strongly objective meaning. As shown earlier, if we take
quantum mechanics as adequately and fully describing the
structure of independent reality (thus moving away from idealism
toward realism), we must conceive independent reality as a highly
entangled whole. From that viewpoint it is impossible to carve
this whole into parts that each occupy definite places.
Consequently, as with the taste of fruit, it is we who create—or
collectively imagine—the localizations of objects. Strictly
speaking this argument applies directly to space, not to time.
But given the relativistic partial equivalence of space and time,
the fact that we create space suggests there is also, in some
sense, a role we play in creating time. There are known
mathematical difficulties in formulating quantum mechanics (even
in the Heisenberg picture) in a fully general,
interaction-including, manifestly covariant way; given those
difficulties, it is not surprising that the foregoing argument
does not directly demonstrate that we "create" time as well as
space, and this absence of a direct demonstration is not good
evidence that we do not.

At this stage we already have the material needed to reach
a reasonable overall conclusion about the concept of reality;
that conclusion is stated in Section 14.12. Readers pressed for
time may go directly to it. The following sections (Sections
14.7–14.11) are complementary material intended for readers who
want to pursue points they consider delicate in more detail.

### 14.7 Complements on Weak Objectivity

As mentioned earlier, weak objectivity (intersubjectivity)
deserves a more detailed philosophical analysis than has been
given so far. As explained in Chapter 1, one delicate point is
that weak objectivity can be a property either of statements or
of concepts. A statement is weakly objective if it is true for
everyone but essentially refers to what humans actually do, can
do, or observe. Analogously, one might call a concept "weakly
objective" if its definition explicitly refers to some human
procedure. However, as hinted in Section 1.6, that definition is
too coarse: if taken at face value it would render virtually
every scientific concept weakly objective, since explaining any
concept to someone who lacks it typically involves reference to
human observations. Conventional realism offers a philosophical
standpoint under which strongly objective concepts can be defined
more straightforwardly. Following Dummett, realism can be
characterized as the belief that there are statements that
possess an objective truth value independently of our ways of
knowing them: they are true or false in virtue of a reality
existing independently of us [8]. Within any form of realism
there are, by assumption, at least some statements (what we call
strongly objective statements) of the kind Dummett specifies. The
terms used in those statements are, by definition, strongly
objective concepts. Here we begin from realism about material
objects (to be adapted later to modern physics). In that view,
even though the position of a macroscopic object must ultimately
be explained by mentioning observations, it is normally taken to
refer to a state of affairs that does not depend on our
possession of evidence and is thus strongly objective.

Within that form of realism, one can give a more restrictive and
useful definition of weakly objective concepts, which lets us
sharply distinguish strongly objective from weakly objective
concepts, and strongly objective from weakly objective
statements. Strongly objective statements are those that, within
Dummett-type realism, would be considered true or false in virtue
of a reality existing independently of us, and so possess
objective truth values regardless of our means of knowing them.
Strongly objective concepts are simply the entities or attributes
the terms in those statements denote. Weakly objective statements
remain those that explicitly refer to human actions or
observations and are true for everybody. But some theories
present a third category of statements: they look like the
strongly objective statements of classical physics because they
appear to describe objects as we see them (they make no explicit
reference to actions or observations), yet internal consistency
of the theory prevents us from interpreting them as having
strongly objective truth. For example, they may conflict with
predictions the theory makes about "conceivable but practically
unfeasible" experiments (see Section 10.6). In such a theory we
may choose to call weakly objective (that is, merely weakly
objective) the concepts to which this third category of
statements refers. A typical illustration is the position of
a pointer after a measurement has been performed and before
anyone has looked (see Eq. (10.5)). This definition will need
refinement (see Chapter 15), but it already makes clear that
within certain theories that aim at realism, calling a concept
"only weakly objective" is a meaningful, non-tautological claim.

A semantic remark is appropriate here to prevent confusion. The
class of weakly objective concepts, as just defined, is
comparatively small because these concepts refer specifically to
objects. Many other concepts—such as mathematical functions or
the notion of truth—are objective in the ordinary sense but are
neither "strongly" nor "weakly" objective in the restricted
senses adopted here.

We may call a theory weakly objective if its basic statements or
axioms—the statements from which the theory deductively
follows—involve at least some weakly objective statements or
concepts. Conversely, a theory is strongly objective if all its
basic laws are formulated exclusively in terms of strongly
objective statements and concepts. Given a strongly objective
theory, one is not forced to adopt a realist interpretation that
sees the theory as describing objects "as they really are"; there
are philosophical reasons for alternative readings. The strongly
objective character of a theory simply means that, taken on its
own terms, its basic statements can consistently be understood as
referring to an external reality.

Aside from the special case of thermodynamics, the theories that
made up classical physics were strongly objective by this
definition. Relativity theory is likewise strongly objective,
despite appearances to the contrary. Rosenfeld [9] argued
correctly that although relativity makes reference to observers
and their reference frames, this does not make the theory
incompatible with realism: the formal invariance of the laws
under changes of reference frame bestows objectivity. A related
argument, more in line with Dummett's realism, is that in
relativity space-time and events can consistently be treated as
elements of a reality that exists independently of us. Since
events are, in principle, knowable within the theory, the theory
can consistently be interpreted as describing "reality as it is";
the basic statements concern events and space-time and are
strongly objective.

From his argument Rosenfeld inferred that, regarding the use of
"observer" notions, there is no essential difference between
(standard textbook or Copenhagen-like) quantum mechanics and
special relativity, and so he judged quantum mechanics to be as
strongly objective as relativity. That conclusion cannot be
sustained once the second argument above is taken into account:
unlike in relativity, in standard quantum mechanics no
concept—neither particle, nor wave, nor state operator—can be
consistently interpreted as referring to contingent elements of
a reality that exist independently of us. In relativity one can,
if desired, unfold the theory in terms of many point events
inhabiting a preexisting space-time (with the connection to
experience stated later). In standard quantum mechanics such
a procedure is not available, as the earlier chapters have shown.
It is therefore not surprising that foundational texts such as
von Neumann's [10] and Dirac's [11] present basic quantum axioms
in terms of measurement outcomes. As John Bell [12] emphasized,
Dirac's textbook contains axiomatic statements like "any result
of a measurement of a real dynamical variable is one of its
eigenvalues," "if the measurement of the observable is made
a large number of times the average of all the results obtained
will be ...," and "a measurement causes the system to jump into
an eigenstate of the measured dynamical variable." These axioms
show that the theory involves fundamentally the notion of
measurement. As Bell put it, "it would seem that the theory is
exclusively concerned about 'results of measurements' and has
nothing to say about anything else."

That situation is unsurprising to phenomenalists, but it is
deeply unsatisfying for realists, who assume an intelligible
reality that physics describes. This motivates questions like
Bell's: What exactly qualifies some physical systems to play the
role of "measurer"? Was the wave function of the world waiting to
collapse for billions of years until a single-celled organism
appeared, or did it have to wait longer for some better qualified
system with a Ph.D.? It is natural that many theorists tried to
clarify this question and to rescue quantum physics from what
they saw as a serious conceptual mess.

Most of those theorists followed a "direct" approach: they tried
to define physically—and to incorporate into the formalism—the
disputed notion of "measurement," while remaining within the
Hilbert-space framework of quantum mechanics. Their program can
be understood as an attempt to reconcile Hilbert-space quantum
theory with a kind of macro-objectivism where some macroscopic
statements could consistently be treated as strongly objective,
and thus where the dynamical quantities involved in those
statements would be strongly objective notions. The program
centered on the interaction of macroscopic systems with their
environment and on decoherence is the most prominent example and
can serve as a paradigm. Decoherence accounts remarkably well for
the appearance of a classical world, and many rival
classical-limit or measurement theories do so to a large extent
as well. However, if one insists on Dummett-style realism,
material-object statements must be taken to reveal attributes as
they really are; for instance, the observation of a pointer
position should reveal the pointer's ontological position
independently of human observational and technical limitations.
That condition is not met (see Section 14.10), and therefore the
corresponding statements cannot be interpreted as referring to
strongly objective concepts.

This line of physical work has affinities with a philosophical
effort, pursued independently of quantum mechanics, to find
a middle path between conventional realism and the radical
phenomenalism discussed in Chapter 1. Dummett [8], for example,
suggested that the phenomenalists' reduction of all physical
statements to sense-data might be avoided—allowing a kind of
moderate realism—if we reject the law of excluded middle for
material-object statements. More precisely, Dummett proposed that
an unasserted material-object statement (a statement about
a physical property, for example) reduces to a subjunctive
conditional still couched in material-object language. Such
a conditional should be judged true only on the basis of
observations and generalizations from them; in Dummett's sketch
it is true only when there are actual observations that serve as
its grounds. If no such observations exist, the statement is
neither true nor false; in that case we should regard it as
meaningless.

There is some resemblance between Dummett's idea and Bohr's main
guideline that the role of instruments is essential for defining
a phenomenon. But the detailed discussion of Bohr's views in
Sections 11.1–11.3 shows that Bohr cannot be classified as
a realist in Dummett's sense; appealing to Bohr will not turn
quantum physics into a strongly objective theory.

More generally, the investigations reported in this book lead to
the following picture. Recent advances in environmental theories,
the theory of complex systems, and computational procedures using
quasi-classical operators have greatly clarified why so many
phenomena look classical in a presumably quantum universe. Yet
none of these developments meets the demanding conditions that
conventional realism requires. None yields a truly strongly
objective theory. That suggests that anyone who insists on strict
realism must be prepared to abandon strict adherence to standard
quantum theory and turn instead to alternatives such as the
nonlocal hidden-variable theories or nonlinear modifications of
quantum mechanics discussed in Chapter 13.

### 14.8 Complements on "Do We Need a Reality?"; The Notion of
Explanation

Section 14.3 presented some arguments in favor of open realism;
I will now supplement those with further considerations.

The notion of explanation has long been invoked by philosophers
who question realism. Even elementary pieces of physical
knowledge can bolster that questioning. At first glance,
explanation seems tightly linked to positing elements of reality
that exist "out there." But, as recalled earlier, explaining
a phenomenon is not always equivalent to discovering "how things
really are." The history of physics shows that this can be an
overambitious goal: explaining Halley's Comet by invoking a real
gravitational force later had to be revised when general
relativity showed that force is not a fundamental entity. Two
more modest definitions of "explanation" are therefore useful.

Definition (a): "To explain" means "to reduce to what is already
known." Often that is possible and feels like a genuine
explanation. But this approach can be misleading. For instance,
explaining the sensation of heat by picturing energetic
collisions of classical molecules with our skin reduces the
phenomenon to a familiar mechanism (like tennis balls hitting
a net) and seems convincing. Yet hydrogen molecules are quantum
objects, and quantum theory does not ascribe them definite
trajectories; thus that classical picture cannot be taken
literally. Present-day statistical mechanics explains thermal
phenomena using concepts such as Gibbsian ensembles—abstractions
that resist literal, classical descriptions.

Hidden-variable theories can also be seen, aside from their
realist motivation, as attempts to reduce the unknown to the
known: to return to familiar notions of point particles, forces,
positions, velocities, and so on. But the discoveries of
nonlocality and contextuality show that even hidden-variable
theories do not accomplish this reduction in any simple,
classical way. That motivates a fallback to a still more modest
notion of explanation.

Definition (b): "To explain" means to connect many different
facts by showing they fall under the same law. On this view,
explaining the return of Halley's Comet means relating it to the
general law of gravitation, not by claiming the law constitutes
the essence of nature, but by showing it unifies various
phenomena.

The basic quantum facts discussed throughout this book support
these weaker notions of explanation—especially (b)—more securely
than they support the stronger, realist-centered notions. This
strengthens doubts about realism as defined by Dummett.

Why Not Radical Phenomenism?

One might be tempted to take the other extreme, abandon the idea
of an independent reality altogether, and adopt radical idealism
or phenomenalism (sometimes called radical phenomenism).
Schrödinger, for example, criticized Kant's notion of
things-in-themselves, arguing that we cannot meaningfully
distinguish between the realm of our perceptions and any putative
realm of causes: "The story is occurring once and not twice. The
duplication is an allegory." Even if Schrödinger had some grounds
for objecting to Kant's language, the situation we face now is
different because nonseparability shows that any attempt to
describe independent reality as a multiplicity of localized items
is bound to fail. Phenomena appear multiple and localized, but
independent reality resists such a description; therefore it is
impossible to regard phenomena as a mere duplication of
independent reality, or vice versa. Still, many reasons make it
difficult—especially for physicists—to give up realism entirely.
Let us make explicit some of them.

First, most scientists would agree with Pierre Duhem's
observation: however much a physicist may try to believe that
theories only provide condensed and classified representations of
experimental laws and do not grasp the Real, he cannot help
having faith—in an intuition that reason does not justify—that
there is a real order underlying the laws, and that his theories
are increasingly clearer and more accurate pictures of that
order.

Duhem is right to acknowledge that there are no logically
conclusive proofs of realism in Dummett's strong sense.
Nevertheless, that does not imply there are no arguments at all
for any form of realism. There are several arguments—some
sketched in Section 14.3—that, while not decisive, have
significant plausibility. Most are not the traditional arguments
for realism (many of those are shaky in the light of modern
quantum physics); rather, they are philosophical and point toward
a different sort of reality than classical versions. They are
worth considering.

1. If one accepts the Kantian claim that our concepts, including
   scientific ones, are a priori forms of our sensibility, one is
   led to expect that the concepts suitable for any theory will
   be a subset of our natural concepts. That implies our basic
   scientific concepts should be highly "visualizable"
   (Anschaulichkeit). Kant's own concepts of Euclidean space and
   universal time exhibit this feature. Excessive confidence in
   Kantian or neo-Kantian ideas may have hindered early
   twentieth-century physicists attempting to build a theory of
   atomic processes. In practice we now distinguish concepts used
   to describe experience—which, as Bohr insisted, must be
   expressible in ordinary language and visualizable—from the
   abstract concepts used to formalize theories. If Kant's
   account of the origin of all our concepts is not valid,
   a question remains: where do the concepts we use in theory
   come from? Why do we use certain concepts rather than others?

---[chunk-boundary]---

Clearly, the (obvious) answer “It is because they work” is not
fully satisfactory, because it immediately invites the further
question already raised above: “Why do these concepts work while
others do not?” Kant’s scheme avoided such a question by fiat,
but dismissing it simply by decree (“no question beginning with
‘why’ shall be considered”) is an inadequate response. A slightly
better reply points out that many of the successful concepts in
physics are borrowed from mathematics, which is at least
a structure of our understanding if not of our sensibility. But
then one must still ask: why precisely those mathematical
structures—curved spaces, tensors, Heisenberg-projectors, and so
on—instead of the many other constructs available in mathematics?
After reflection, the view that scientific descriptions owe
a great deal to the mind’s structures but not everything to them
appears hard to avoid.

2. We also noted the striking argument that, in physics, we
   sometimes construct highly rational theories that experiment
   nevertheless falsifies. Experiment cannot falsify the rules of
   a game of chess, or of any other purely invented game, because
   those rules are just created by us. In that case there is
   nothing “external” that can say “no.” But in physics there are
   often things that do say “no” — experiments that contradict
   our theories. How could this “something” still be nothing but
   us? To maintain that it is would require an extreme
   intellectual contortion. As Bonsack put it: “The world
   constructed by the subject lacks an important dimension, that
   which gives a sense to error. Somehow it is its own measure.”

3. Another previously noted argument is more metaphysical.
   Radical phenomenism starts from the claim that we know only
   phenomena and follows the prudent maxim that we should speak
   only of what we can possibly know (Wittgenstein’s “Whereof we
   cannot speak, thereof we must be silent”). Radical phenomenism
   takes this further and asserts that only phenomena have
   meaning. This may seem reasonable at first glance, but it
   places the concepts of knowledge and experience logically
   prior to existence, a priority that is questionable. It seems
   impossible to give any noncircular meaning to “knowledge”
   without implicitly assuming the existence of someone or
   something that knows. Thus the logical ordering implicit in
   radical phenomenism does not meet ordinary requirements for
   meaningfulness.

4. A fourth point, hinted at in Chapter 1, concerns the discovery
   that the universe has a finite age — a fact unknown to
   Berkeley, Kant, Poincaré and most classical advocates of
   phenomenism. This discovery strengthens the evolutionary
   message: in a noneternal, hence essentially evolutionary,
   universe more complex systems gradually appeared (atoms,
   molecules, then life and finally animal and human minds). Mind
   itself developed gradually, forming good concepts over time;
   concepts that were thus formed hardly seem likely to be the
   ultimate building blocks of the “outside world.” This argument
   is partly circular — for the phenomenist, time is not an arena
   for evolution — so it is not decisive, but it does reduce the
   a priori plausibility of radical phenomenism.

5. Finally, these arguments must be supplemented by recalling
   a fact discussed in Section 10.8 about the Heisenberg picture.
   Mathematical symbols used in physics — p, E, B, r, and so on
   — play two distinct roles that classical physics tends to
   conflate: one role is to denote the contingent value of
   a dynamical quantity for a particular system; the other is to
   serve in writing down the general laws of physics (Maxwell’s
   equations, the Lagrangian or Hamiltonian of a field theory,
   etc.). If these two roles are identified, it becomes almost
   inevitable to adopt a form of realism that treats basic
   theoretical concepts (for instance, the electric field at each
   space-time point) as describing elements of a reality that
   exist independently of us. This “mathematical realism” is
   close to material-object realism and conflicts with what
   standard textbook quantum mechanics implies.

However, if we distinguish the two roles, it becomes possible
— without contradicting quantum mechanics, even in its standard
weakly objective form — to hold that the general laws reflect, in
some way, structures of a reality that exists independently of
us. This distinction helps explain why general laws such as
Maxwell’s equations have remained so effective despite radical
changes in the underlying concepts. This modern version of
Poincaré’s structural realism can be viewed as accounting for the
regularities of observed phenomena rather than merely recording
them, which is an advantage.

Remark: In quantum field theory, the Heisenberg field operators
φ(x,t) are defined as functions of space-time. For reasons
explained in Section 14.6, this does not imply that independent
reality is literally embedded in space-time. Nevertheless, those
Heisenberg operators can consistently be given the role (call it
“Role a”) of representing structures that enter the Lagrangians
and Hamiltonians and that reflect general features of an
independent reality. If one accepts that reasonable assumption,
then the fact that these operators depend on space-time
coordinates suggests that space-time is, in some sense, more than
a pure a priori form of our sensibility (as some contemporary
followers of Kant might hold). Even if space-time is
a construction rather than a preexisting arena, that construction
seems to capture some truth about independent reality.

To sum up: there are good reasons not to accept radical
phenomenism, i.e., for answering “yes” to the question “Do we
need a reality?” Overall, the detailed considerations of this
section support the standpoint of open realism, as stated at the
end of Section 14.3.

### 14.9 Intersubjective Agreement Revisited

Our most common and recurring human experience is intersubjective
agreement about contingent facts. Together with the apparent
permanence of many objects and phenomena, this shared agreement
underlies our strong intuitive belief in a “real world out
there.” Prima facie, intersubjective agreement is a powerful
argument for some form of realism. Yet, as with the other
arguments reviewed above, this one deserves closer scrutiny; the
issue is delicate.

The naive, commonsense explanation of intersubjective agreement
about contingent facts is realist: if we both agree that an
object is red or in a particular place, it must really be red or
at that place. As noted in Section 1.4, idealists reject that
explanation as metaphysical but often do not offer an
alternative. In quantum contexts, explanations of the form “we
both observed the electron in the upper beam because it really
was there before we observed it” are typically flawed. So the
question deserves further study.

Agreement with Respect to Secondary Qualities

Start with the least controversial case: secondary qualities such
as color. Historically, investigators noticed early on that
whatever “reality” underlies intersubjective agreement about
color is comparatively fragile. It is doubtful that when Peter
and Paul both call an object “red” they have identical sense
impressions; the phrase “the same” is problematic here. What is
clear is that, in the vast majority of cases, Peter says “yes, it
is red.” Given this, the simple realistic explanation — that the
object simply has the property “red” and we observe it “as it
really is” — now seems too naive.

Still, that observation did not deter people from seeking
explanations. A schematic theory of vision combined with physical
laws can explain the agreement: for color, the laws of classical
electromagnetism provide a straightforward account of why
different observers have correlated color impressions.

At this point two philosophical options remain. Option O1 is to
stop here: the fact that well-established physical laws reliably
predict the correlation between Peter’s and Paul’s sensations
constitutes the sought explanation (this matches definition (b)
of “explanation” in Section 14.8). Option O2 is for those who
want more: they invoke realism and claim that the general
physical laws (classical electromagnetism in the color example)
describe primary qualities (field strengths, etc.), and the
explanation is that these fields have definite values at definite
times. Within O2, the secondary-quality problem reduces to the
question about primary qualities.

Primary Qualities

If we accept option O1, quantum mechanics entirely solves the
intersubjective agreement problem for primary qualities: it
explains why different observers agree about primary physical
properties. Quantum theory offers several equivalent ways to
state this, all based on the fact that only measurements (in
a broad sense) yield sense-data. One way invokes wave-packet
collapse: the first person performing a position measurement
obtains an outcome and thereby reduces the wave packet to the
corresponding eigenstate; anyone immediately measuring the same
quantity then works with the reduced state and must obtain the
same outcome. This account needs refinement to accommodate
relativity, and Section 10.12 sketches how that can be done.

A second way avoids explicit collapse by describing the quantum
states of the two measuring instruments. After both measurements,
the total quantum state of system plus the two instruments is
generally a superposition of eigenstates of the measured
quantity; but in each component of that superposition the two
instruments occupy correlated pointer eigenstates. Under these
conditions the probability is zero that, when each observer reads
his instrument (measures its pointer coordinate), they will
obtain different outcomes. A variant is Everett’s “relative
state” approach, where even observers’ consciousnesses are
included in the overall quantum state; again, there is zero
probability that the states of consciousness of the two observers
will disagree.

Remark 1: Within option O1 (or equivalently under definition (b)
of “explanation” in Section 14.8), the question whether animals
participate in our intersubjective agreement receives an
unambiguous positive answer. We do not have to accept Everett in
full; as soon as we attribute to animals the ability to observe
(for example, to become aware of a pointer position) and assign
ordinary quantum probabilities to such events, quantum rules
predict the same correlations between their sense-data and ours.
Dogs, cats, and so on therefore see and inhabit the same
empirical reality as we do.

Remark 2: One reason the idea of a hidden-variable “position”
seems attractive is that for macroscopic objects we habitually
explain intersubjective agreement by the commonsense realist
claim “we both see the object at the same place because it is at
that place” (option O2), and we tend to extend that explanation
to microobjects. For microobjects, however, this realist
extension can be a delusion. For example, if two observers
measure the position of an electron one immediately after the
other with independent apparatus, they will both obtain the same
outcome, and standard quantum rules account for this even though
the electron had no definite position beforehand in the standard
interpretation. Thus, in pilot-wave theory the fact that the
electron has a definite position at all times adds nothing
empirically new; it can be seen as redundant. For such hidden
variables to be regarded as the genuine explanation of
intersubjective agreement would require a wholesale
reinterpretation of physics, treating hidden variables as the
sole direct objects of our knowledge — a reinterpretation that
generally lacks sufficient justification.

The foregoing shows that, if one adopts option O1 (“explanation”
= showing that different facts follow the same general law),
quantum mechanics provides a satisfactory account of
intersubjective agreement. And since quantum mechanics is usually
taken as universal, this conclusion extends to agreement about
any contingent material fact. This undermines the naive intuition
that intersubjective agreement by itself demonstrates the
existence or meaningfulness of an independent external reality.
But the issue is not fully settled by this observation. One can
ask: what is the nature of the quantum rules themselves? They are
not mere rules of an invented game, because unlike game rules
they are not arbitrarily chosen by humans. A skeptic can
therefore respond: “If intersubjective agreement comes from the
quantum rules, where do those rules come from?” This challenges
the acceptability of option O1. The remarks on the Heisenberg
picture and the dual role of symbols such as r, v, E, B in
Sections 10.8, 14.6, and 14.8 suggest that causality between
phenomena might need to be supplemented by a notion of causality
from the general theoretical structures to phenomena. For that
notion to make sense, one seems obliged to attribute some kind of
reality to those general structures.

### 14.10 Complements Concerning Limited Knowability and Realism

Section 14.5 listed the main reasons why building an
“ontological” physical theory that describes independent reality
“as it really is” appears hopeless. Those critical arguments are
developed here in more detail.

The Environment Theory and the Conventional Measurement Theories

The environment theory aims to be more than purely
phenomenalistic because, unlike Bohr’s approach, it assigns
meaning to the quantum state of an instrument independently of
whether the instrument is actually used. It rests on the
important observation (Zeh, Baumann) that macroscopic systems
— even rather small ones such as dust specks — have such dense
energy spectra that they cannot be treated as truly isolated from
their environments.

There are several reasons why this elegant theory, which nicely
accounts for classical appearances, cannot be interpreted as
reconciling quantum mechanics with conventional realism. We
analyze three such reasons, two of which were only sketched in
Section 14.5.

1. The first concerns the description of measurement within this
   theory. As noted in Section 10.6 and again in Sections 14.2
   and 14.5, consider a Gibbsian ensemble of systems,
   instruments, and environments. Let a measurementlike
   interaction occur in which the measured system S initially is
   in a superposition of, say, two eigenstates of the observable
   of interest. Let to be a time shortly after this interaction
   and after the apparatus–environment interaction has produced
   the decoherence effects analyzed by the theory. There then
   exist operators — the so-called “sensitive observables”
   introduced in Section 1.6 — whose predictions for outcomes of
   measurements performed at time t ≥ to are incompatible with
   the assumption that instrument pointers are in definite
   macrostates. It is true that these sensitive observables
   involve the environment in such a way that they are
   practically unmeasurable, but “in practice” refers to human
   limitations and cannot be used as a premise in any statement
   that claims strong objectivity.

To make this more concrete, follow Zurek’s simplified model
representing the combined S + A system by a single particle
moving on the x axis. If S begins in the superposition that led
the apparatus to correlate with two alternative outcomes, the
S + A state shortly after their interaction can be modeled by
a coherent superposition ψ(x) = 2^{-1/2}(ψ1(x) + ψ2(x)) of two
well-peaked wave packets ψ1 and ψ2. For simplicity take ψ1(x)
peaked at x = −a and ψ2(x) peaked at x = +a; the corresponding
density operator at time to has four peaks in the x, x′ plane:
two on the main diagonal at x = x′ = ±a and two off-diagonal at
(x = −a, x′ = +a) and (x = +a, x′ = −a). From to on, interaction
with the environment — modeled by an interaction qualitatively
similar to that in Eq. (10.30) — and tracing out the environment
multiplies the initial density operator by a factor that rapidly
suppresses the off-diagonal terms. To the extent that the
off-diagonal peaks can be treated as negligible, the resulting
reduced density operator is identical to one describing a proper
mixture of ψ1 and ψ2. If one reads Zurek literally, one might
conclude that an ensemble of such representative particles can be
regarded, approximately, as composed in equal proportions of
particles in state ψ1 and particles in state ψ2.

But this literal reading is not warranted. Nothing in general
physics rules out observing the environment, so nothing in the
formalism makes it meaningless or “unphysical” to consider the
sensitive observables. Section 10.6 shows that, if the
environment observables in question were accessible, there would
be a tangible discrepancy between quantum predictions and the
claim that the instrument pointer is in one definite macrostate
or the other. The only available reason to dismiss such
observables is that they cannot be measured in practice (perhaps
because the universe’s finite lifetime will prevent humanity from
doing so). While that is a good practical reason for treating the
theory as accounting extremely well for phenomena, it cannot
justify a claim of strong objectivity.

Remark 3: Even if we could ignore the sensitive observables
issue, the proposition Q — “under the stated conditions, the
combined system Σ is either in state ψ1 or in ψ2 (i.e., the
pointer has one or the other definite value)” — is not
tautological. As remarked in Section 4.3, a condition for the
statement “A has value a1 on Σ” to be true is that we can imagine
somebody who would know beforehand that a measurement of A on
Σ would yield outcome a1. Symbolically, this can be written
D > P, where D stands for “an instrument suitable for measuring
A is made to interact with Σ,” P stands for “outcome a1 is
registered,” and > denotes strict implication. If A has eigenkets
ψ1 and ψ2 with eigenvalues a1 and a2, then Q has the form Q = (D > P) ∨ (D > ¬P), 
where ¬P is the orthogonal complement “outcome a2 is registered,” 
and ∨ is “or.” While (P ∨ ¬P) is a tautology, Q is not; 
this is a standard theorem in modal logic.

If the sensitive observables were measurable, their existence
would produce gross differences between quantum predictions and
the localized-pointer worldview. Some have argued that measuring
such observables would require “superinstruments” vastly larger
than ours, and so these differences are only visible at
unrealistic scales and hence negligible. That argument works in
classical physics because any sharp, approximate statement can be
replaced by an unsharp but exact one applying to individual
objects (statements like “quantity X is at least x percent
smaller than quantity Y”). In quantum physics no such replacement
is generally possible for the sensitive observables of interest.
Thus the superinstrument argument cannot justify dismissing the
discrepancy.

A further general remark: among physicists there are those who
aim for physics to describe what really is, and those who reject
ontological objectives as metaphysical but nevertheless insist
that science must describe phenomena in a realist language. That
latter stance might seem merely formal, but it has implications
for the sensitive observables problem. If one denies ontology
entirely, one can declare such observables devoid of physical
meaning because they are practically unmeasurable; a realist
cannot do that.

2. A second reason why environment theory does not reconcile
   quantum mechanics with conventional realism concerns its
   application to the general problem of accounting for
   macroscopic classical properties. In measurement theory, the
   S + A state at time to naturally takes the form of a quantum
   superposition of several well-peaked, weakly overlapping wave
   packets. For macroscopic objects like dust grains, however,
   the individual wave functions typically are not superpositions
   of distinct localized packets; yet environment theory also
   accounts for their classical appearance. This generality
   yields an additional reason preventing the theory’s
   interpretation as a straightforward “macro-objectivist”
   theory.

As discussed in Chapter 12, the environment argument is
fundamentally ensemble-based: the reduced density matrix ρs
describing an ensemble of the macroscopic systems (after tracing
out the environment) correctly represents infinitely many proper
mixtures, most of which — including the “most natural” one
composed of eigenvectors of ρs — do not correspond to
macroscopically localized objects. Consequently, although one
particular decomposition of the ensemble exhibits localization,
an individual who lacks the preconceived notion that macroscopic
objects must be localized could not derive localization from the
theory alone.

3.

---[chunk-boundary]---

To make the analysis more complete, I will develop a third
argument that goes in the same direction as the two previous
ones. It concerns the use, in environment-based accounts, of the
Wigner phase-space distribution f(x,p) defined in Section 6.9. As
shown there, the expectation value of any observable A for an
ensemble described by a statistical operator ρ can be written by
a formula (642) that closely resembles the classical expression
for the corresponding mean. Indeed, to each A one can generally
associate a phase-space function a(x,p) via formula (6.41), and
then formula (642) is formally identical to the classical
expression for the mean of a(x,p) when the ensemble is described
by the joint probability distribution f(x,p).

The main — and most conspicuous — difference between f(x,p) and
a classical probability distribution in phase space is that, for
some quantum states, f can take on negative values.

The most interesting case is of course when f(x,p) is nonnegative
for all x and p. This happens, for example, when the wave
function is a Gaussian wave packet; then f(x,p) is a Gaussian in
x and p. Some authors seem to infer from this that the system’s
x and p coordinates both simultaneously have definite values.
This is the most natural reading of Zurek’s remark [19] that
a system described by the Wigner function of
a minimum-uncertainty Gaussian wave packet “is localized in both
x and p.” As noted in Section 6.9, however, Heisenberg
uncertainty relations imply that those values cannot both be the
actual outcomes that would be obtained by successive measurements
of x and p on the same single system. One might still suppose
that x and p are supplementary or hidden variables, taking
definite values that vary from one element of the ensemble to
another. The mere hypothesis that such variables exist does not
by itself contradict the fact that quantum mechanics gives exact
experimentally verifiable predictions. But if the claim that the
system is localized in both x and p is intended as an implicit
acceptance of hidden variables, it entails more: it corresponds
to a specific form of the hidden-variables hypothesis. Following
Home and Whitaker (see Section 13.6), this is the
preassigned-values assumption, which asserts not only that the
outcomes of measurements of x or p are predetermined, but that
even in the absence of any such measurement, x and p on each
individual system actually have the very values those
measurements would reveal, with those values distributed across
the ensemble according to |ψ(x)|² and |φ(p)|² (ψ being the wave
function and φ its Fourier transform). As shown in Section 13.6,
when this assumption is taken as a general principle valid for
all quantum systems, it conflicts with the empirical predictions
of quantum mechanics. One might ask whether consistency could
nevertheless be recovered for the special class of systems whose
Wigner function is everywhere nonnegative. Fortunately, an
argument originally given by Schrödinger [21] (and mentioned by
Home and Whitaker) addresses precisely such a special case. It
concerns the ground state of the harmonic oscillator, which is
a Gaussian and therefore has a positive-definite Wigner function.
Its energy is sharp and equal to hν/2, so in an ensemble of
oscillators in the ground state every element has exactly the
same energy hν/2 (no fluctuations). Since the kinetic energy is
nonnegative, the potential energy kx²/2 of any oscillator cannot
exceed hν/2. But this contradicts the idea that the sharply
defined x coordinates in the ensemble are distributed according
to |ψ(x)|², because that distribution has infinite support and is
nonzero for x large enough that kx²/2 would exceed hν/2.
Therefore the answer is negative: even systems whose Wigner
function is everywhere nonnegative generally cannot be regarded
as localized in both x and p.

This observation should not prevent us from taking seriously what
environment theorists say about states for which f(x,p) does take
negative values for some x and p. That is the case, for instance,
for the two-peaked wave function (14.1). In such situations it is
immediately clear that f(x,p) cannot be interpreted as
a probability distribution. What the environment interaction does
— once the environment variables are traced out — is to suppress
the negative parts of f(x,p). Consequently, physicists who feel
justified in asserting that a system with a nonnegative f(x,p) is
localized in both x and p will similarly say that, after
interaction with its environment, the combined S + A system can
be treated as if its Wigner function were nonnegative. They may
thus conclude, for example, that the pointer of each measuring
instrument in the ensemble lies within a single definite
interval. But the Schrödinger-type objection just recalled
remains relevant here as well. It shows that we cannot upgrade
such a description to a strongly objective account of the real
state of affairs. The best we get is that, for all practical
purposes, after decoherence one may act as if the system were
localized in both x and p (or as if each pointer were in one
definite interval), and thus the theory “saves appearances.” In
other words, quantum mechanics gives a fully consistent
description of empirical reality in the operational sense defined
in Section 10.6. Yet the Schrödinger objection prevents
interpreting this as a strongly objective description of
reality-as-it-is. The same conclusion applies to the cosmological
quantum theory of Gell-Mann and Hartle (see Section 12.4).

There have been, especially within measurement theory proper,
many ingenious attempts to construct “realistic” descriptions
that differ little from the completeness (no-hidden-variables)
hypothesis (see Chapter 10). Although these proposals are rather
different in detail, when carefully examined they display the
same basic general feature as the environment theory: they
account well for phenomena as phenomena, but they cannot be read
as descriptions of “reality as it is.”

An obvious, but still worth-repeating, consequence is this: when
proponents of such approaches claim to offer a physical
interpretation of the measurement process, the qualifier
“physical” must be taken operationally, not ontologically. In
that context “physical” cannot mean that the process is
identified with an occurrence in a physical reality existing
independently of us and our way of conceiving it. Rather, it can
only mean that the process is not fundamentally different from
other processes, because all of them are phenomena in the
philosophical sense. In this respect, the common phrase among
environment-theory authors that “the environment continuously
measures the system” is more than a metaphor: it underlines the
implicit reference to mentality (weak objectivity) that underlies
textbook quantum mechanics and its measurement-theory extensions.

The Explicitly Ontological Approaches

Do the previous analyses imply that any strongly objective theory
reproducing the empirically verified predictions of quantum
mechanics is impossible? No. There are unconventional approaches
that do succeed in reproducing quantum predictions; these were
reviewed in Chapter 13. Here I shall confine myself to general,
conceptual remarks that extend those in Section 14.5.

First, any theory that reproduces the quantum predictions for
measurements on particle pairs must violate local causality (in
the broad sense discussed in Chapters 8 and 9); it must violate
either outcome independence or parameter independence or both.
Thus none of these theories can restore anything resembling the
classical worldview: all of them display a form of what we called
“nonseparability” in Section 8.5. For example, pilot-wave (Bohm)
theory involves either a quantum potential that does not decay
with distance (Bohm’s original view) or, in Bell’s formulation,
highly nonlocal fields corresponding to the real and imaginary
parts of the wave function and having the same ontological status
as the classical electric and magnetic fields. The GRW collapse
theory involves no hidden variables but assigns ontological
status to wave functions that, for microscopic systems, are
practically identical to the standard wave functions and are
therefore highly nonlocal for composite systems.

Second, because these theories violate local causality, one might
think they stand on the same footing as conventional quantum
mechanics regarding their relation to special relativity.
Actually, this is not so. Two facts matter: (a) as shown in
Section 8.2, no theory that exactly reproduces quantum
predictions permits superluminal signaling, and (b) conventional
textbook quantum mechanics is only weakly objective. If one
accepts conventional quantum mechanics, one thereby renounces
strong objectivity in physics and thus has no strict ground for
demanding that relativity be strongly objective. This allows one
to retain a formal, operational Einsteinian causality, namely the
rule that quantum field operators localized in
spacelike-separated regions commute. That is the causality
principle adopted in quantum field theory texts. In this way
a kind of “peaceful coexistence” (to borrow Shimony’s and
Redhead’s phrase) between quantum theory and special relativity
is maintained in practice. But this reconciliation is achieved at
the expense of realism, so it cannot be applied to ontologically
interpreted theories. For ontological theories, a lasting
conflict with special relativity appears unavoidable.

Compared with this defect, the other shortcomings of the
ontological approaches seem relatively minor, although they are
still significant. Essentially they are (a) that there are
several such theories, many of which are hard or impossible to
distinguish experimentally because they all reproduce the same
verifiable predictions (those of ordinary quantum mechanics), and
(b) that they have been “unfruitful” in the sense that they have
not led to experimentally verifiable predictions beyond those
derivable more simply from ordinary quantum mechanics. This state
of affairs may relate to the fact that the most distinctive
feature of these theories is that they “materialize” nonlocality,
whereas nonlocality itself is “nonoperative” — it does not
provide a way to alter distant phenomena. For these reasons it is
not exaggerated to regard many of these theories more as elegant
metaphysics fitting a scientific age than as fully scientific
proposals.

### 14.11 Status of Nonseparability

We defined “nonseparability” (Chapter 9) as the failure of two
concepts long regarded as valid: divisibility by thought and
locality. The discovery that these concepts can be violated is
analogous to earlier conceptual revolutions, such as the
inadequacy of universal Newtonian time revealed by relativity
(time dilation of moving clocks, etc.). The parallels are clear:
both discoveries originated in theoretical developments
(relativity theory and the Bell theorems) and were then confirmed
experimentally (many relativistic experiments in one case;
Clauser, Fry, Aspect, and others in the other). From this
viewpoint, both the relativity of time and nonseparability
deserve to be called scientific discoveries.

There is, however, an epistemological difference between the two.
To make this precise, I suggest distinguishing two levels of
scientifically established knowledge. The lower level requires
experimental confirmation and preferably a place in some general
theory. The higher level is reached when the piece of knowledge
must also be taken into account to explain data other than those
collected to test it. Relativity of time attains this higher
level: it must be explicitly included when predicting the
behavior of relativistic unstable particles and in many other
experiments not designed to test relativity. Because scientific
laws are general, many findings that reach the lower level
automatically reach the higher one. But it seems unlikely that
nonseparability will do so. The reason is tied to parameter
independence (Chapter 8) and the fact that nonseparability cannot
be directly exploited to transmit information. The fact that
standard operational formulations of parts of physics, such as
quantum field theory, do not need to mention nonseparability is
connected to this circumstance.

### 14.12 Outlook, Veiled Reality

To summarize the preceding sections: we have strong arguments
against conventional (traditional) realism. In essence:

1. Conventional realism assumes strong objectivity, which
   conflicts with the standard — and by far the most empirically
   successful — formulation of quantum mechanics.

2. There are ontologically interpretable alternatives (see
   Chapter 13), but as we have seen they can be made to agree
   with the empirically verified predictions of quantum mechanics
   only at the price of sacrificing the kind of peaceful
   coexistence between quantum mechanics and relativity described
   in Sections 14.5 and 14.10. Accepting strong objectivity thus
   forces us to abandon at least some essential features of
   special relativity. That is a high price for realism. Even if
   one is willing to pay it, the situation is problematic: many
   models exist (and more will likely appear), none of the
   existing ones has yielded distinct, experimentally verified
   predictions over the decades, and attempting to discriminate
   among them experimentally seems an endless task.

3. The reviews in Chapters 10–12 of measurement theories and
   accounts of classical appearance within the conventional
   quantum framework are instructive. Many authors who developed
   these accounts apparently believed they were building theories
   that, like classical physics, could be interpreted as
   describing physical reality as it really is. But scrutinizing
   the premises of these theories shows that, in their present
   form and without adding new ingredients, none of them
   — whether the environment-based theory, the
   operations-and-effects formalism, Griffiths’
   consistent-histories approach, Omnès’ work, the Gell-Mann and
   Hartle decoherent-histories account, or others encountered in
   those chapters — is ontologically interpretable.

Against radical idealism or radical phenomenism we have also
assembled strong arguments (Sections 14.3 and 14.8). Making
knowledge logically prior to existence (a) appears basically
inconsistent, and (b) even if one overlooks that inconsistency,
leads to misleading consequences for the development of
scientific research.

If radical idealism (neo-Kantianism) and its crypto-radical
descendants are rejected — that is, if the existence of
“something” independent of us (whether this “something” is a set
of things, events, minds, or whatever term one prefers) is taken
as logically necessary — and if, at the same time, the detailed
features of that “something” are beyond our reach (so that
ontological theories remain too speculative), then two
possibilities remain: either this “something” is entirely
unknowable (a “pure X”), or we can get some limited, possibly
allegorical knowledge about it. I argue here that several
convincing arguments favor the latter possibility.

Some of these arguments were mentioned in Sections 14.3 and 14.8.
Argument 1 (Section 14.8) led us to the view that while the form
of our scientific descriptions owes much to the structures of our
own minds, it does not owe everything to them. For example, it
would be mathematically simpler if the radiation field were just
a scalar, but it is not. Although we cannot make a definitive
ontological claim about its ultimate nature, we do possess
knowledge that cannot be regarded as a mere construction of the
human mind.

Argument 2 (same section) observes that some beautifully rational
theories are falsified by experiment. Such facts strongly
indicate that there is “something” outside us that says “no”
— and they give us some (even if negative) knowledge about that
something. Nonseparability is an example of this sort of negative
knowledge.

Remark: Nonseparability, although a scientifically established
piece of knowledge, does not share the same status as many other
theoretical results because it cannot be directly exploited in
various areas of physics. This supports the view that
nonseparability is not (or is hardly) relevant to empirical
reality — the subject matter of physics proper — but instead
pertains to the deeper layer that independent reality is.

Argument 5 in Section 14.8 favors a modern version of Poincaré’s
structural realism, suggesting that the general structures of
independent reality are not entirely hidden from us.

Taken together, these considerations favor the conception of
a “veiled” independent reality. A preliminary explanation of this
phrase is as follows (Section 15.1 expands on it):

1. Independent reality is not in space-time (see Section 14.6).
   Since Schrödinger’s discovery of entanglement (and even
   earlier), it has been known that quantum mechanics does not
   square well with the concept of locality. That this mismatch
   is not a temporary shortcoming but something deeper has become
   clear because any ontological reformulation of quantum
   mechanics necessarily involves nonlocality (Bell). Thus while
   space — and time and space-time — is the arena where phenomena
   rightly appear to take place, it is not an arena in which
   independent reality itself evolves. Space-time should be
   viewed primarily as an allegorical, human mode of apprehending
   independent reality.

2. This veiled reality partly exceeds our cognitive powers; on
   this basis (and not merely on the basis of indeterminism) we
   can agree with Pauli that reality has an “irrational” aspect,
   a term Pauli used in his correspondence to express that the
   theory may be rational while reality itself resists complete
   rational description.

3. Still, science says something about independent reality, but
   that information seems limited to general structural features
   and is far from exhaustive. Thus the object of physics — the
   set of phenomena — should be called empirical or effective
   reality. Both notions — veiled reality per se and empirical
   reality — are significant. It is worth noting that the
   veiled-reality idea here is not derived from any particular
   theoretical model (for example, bootstrap models); deriving it
   from a specific model would make its validity dependent on
   that model. The derivation given here does not suffer from
   that drawback.

Finally, although the veiled-reality idea resembles Poincaré’s
structural realism, there is at least one important difference.
When Poincaré spoke of “the images substituted to the real
objects that Nature will hide from us for ever” he used “objects”
in the plural. Nondivisibility by thought — or nonlocality — now
suggests that the plural may no longer be reasonable when
referring to independent reality. We should therefore speak of
independent reality in the singular rather than of real objects.
This shift from plural to singular has far-reaching consequences
for any metaphysical extrapolation one might make from these
developments.
