### Postface

By now many physicists are well aware of both the shortcomings of
radical idealism and the maladjustment of physicalism to the
physical knowledge of today. Lacking a clear "third way," these
scientists are on the verge of accepting the veiled-reality
concept. Some adopt it only reluctantly; they ponder and mutter,
"Somehow I hardly like it." Being rationally minded, they do not
regard this as a logical objection, but the hesitation is real.

My main purpose in this postface is to explain to such fellow
scientists why, in my view, the veiled-reality concept is not at
all depressing but rather both correct and appealing. I am eager
to offer this explanation, but to do so I must first recall a few
very general ideas. Let us go over them quickly.

One of these ideas is that scientific thought is not the only
valid form of thought. This age-old truism must today be
supplemented by a simple observation. Given the present state of
physical science, we can no longer imagine it as confined to
a well-defined factual domain outside of which it would have
nothing to say or raise no meaningful questions. For any one of
us, this should rule out a strict, comfortable separation between
a domain where our scientific thought dominates and another
domain where other valid modes of thinking would legitimately
rule. All this implies that, however strong the need to sort out
questions and procedures and not to mix everything
indiscriminately, there is an even stronger necessity for
a physicist interested in foundational questions not to ignore
these other—nonscientific but nevertheless respectable—modes of
thought.

In this postface some "other ways of thinking" are therefore
considered first (Section 1). They are, as will appear, neither
deductive nor inductive in the scientific sense: they are deeply
rooted in implicit or explicit philosophical options.
Consequently, taking them seriously requires that I state my own
option, which I do in Section 2. That option is personal; readers
who find it unpalatable may simply ignore these pages. The
conclusions of this book, stated in the preceding chapters, stand
independently of the personal option explained in Section 2.

Other Valid Ways of Thinking

There are many other valid ways of thinking, but for conciseness
I shall focus mainly on philosophical approaches. One common
question is whether the great philosophical systems—each often
associated with a philosopher's name—can be considered eternal.
In philosophical circles the answer is often yes. The claim is
that, whereas in science cumulative effects make past theories
either obsolete or fully integrated into present knowledge, in
philosophy the major systems of the past remain "alive" with
their peculiar flavor and continue to be sources of inspiration
and productive development.

In a sense this view is true and supports the legitimacy of
"taking options." Its truth is partly due to the fact that
philosophical systems are rarely disproved by other philosophical
systems, even when they are incompatible. Admittedly, elements of
many systems have been falsified by later scientific discoveries,
but philosophers usually argue that such refutations affect only
side issues and that the backbone or "spirit" of the system
remains acceptable.

That investigations in this book had to refer to Kant, Hume, and
the like testifies, in a way, to this opinion. On the other hand,
one should not push this view to the extreme of claiming that all
philosophical systems are equally tenable. If too many "side
issues" have been falsified, the system loses plausibility. And
in some cases, elements that were falsified are not mere side
issues. For example, at the dawn of inquiry, when we knew nothing
of modern physics, the Aristotelian view—that sensible forms are
real, so familiar concepts such as matter, motion, space, and
trajectories correspond to basic elements of independent
reality—might have seemed as plausible as the Platonic-Kantian
view that these concepts refer to mere phenomena. Today, as shown
by the investigations reported in this book, the second view
remains tenable (and is, to some extent, even supported by
physics), while the Aristotelian view is, if not outright
falsified, at least relativized. One of the main pillars of
Aristotelianism (or at least of its most usual interpretation) is
thereby shown to be shaky. This has implications for
philosophical systems that rely substantially on
Aristotelianism—materialism and, in some aspects, Thomism, for
example—although this is not a fundamental blow to Christianity,
since many prominent Christian thinkers did not base their
doctrine primarily on Aristotle.

Still, most major philosophical schemes of the past somehow
"manage to survive," albeit sometimes after dramatic changes in
their basic views. For what follows, let us briefly review the
main types.

(a) Conventional Realism

Conventional (sometimes wrongly called "naive") realism is the
theory that, in principle, independent reality (the "external
reality" of philosophers) is knowable in all its details,
including the microscopic ones. Contemporary physics has dealt it
serious blows, as we saw throughout this book. Yet it is not
completely dead, as shown by the ontologically interpretable
models reviewed in Chapter 13. Indeed, a number of clearheaded
physicists still believe in it; they recognize how demanding this
option is, avoid sham solutions that conflate two meanings of the
word "phenomenon" (phenomena per se and phenomena for us), and
build models that are genuinely ontologically interpretable. But
these models face substantial technical
difficulties—unfruitfulness, plurality, and problems of
compatibility with relativity theory—and an even more pernicious
defect.

Originally, the rationale for conventional realism was the
commonsense view that it seemed the most immediately plausible
way of accounting for what we observe, including correlations and
trajectories. However, as we saw, for basic reasons these models
can account for such phenomena only in roundabout ways that look
extremely contrived and far-fetched. For these reasons,
conventional realism today seems to be in a state of artificial
survival. I will not consider it further.

(b) Schemes That Place Language at the Top

Between the systems of philosophers such as Carnap, Bohr (in his
philosophical essays), and Wittgenstein (in the Tractatus) there
are momentous differences, of course. But one point they—and many
others—have in common is the importance they attribute to
language. This is illustrated by Carnap's "linguistic framework,"
Bohr's remark that "we are suspended in language," and
Wittgenstein's famous "Whereof we cannot speak, thereof we must
keep silent." Within this conception, Föllesdal's definition of
meaning—quoted by J. A. Wheeler—"Meaning is the joint product of
all the evidence available to people who communicate" is adequate
and telling. Communication between human beings is thereby
elevated to the status of what is logically prior to what we are
allowed to think. Idealism, neo-Kantianism, phenomenalism, and
proposals by several leading contemporary philosophers (who
cannot easily be classified into those schools) move more or less
along these lines. This shared critical basis is, despite their
differences, less easily falsified (at least by physics) than the
foundations of conventional realism.

(c) Approaches That Deny the Logical Priority of Language:
Bergson

A second family of conceptions opposed to conventional realism
differs fundamentally from the language-centered view. Instead of
placing discursive language at the top, these conceptions treat
it as a tool that, indispensable for action, produces deceptive
categorizations when applied to what these thinkers consider most
important—which, for them, is not action.

Most advocates of these conceptions ground them in an analysis of
the nature of intelligence. Such an analysis is difficult because
intelligence can be scrutinized only with the tool called
intelligence. The efforts of philosophers who have pursued this
thorny path should therefore be judged with benevolence, even by
scientists who consider some of their arguments insufficiently
supported by empirical evidence.

One of these philosophers was Henri Bergson. Although on several
subjects Bergson's views were distinctly speculative, his ideas
on the nature of intelligence are interesting and worth
considering. He derived them from his conceptions of evolution,
which he grounded on a thoughtful analysis of the scientific
knowledge and theories of his time. Similar conceptions can be
found in the works of more recent authors.

In short, Bergson accepted neo-Darwinism and mutation theory, but
with the restriction that he could not believe mutations are
purely accidental. One of his arguments was that even orthodox
mutation theorists seemed to accept that, after long periods of
stagnation, a whole species can develop a tendency to change,
which suggests that the tendency itself is not entirely
accidental; under such conditions it is logically conceivable
that the direction of change could also be nonrandom. Since
strict neo-Darwinism is still debated in some scientific circles,
this premise of Bergson's approach cannot be dismissed as mere
fantasy.

He concluded that past evolution was far from predetermined:
chance played a major role, but chance was not the sole agent.
Some vague general tendency must have been at work—not directed
toward a specific project (Bergson was not a finalist), but
a general impetus toward increasing sophistication. This impetus
produced progressive diversification. A few major directions
emerged along which, despite many accidents, life could develop:
one toward plants, another toward animals. In the animal
direction the "tool" that was selected in the struggle for life
was mobility. That quest for mobility took different forms and
led to important distinctions, notably between arthropods and
vertebrates. In arthropods motricity is distributed among many
specialized appendages; in vertebrates activity is concentrated
in a few paired limbs whose functions are less tied to their
form. The human hand exemplifies maximal independence, capable of
many kinds of activities.

Bergson next observed that any activity requires knowledge,
though not always conscious knowledge. He called the most
general, primitive form of knowledge "intuition." Life is an
endeavor to get certain things out of matter, and intuition, in
its various forms, is essentially a means to that end. Because of
the noted differences, arthropods and vertebrates developed
intuition differently. In arthropods the tool is an inherent,
highly specialized part of the organism; their intuition evolved
into instinct, with little need for conscious choice. In
vertebrates, the capacity to use external tools improved and was
eventually mastered by humans. Making and shaping tools requires
considering alternatives, rejecting some and keeping others—that
is, conscious knowledge.

Bergson emphasized that it is a longstanding error to regard
vegetative, instinctive, and rational life as three successive
steps of the same evolutionary tendency; rather, they are three
divergent directions that split while increasing in complexity.
Viewed this way, intelligence is not primarily aimed at knowing
and understanding; those are means by which instruments may be
conceived and built. By natural selection, these means developed
most fully where immediately useful. This explains why we have
precise, nonambiguous concepts for stones, trees, fruits,
animals, and other macroscopic solid bodies, yet encounter severe
difficulties when speaking of particles or quantum states without
ultimately referring to macroscopic objects (for example,
instrument pointers). Bergson never claimed that we can
meaningfully speak only of macroscopic objects; intelligence is
adaptable and can extend its domain remarkably, especially when
we can refer to pointers, but not only then. Nevertheless, on
Bergson's line, it should not surprise us that our intelligence
does not enable us to describe independent reality as it really
is: intelligence evolved chiefly as a means for action, so it
became a knowledge of relationships between things rather than of
things in themselves. By contrast, instinct, by its nature,
remains a genuine—though virtual and unconscious—knowledge of
things.

Bergson supplemented these arguments with a speculative belief:
although intelligence and instinct diverged, the split was never
complete. A fringe of intelligence remains at the outskirts of
instinct, and conversely a fringe of intuition remains at the
outskirts of intelligence. Since discursive language is the
channel by which intelligence's knowledge is conveyed, it cannot
adequately express the elements of this deep intuition of things;
indeed, it may even obscure them. Our best hope of approaching
them is through nondiscursive modes of thought and
expression—contemplation, music, art (and later thinkers would
add the unconscious and archetypes)—because these modes might
relate to the primeval intuition of things.

A book on quantum physics is not the place for a detailed
critique of Bergson's ideas in light of present knowledge of
ontogenesis. But taken with due reservations, Bergson's views can
suggest useful ideas. The next brief section explains in what
respect I find them inspiring.

2 Own Options

There is no accounting for taste. I prefer Debussy to Schoenberg
and Fra Angelico to Andy Warhol. On matters where no decisive
clue is available, I favor freshness of approach. Perhaps for
this reason, of the two schemes labeled (b) and (c) in Section 1,
I am instinctively inclined toward the latter. Walking at night
along a seashore we may have intuitions of deep things lying
beyond language. I appreciate having reasons to consider that
some of those intuitions may point to something nonillusory.

Is that naive or dangerous? Not at all.

It is not naive. Neither philosophical nor scientific objections
to such a view withstand scrutiny, as we have seen throughout
this book. The idea is no more naive than the common belief that
physics relates to Being. Science and commonsense both take
phenomena as their unquestioned domain, yet physics can still
offer glimpses of the real structure of what is. Why should not
other approaches do so as well? This is consonant with the idea
encountered in scheme (c) that the true realm of intelligence is
limited to what relates to possible human action—especially if
one accepts Bergson's postulate that a fringe of the intuition of
things remains at the outskirts of intelligence.

Admittedly, the similarity between the veiled-reality conception
and Bergsonism is only partial. What the veiled-reality
conception can borrow from Bergson is the notion that
intelligence is essentially conscious knowledge of
relationships—a facet only of a more general faculty Bergson
called intuition—and the assumption that, although humans
developed intuition into intelligence, we retained a glimmering
halo of the original intuition and a corresponding power to
reach, albeit indistinctly, something deeper. The point on which
the veiled-reality conception must differ concerns the nature of
that "something." For Bergson it is the élan vital, an original
life-impetus forcing its way through matter and dispersed by
resistance. In the veiled-reality conception that something is
Being. Conceptually this is a large difference: if we expressed
both views in terms invoking "God," Bergson's picture would
suggest a small, almost "Kantian" God, whereas the veiled-reality
conception hints at a great, hypercosmic God prior to any
detailed discursive reality. Yet in practice the difference is
less important than what both views bring to human existence and
possible goals: both exalt a quest for an ultimate Real that goes
beyond the strictly scientific search and appeals to affectivity.

This enlarged spirit of quest for an unreachable Ultimate Real is
precisely what has inspired, at all times, the most splendid
works of art—poems to Ammon, psalms, Greek temples, cathedrals,
the music of J. S. Bach, and many others. My claim that this
notion of the Real is valuable should therefore not be dismissed
as mere fancy.

Is veiled reality dangerous? Some related views can be. When the
idea is personified as a personal God, the wishes and
commandments attributed to such a God have sometimes led to
slaughters and wars. Belief in things beyond human reason can
foster credence in clairvoyance, astrology, witchcraft, and so
on. But these deviations stem from identifying phenomena with
Being—the opposite of the position advocated here. Properly
understood, the veiled-reality concept does not commit that
identification. Therefore the answer to the question of danger is
no.

To sum up: the veiled-reality concept is likable. It is not
naive, and when properly understood it is not dangerous. That it
sounds unfamiliar to scientific ears should not, in principle,
make it unattractive to scientists.
