### Preface

This whole book is built on the conviction that anyone who tries
to form an idea of the world—and of man's place in it—must take
the findings of quantum physics very seriously. I don't deny the
difficulty or the need to resort to scientific technicalities.

The truth just stated is not immediately obvious. Quantum physics
opens wide vistas precisely because it clashes with commonsense
views; at first, of course, that clash produces dissatisfaction.
It may take time before one recognizes that the mismatch points
to a deep and fascinating subject.

- I still remember the strange perplexity I felt as a beginner
  reading texts on quantum physics and, later, articles on its
  foundations. Those sources described the relevant facts and the
  formalism correctly, but I found them unclear about what the
  authors meant the formulas to represent. Were the rules merely
  efficient recipes for predicting experiment _outcomes_, or were
  they intended to describe the very _processes_ the experiments
  probe?
    - In some places the argument seemed to rest on the tacit
      axiom that correctly predicting observations is all we may
      expect of science, and everything else is just empty words.
    - Yet in introductions, summaries, and applications (to
      solid-state physics, thermodynamics, cosmology) the same
      authors would speak as if the probabilities and other
      formal quantities referred to events “as they really
      happen,” expecting me to accept that meaning without
      question. This inconsistency was frustrating.

- My unease was not relieved by the quantum measurement theories
  available at the time. It grew particularly acute when I read
  a then-common suggestion about correlations at a distance: that
  entanglement could be eliminated simply by describing systems
  with density matrices instead of state vectors.
    - Reflecting on this convinced me that any tentative
      realistic description (in the sense of conventional
      realism) requires distinguishing proper from improper
      mixtures, as I called them. The negative but
      epistemologically significant consequence is that
      entanglement, or nonseparability, is not merely a quirky
      feature of the wave-function or state-vector formalism.
    - In slightly different form it appears in any conventional
      (that is, hidden-variables–free) quantum-mechanical
      description that one treats as revealing how “things really
      are,” whether that description uses density matrices or
      some other concept.
    - When John Bell, who worked with me at CERN, told me about
      his disproof of “local causality,” I at first thought his
      result was simply another demonstration of the same point.
    - He convinced me, however, that although violation of local
      causality and entanglement are closely related, his result
      is more general because it also applies to quantum theories
      that admit hidden variables.
    - I regarded that generalization as crucial:
        - it showed something philosophy alone had failed to
          prove, namely that many of our so-called commonsense
          views of the world (indeed, views inherited from the
          eighteenth century) are mistaken and cannot be rescued
          by speculative extensions confined to their conceptual
          framework.
        - Viewed this way, `entanglement` and the failure of
          `local causality` together raise fascinating conceptual
          problems. I should add that Bell and I agreed amicably
          that we differed about the directions in which
          solutions to these problems should most fruitfully be
          sought.

- This book aims to analyze further that constellation of
  conceptual problems. In doing so it departs from the mainstream
  of the scientific literature. Most books on QM fall into one of
  two categories:
    - they either focus mainly on mathematical techniques and say
      little about interpretation, or
    - they concentrate on epistemological or historical aspects.

- When the latter refer to the mathematical formalism, they
  rarely go into the detail needed to analyze the implicit
  philosophical assumptions and epistemological implications of
  the more elaborate theoretical developments.

- Here I attempt to combine the two approaches. My final
  objective is interpretation of the existing theory (or
  theories). I believe that sound views about interpretation
  cannot be reached without a fairly detailed description of the
  formulas and physical arguments that constitute the theoretical
  models—including the most recent ones. Such a description is
  given in the central part of the book.

- By contrast, the first chapter and the last three chapters
  focus on the philosophical problems themselves. Chapter 1 is an
  introduction for physicists: a reminder of essential proposals
  from past and present philosophical research about reality,
  experience, and the link between them, with special emphasis on
  problems raised by physics.
- The last three chapters (14–16) form the essential part of the
  book; they present an attempt to extract philosophical
  implications and suggestions from contemporary knowledge of
  quantum physics. There are stylistic differences between these
  four chapters and the rest of the book, reflecting the fact
  that physics and epistemology are two genuinely different
  approaches, which I try here to bring closer together.

- My interest in conceptual and interpretive problems should make
  clear that I am not one of those who reject realism on a priori
  grounds—even in its so-called naive form.
- If physics unambiguously offered a picture of the world
  satisfying the conditions of physicalism (also called “physical
  realism,” the doctrine that physics is a candidate to describe
  reality exhaustively and in detail), I would be inclined to
  accept it.

- My point, however, is that physics does not unambiguously do
  so, and one main purpose of this book is to make that point
  clear. This is not easy.

- Elementary claims that merely inspecting the formal structure
  of QM (Heisenberg relations and so on) suffices to rule out
  physicalism are incorrect.

- From time to time a theorist proposes a brilliant scheme
  intended to restore physical realism, and some (though not all)
  come quite close.

- Several of those theorists are friends whose work I thoroughly
  admire; I therefore apologize to them collectively that this
  book contains a critical analysis, not so much of their
  theories as of the plausibility of regarding any one of them as
  the true description of reality. That is not to say I gained
  nothing from their investigations—most chapters of this book
  testify otherwise.

- I could not, however, benefit from everything published in this
  vast domain, and I did not attempt an exhaustive review. In
  particular, I systematically ignored theoretical proposals that
  do not meet a certain condition of epistemological strictness
  explained below (Section 1.6).

- I tried to do justice to all proposals that do meet that
  condition, but I cannot rule out the possibility that
  I overlooked some. Perhaps I missed the rare idea that would
  make physical realism scientifically convincing. If later
  developments show that to be the case, my only excuse will be
  that I surveyed the field with zeal and impartiality.

- We live at a time when many students in applied fields need to
  learn the basic ideas and calculation techniques of QM and to
  do so competently.
- Students of the philosophy of science would no doubt benefit
  from the same path, and it would be regrettable if they alone
  found that path unduly steep. Many of them have been trained
  more in the humanities than in the natural sciences.

- With this in mind, although my ambition is to tackle delicate
  questions at the level of experts, I have chosen to begin more
  or less from the beginning: I explain, even if concisely, the
  essential elements of quantum-mechanical techniques and give
  calculation details that experienced theorists are, of course,
  free to skip.

- The book is intended to be readable by first-year graduate
  students in physics and, for its main part, understandable by
  students of epistemology with a smattering of physics.

- It can be seen in some respects as a sequel to my Conceptual
  Foundations of QM, going more to the core of the philosophical
  problem and taking account of important theoretical
  developments since that book appeared.

- I also aimed to make this book fairly self-contained; to that
  end I incorporated the small introductory scientific chapters
  (1–7) of Conceptual Foundations with relatively short
  additions.

- Still, my main purpose in writing this book has been to argue
  for my own views. In that sense it is not a textbook. I am well
  aware that the arguments here do not settle matters once and
  for all.

- In particular, I reiterate that the critical analysis in the
  central chapters of a set of current physicalist proposals does
  not have the force of a no-go theorem.

- New, brilliant proposals for reconciling quantum physics with
  physical realism will no doubt continue to appear: how could
  one demonstrate in advance that none of them will ultimately
  prove satisfactory and convincing?

- This subordination to the passage of time is, however, the
  common fate of all scientific inquiry. Such considerations do
  not, in my view, undermine the reasoned expectation that the
  general idea underlying the conclusions of this book will
  remain credible.

- In other words, it seems to me that, when all is said and done,
  the notion of a veiled reality emerges with increased
  plausibility from what is presented here.

- Bernard d'Espagnat Paris and Orsay, 1994
