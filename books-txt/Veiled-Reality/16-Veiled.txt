# 16 Lessons and Hints from Quantum Physics

The two main conclusions developed in this book, drawn in
Chapters 14 and 15, are that independent reality is veiled and
that physics is free from paradoxes when viewed as a description
of empirical reality. What remains is to examine what these views
imply for areas we have not explored in detail. In doing so, it
is important to distinguish between genuine information and mere
hints.

### 16.1 Information

#### An indication to physicalist scientists

This is an elementary point and it concerns only those
physicalist scientists who regard the ultimate purpose of science
as discovering and faithfully describing whatever “really
exists,” believing that this process of lifting the veil of
appearances is steadily progressing. Many scientists, especially
outside physics, still think this way and are usually unmoved by
philosophical objections. But contemporary physics — particularly
the aspects discussed in this book — shows that such a view is
naive. A special version of this naive view is actually
contradicted by present-day physics: high-energy physics
vocabulary (terms like “elementary particles,” “particle states,”
etc.) suggests a philosophical atomism, the idea that Reality is
made of many tiny localized elements. As we have seen, that
picture is incompatible with modern knowledge. In fact, the
information we now have (see Section 14.4) makes such an
atomistic picture less scientifically plausible than its
opposite: a Plotinian-like view of a more unified Being.

#### A point to be noted by “nonscientists”

There is a common, lazy way of dismissing questions about the
concept of reality. People with only a smattering of philosophy
sometimes say: “Of course the ultimate elements of Reality are
unknowable, but so what? It would be preposterous to think we
could know all of Nature’s secrets. We already know a lot and we
will know more; what more could we reasonably ask for?” This
stance implicitly assumes that common sense, corrected here and
there by science, already gives us ontological knowledge of
Reality that raises no basic problem and is essentially
consistent with everyday ideas. However, an essential lesson of
contemporary physics — confirming the concerns of distinguished
thinkers of the past — is that this conservative, reassuring view
cannot be maintained. In Chapter 1 we reviewed philosophers’
doubts about the ontological significance of notions such as
independently existing “objects,” absolute time or space-time,
and causality. After analyzing contemporary quantum physics, we
must admit, even if we are realists, that whatever theoretical
framework we accept requires discarding many of our old
commonsense ontological certainties. Indeed, even the least
speculative “realist” theories that remain empirically viable are
“realist” only in the sense I called “far realism” (see Section
13.8): they describe “what exists” in ways that are totally
unlike our daily and scientific experience and — it seems — are
useless for predicting future experience.

#### An item that might interest materialist philosophers

Some contemporary materialist philosophers concede that old naive
forms of materialism, equated with mechanism or atomism, are no
longer tenable. They propose instead a version of materialism
about mind: the claim that thinking, feeling, and perceiving
emerge from what does not think, hope, or perceive (see A.
Comte‑Sponville). Faced with this definition we must ask whether
the nonthinking substratum is assumed to be knowable. If not,
then this form of materialism is an arbitrary postulate,
extrapolating from commonsense observations (stones do not think)
to a Reality assumed to lie beyond experience. If this Reality is
truly unknowable, what justifies thinking it resembles stones
rather than minds? More precisely, what evidence is there that
Being has “parts” and that the most basic parts do not think? If,
on the other hand, the answer is that Reality is knowable, we
must ask whether Reality should be described entirely in strongly
objective terms. If one accepts only weak objectivity, then the
definition of materialism under discussion becomes incoherent,
because in a weakly objective description basic statements are
given meaning only by referring to observations, and observation
presupposes mind. To be self-consistent, the materialist must
therefore demand that whatever (complete or provisional)
description of Reality is proposed be interpretable in strongly
objective terms (as classical physics largely was, perhaps
excluding statistical thermodynamics). That demand can be met
because most of the ontologically interpretable theories
discussed in Chapter 13 are consistent. But those theories face
such serious difficulties that it is nearly impossible to
scientifically endorse any one of them with confidence.
Consequently, this form of materialism renders what contemporary
science reliably provides essentially uninteresting (merely a set
of working recipes), while what is interesting from science is
not reliable. This departs substantially from the traditional
notion of scientific materialism.

#### A point concerning theorists

Theorists should not be unduly awed by the formalism of quantum
physics. The Hilbert-space formalism is powerful, efficient, and
mathematically elegant, and for those reasons many theoretical
physicists understandably treat it as embodying, at least in
principle, the whole of possible operational knowledge or even as
the “ultimate truth.” If one adopts that completeness hypothesis
(see Section 4.2), then various conceptually striking
consequences might follow for would-be realists: for example,
abandoning ordinary two-valued logic or accepting “delayed
choices” in certain experiments. But we must remember that the
completeness hypothesis is only a hypothesis. The existence of
alternative ontologically interpretable models, such as
pilot-wave and GRW theories, suffices to show that the formalism
need not be the ultimate account. Therefore, when we speak of
positive consequences that quantum physics has for our worldview,
we must be cautious.

#### A point concerning epistemologists

This point concerns the notion of Reality or “Being” and is
subtler than the foregoing ones. Because of the previous remarks,
physicists must concede that, despite appearances, the notion of
reality has unavoidable philosophical aspects, and we must accept
the philosophers’ objections to its uncritical use. However, we
should not push their rebuttal to extremes. In the last two
chapters I developed arguments showing that a notion of reality
independent of our collective mental structures is unavoidable
and necessary. Those arguments are mainly philosophical, and some
philosophers will understandably remain unconvinced and continue
to deny that Reality‑per‑se has meaning. Consequently, the
question of whether quantum mechanics allows any lesson in
epistemology or ontology that does not depend on ontological
assumptions is not obvious.

A useful way to avoid getting lost in side issues is to ask how
a leading physicist with epistemological and ontological
interests — for example, Einstein — would have his views altered
by modern developments in quantum physics. Einstein has often
been portrayed as moving from skeptical empiricism to realism.
That characterization is partly correct but, as Michel Paty has
emphasized, Einstein was neither a radical empiricist nor a naive
realist. His mature stance on reality was subtle. Early on he
rejected traditional empiricist claims that the axioms of
a theory are logically derivable from sense data, insisting that
concept choice in science involves free inventions. He thus
departed from positivism while retaining the important insight
(from Mach and others) that realism is neither obvious nor
provable. Einstein's realism was critical: concepts gain meaning
through their overall relation to experience, but only a whole
system of concepts can be assigned truth values, and such systems
are free human creations constrained by the need to coordinate
sense data consistently and economically. For Einstein,
verification concerns an inseparable set of mutually related
ideas and experiments. He wrote that notions like “physical
reality,” “reality of the external world,” and “real state of
a system” are not a priori necessary or illegitimate; their
status should be settled by verification in this systemic sense.
Behind these words, he placed a program.

Einstein also insisted on further conditions for a valid
conceptual system. One (a), stated explicitly in nonquantum
physics, was that descriptions should ultimately refer to
spatiotemporal conceptual objects. Another (b), related but
distinct, was that some forms of counterfactual reasoning—those
that support the notion of “elements of reality” discussed in
Section 9.2—are legitimate. Although Einstein did not state (b)
in exactly this formal way, he expressed similar ideas, for
example: “Nobody doubts that at any given time the center of mass
of the Moon has a definite position, even in the absence of any
real or potential observer.” Such certainty must be tied to
observations we could in principle perform. He also wrote that
physics aims to conceptually apprehend what exists independently
of its observed becoming and that the scientist seeks to describe
a world independent of perceiving procedures. These remarks show
his caution.

This brief review of Einstein’s critical approach shows that,
even if we adopt a programmatic notion of reality à la Einstein,
it is not an empty or meaningless notion. Thus, even philosophers
eager to reject metaphysical commitments should concede that
“reality” can be meaningful in physics. It is therefore
conceivable that physics can teach something about this notion
even to those who wish to avoid metaphysical assumptions.
Admittedly, rejecting any metaphysical postulate entails denying
the priority of existence over knowledge — a move that makes
concepts like nonseparability hard to regard as genuine lessons
from physics, because when priority is reversed the distinction
between “reality” and “empirical reality” blurs. As we saw in
Chapter 15, there is a consistent way to conceive empirical
reality that is free from nonseparability effects
(nonseparability cannot even be defined in that realm). Then what
remains, if anything?

One positive result that remains is that the two statements
“Physics describes an intelligible reality that exists
independently of us” and “That reality is separable (or local)”
cannot both be maintained in light of current knowledge. They
cannot both be true. Another significant point also remains: even
if we avoid the stronger ontological assumption that reality
exists independently, adopting Einstein’s cautious programmatic
stance, we can still assert a result Einstein would not have
liked. Specifically, the conditions (a) and (b) above — ultimate
recourse to spatiotemporal ideas and the unrestricted
appropriateness of certain counterfactuals — cannot be maintained
in full generality. Consequently, we have no rational basis for
moving from the modest Reality Criterion 1 (Section 9.2) to the
stronger Reality Criterion 2. The rejection of these conditions
is a philosophically meaningful new piece of information that
physics provides.

Overall, then, contemporary physics teaches, with some
independence from contentious metaphysical options, that there is
no “real, factual, noncontextual situation” of a localized
microscopic system. Thus, if one still proposes, even as
a program, to describe a world strictly independent of human
observational capabilities, that world must be nonseparable (or
“nonlocal”) and therefore must differ radically from the ordinary
notion conveyed by the word “world.”

### 16.2 On the Concept of Influence at a Distance

It is worthwhile to scrutinize the validity of the notion of
influence at a distance. When we hear that local causality is
violated, our first instinct may be to imagine some influence
that travels across space without decaying with distance and that
violates the relativistic velocity bound. But since neither
energy nor information can be transmitted by such means
(parameter independence; see Section 8.2), the issue is more
delicate. Some physicists have argued that nonseparability is not
essentially new because classical physics also admits
“superluminal” effects that carry neither energy nor information,
such as the lighthouse effect discussed in Section 9.3, and they
claim this shows nonlocality is not novel. We saw, however, that
this argument is flawed: such classical effects are not the same
as quantum nonlocality, which remains a new and essential feature
of any ontologically interpretable description of independent
reality.

Whether influences at a distance “really exist” is ultimately
a question of epistemology and semantics rather than physics.
Nonlocality introduces new conceptual content that partly
resembles familiar notions of cause and influence, but in other
respects differs from them. If one accepts an ontologically
interpretable theory that embeds independent reality in space and
time (or space-time), as in the theories surveyed in Chapter 13,
one is led to stress the similarities and conclude that the
influences are real. If, however, one follows Kant in restricting
efficient causality to empirical reality, or adopts the view
suggested in Section 14.6 that independent reality is not
embedded in space-time (or both), then it makes no sense to
ascribe influences at a distance to independent reality.

A related question is whether influences at a distance can be
said to exist within empirical reality. Their existence cannot be
inferred from the violation of local causality or
nonseparability, since those concern independent reality. Two
suggestions have been made that try to define a sense of
“influence at a distance” that could apply to empirical reality.

#### Suggestion 1 (Stapp; Eberhard)

Some proposals derive Bell inequalities without explicit
reference to independent reality. Consider two space-like
separated space-time regions R_u and R_v where yes-no
measurements are made. Suppose in R_u one could perform either
measurement M_u or M_u' (of observables A_u or A_u'), and
similarly in R_v one could perform M_v or M_v' (of A_v or A_v').
The key counterfactual premise is this: if M_u were performed in
R_u and M_v in R_v and the outcome “yes” occurred in R_u, then
the same “yes” outcome in R_u would have occurred even if,
instead of M_v, the alternative measurement M_v' had been
performed in R_v. Together with the symmetric statement
(exchanging u and v), this assumption yields Bell inequalities,
so observed violations of those inequalities invalidate the
premise. From this it is argued that some superluminal influence
between R_u and R_v must exist. But the highly counterfactual
premise is itself ambiguous: critics have argued it may covertly
presuppose determinism or refer implicitly to independent
reality. There has been an intricate debate about this, which
cannot be fully reviewed here.

#### Suggestion 2 (d’Espagnat)

Within the empirical‑reality approach, one cannot exhaustively
enumerate the facts (hidden parameters or otherwise) that could
serve as causes. As noted in Section 15.5, this makes local
causality irrelevant to empirical reality. More generally,
a direct causal link between two repeatable events cannot be
established solely from observed correlations, because the
correlation might be due to unknown common causes. Accordingly,
in empirical reality a causal link between events A and B must be
defined in terms of human action possibilities. One such
“entailment” definition runs: if A and B are repeatable events,
A precedes B, and A is of a type we can make occur at will (or
imagine making occur), then A causes B if and only if whenever we
make A happen then B happens, and whenever we do not make
A happen, B does not occur.

An essential element of this definition is that A must be
something we can bring about at will. This human-oriented
reference is necessary for the reason just stated. But it raises
nontrivial questions in quantum examples such as Bohm’s two-spin
case: can a spin measurement outcome on one particle be said to
“cause” the corresponding component of the other particle? We can
choose to set an apparatus to measure a spin component on the
first particle, but we cannot choose the measurement outcome.
Thus, if we take event A to be the first particle’s outcome
having a specific value, A is not something we can make happen at
will. The only event we can make happen at will is that the
measuring apparatus interacts with the first particle (we can
decide to perform that measurement), and the outcome then
appears. Call that event A. Because of the strict correlation in
Bohm’s example, the second particle’s spin along the same axis
must then have a definite value, which one could call event B.
But is “B” really an event, or is the proposition “B” a tautology
because only those two correlated values can occur? Formally, the
structure of the proposition “B” mirrors the modal proposition
Q in Eq. (143) (see Section 14.10), where the roles of P and ¬P
are played by “S_u = +1” and “S_v = −1,” and the role of D is
played by “a Stern–Gerlach apparatus oriented along direction
n interacts with particle v.” As discussed in Section 14.10, such
modal propositions are not tautologies in general. However, here
the propositions “S_u = +1” and “S_v = −1” are only trustworthy
rather than strictly true or false, and lacking a formal modal
logic for trustworthy propositions, one cannot conclusively
assert the nontautological character of the modal claim. Thus
Suggestion 2 is also inconclusive.

The existence of these two suggestions shows that one may define
a notion of “influences at a distance” that would make empirical
reality not entirely devoid of such influences. But the
indication is weak, and such theoretical moves are of limited
scientific and epistemological impact: parameter independence
ensures they cannot transmit information and thus are constrained
to semantic or interpretational roles.

### 16.3 Hints from Quantum Physics

In Section 16.1 we isolated epistemological information that
someone who wishes to avoid metaphysical commitments can
reasonably extract from quantum physics. That viewpoint is useful
for distinguishing genuinely scientific questions from mixed
philosophical ones, but it should not be elevated into the only
permissible stance. As argued in Chapter 14, there are sound
philosophical reasons, some indirectly linked with science, to
regard a notion of reality independent of our minds as meaningful
in itself. If one accepts that, quantum physics suggests
additional points about the relation between knowledge and that
reality. These points are less than firm conclusions and
therefore we call them “hints.” They are the subject of the next
sections.

### 16.4 Hint 1: Extended Causality

Some arguments in Chapter 14 against radical idealists’ rejection
of independent reality rely on physics, so physics indirectly
supports the idea that independent reality is a meaningful
concept. Here I emphasize one particular argument that arises
from contrasting radical idealists with “physical ontologists”
(Bohm, Bell, and others) on explaining intersubjective agreement
about contingent facts.

---[chunk-boundary]---

As we saw in Chapter 1, most of the earlier discussions either
ignore the problem or dismiss it as an unwarranted "why"
question. That kind of response is not fully satisfactory. The
quantum-mechanical formalism is somewhat more informative: it
does predict intersubjective agreement (either by appeal to
collapse or to the correlations encoded in the total wave
function), although that still amounts to explanation by
reference to general laws (see Section 15.2). Ontological
theories reviewed in Chapter 13 go further in the usual,
common-sense sense of providing explanations. Without committing
to any single ontological theory, we can take all these
considerations together as indicating that the notion of an
independent reality is not wholly meaningless.

If we accept this physics-based indication together with the
philosophical arguments in favor of independent reality (a
position I will adopt from now on), then Kant’s qualification of
causality as merely a human way of organizing sensory phenomena
becomes too restrictive. Beyond the causality that underlies
empirical reality (Sections 15.2–15.6) — a notion we should not
underestimate — we must allow that another kind of causality is
meaningful: one that operates from independent reality to
phenomena. Because nonseparability prevents independent reality
from being conceived as composed of localized elements embedded
in space-time, this "extended causality" differs markedly from
Kantian causality (essentially determinism) and from Einsteinian
causality. It does not fit Aristotle's notion of efficient
causes, but it does have room for structural causes. In this
approach these structural "extended causes" are not mere lawlike
regularities among observed phenomena; they are the very
structures of independent reality and thus provide the ultimate
explanation for the existence of the laws we call physics.

Leibniz held that all contingent facts are governed by the
Principle of Sufficient Reason, which he formulated roughly as
follows: no fact is true or existent, no judgment veritable,
unless there is a sufficient reason why it should be so and not
otherwise, even though these reasons often cannot be known to us
(Monadology). He also put it: "Nothing happens without
a sufficient reason; that is, nothing happens without its being
possible for one who should know things sufficiently to give
a reason showing why things are so and not otherwise" (Principles
of Nature and Grace). Today, among physicists, only a small group
— advocates of ontological, deterministic theories like the
pilot-wave theory — would endorse this principle without
reservations. Still, from what has been reviewed in this book,
some spirit of Leibniz's principle seems worth preserving. Not
determinism, certainly, and not Leibniz's suggestion that
a sufficiently knowledgeable human could in principle know all
reasons. What can be retained is the core idea that there is some
ultimate reason for at least the laws that govern the world. To
make this precise, I propose the following basic postulate.

Basic postulate. Any observed regularity (statistical or
otherwise) must have a cause (or set of causes — the unity of the
cause is not emphasized here), where (i) the cause may or may not
be located in time, and (ii) the cause may or may not be
discoverable by humans.

Point (i) implies, in particular, that the general causes of an
event are not necessarily located in its past. Under this
postulate, the claim that efficient causality is scientific while
final causality is not is meaningful only within empirical
reality. Point (ii) extends the notion of cause beyond the
narrower notion of explanation, at least beyond the two senses of
explanation discussed in Section 14.8: (a) reduction to the
known, and (b) grouping under a general law. Here it is not
meaningless to speak of causes even if they are of such a nature
that humans could conceivably never discover them. The "extended
causes" I envisage are taken to be prior to laws. (Adopting this
option means discarding the operationalist rule that the meaning
attached to a concept should not be broader than the totality of
its empirical references; I have argued elsewhere when that rule
is overly restrictive.) We should remember the simple fact that
the number of brain connections is huge but finite, and therefore
it is not a logical truth that mind can discover everything that
exists and has observable effects.

Remark 1.

Many philosophers inherit from Kant the principle that cause and
effect must be of the same nature; therefore, if effects are
phenomena, causes must be phenomena as well. Within that
conception, the basic postulate above and the notion of extended
causality are meaningless — attempts to find the cause of
causality would be a vicious circle. Kant's view did help to
separate science from metaphysics and to free science from many
false problems, and that historical contribution is indisputable.
The ideas proposed here are not a call to reintroduce metaphysics
into empirical scientific practice. Rather, some findings of
science now press at the door of metaphysics, not to borrow
elements to help empirical research, but to bring metaphysics new
information that it might use. With that clarification, the
Kantian restriction of causality to phenomena should be seen as
a methodological principle, highly useful within scientific
activity, but not a logical truth one must impose on all human
reason. Accepting Kant's claim that space and time are not arenas
containing independent reality does not force us to accept his
limitation of causality to phenomena.

Remark 2.

The notion of extended causes (which theologians might call
"primary causes") must be handled carefully. A classic objection
is that it leads to infinite regress, illustrated by the fable of
the earth on an elephant, the elephant on a tortoise, and so on.
This tale rightly warns that naive quests for causes deserve
scrutiny. But it is not decisive: Galileo was right that the
preservation of rectilinear uniform motion requires no cause.
Likewise, one can counter the fable by holding that the general
structures of independent reality — which I claim are the causes
of objective regularities — do not themselves require causes.

Remark 3.

The idea of extended causality is implicit in many thinkers’
conceptions, even among some who reject traditional realism. For
example, Poincaré’s two claims — (a) that we cannot know the
objects themselves, and (b) that we do know the relations between
them — naturally lead to the idea that something causes our
knowledge and that this "something" is not merely a Kant-like
phenomenon. (This does not imply endorsement of all of Poincaré’s
views; I discussed similarities and differences in Sections 14.8
and 14.12.)

Remark 4.

The notion of extended causality connects to the question of how
far science, despite its lack of definite knowledge of
independent reality, brings us closer to it. Philosophers
customarily distinguish two levels within what we have called
"empirical reality": the commonsense level and the scientific
level. The limits of our sense organs, and their dependence on
intermediaries such as light and sound waves, are long known;
scientists are increasingly aware that these factors partly make
our sense-data creations of the observer. If we add that the
interface between perceptual signals and the mind is poorly
known, there is no reason to expect that our commonsense
representations resemble independent reality. Science is an
attempt to correct for these human idiosyncrasies. I agree with
Shimony that science should be seen as a sophisticated,
self-critical, partly successful enterprise aiming to understand
in detail the connection between commonsense experience and
ultimate reality. The word "successful" refers to the fact that
independent reality, especially its general structure, is not
totally unknowable; "partly" expresses that what we reach
concerning contingent facts is empirical reality. Extended
causality is essential for this picture. Accepting extended
causality does not imply we can, in general, trace back from
effects to causes; the reasons from physics making that unlikely
were explained in Chapter 14.

### 16.5 Hint 2: Space-O and Time-O

Within the veiled-reality conception, Einsteinian separability
applies to empirical, not independent, reality; special
relativity therefore does not directly concern independent
reality. In other words (as stated in Section 14.6), independent
reality is not in space-time, and Einsteinian causality does not
apply to it. Difficulties reconciling ontological theories with
special relativity led some authors to revert to Newtonian
absolute space and universal time, but most physicists find that
unacceptable on the grounds that ontological interpretability
alone does not justify such a regression. The veiled-reality view
agrees: independent reality is not embedded in space or time. We
can invoke Bonsack's concept of a world-O (Section 15.1) and
introduce the notions of a space-O and a time-O. Applying the
essentials of Bonsack's analysis leads to the view that, although
space and time do not derive from the subject's existence-O, they
are empirical in character — a point Kant had intuited long ago.

Schrödinger, in Mind and Matter, argued that regarding time
Einstein's special relativity confirmed Kant’s intuition: "the
whole time table is not quite as serious as it appears at first
sight." That is true, but in classical special relativity one
could still view Einsteinian causality as a feature of
independent reality, defining for each event an absolute future
and past. With the discovery of nonseparability and the Bell
theorems showing no ontological escape from it, it has become
clear that Einsteinian causality cannot be a feature of
independent reality; hence the idea of an independently existing
"timetable" is even shakier. Schrödinger's remark — that what we
construct in our mind cannot have dictatorial power over our mind
— captures this intellectual liberation from "the tyranny of old
Chronos."

Remark 1.

One might object that the astrophysicist's notion of cosmic time
challenges this view. General relativity and special relativity
differ, so one could conjecture that cosmic time is more than
a time-O: perhaps a Newtonian-like time lifted up to cosmology,
an element of independent reality. That would suggest science has
lifted the veil on at least one element of independent reality.
There are objections. Cosmic time is defined only within the
approximation of the universe being homogeneous and isotropic
— the cosmological principle — and it seems problematic to base
a fundamental notion on an approximation of observational data.
The universe does appear very isotropic, so this objection is not
decisive now. Still, general relativity admits "exotic"
cosmological solutions (for example Gödel's), where causality can
disappear. Previously that was criticized as unacceptable because
causality underpins physics; with nonseparability and the
recognition that usual definitions of causality (including
Einsteinian separability) do not apply to independent reality,
that criticism loses force. Overall, the idea that the standard
cosmological model and cosmic time apply to independent reality
remains speculative. It is perhaps significant that in at least
one ontologically interpretable model, Bohm’s, the Big Bang
appears as a "small ripple."

Remark 2.

There are more detailed proposals concerning the idealization of
time. Grib, in connection with quantum-logic approaches, proposed
one such idea. I have not described quantum logic here (I treated
it elsewhere and showed it fails as a means to restore a strongly
objective reality in space-time obeying Einsteinian
separability), but Grib’s conception differs. He proposes that
independent reality is composed of truly existing quantum objects
whose properties correspond to propositions obeying a non-Boolean
logic; these propositions do not have truth values by themselves.
Human observers are Boolean and therefore impart truth values
according to Boolean logic, effectively translating non-Boolean
quantum logic into their human language. In this translation the
observer unavoidably encounters "paradoxes" linked to
nondistributivity in the quantum lattice contrasted with the
distributivity of Boolean logic. Grib argues that the notion of
time enters here: the observer "invents" time to remove these
paradoxes. For instance, in a nondistributive quantum lattice
concerning propositions a, b, c, the three might be mutually
exclusive so that neither a ^ b, nor a ^ c, nor b ^ c can be true
— therefore P1 = (a ^ b) v (a ^ c) cannot be true — yet
b v c = I (where ^ means "and", v means "or", and I means "always
true"). For a Boolean-minded person this looks contradictory: if
a is true then P2 = a ^ (b v c) is true, and by distributivity P2
= P1. The observer resolves the paradox by invoking time: at some
time t1 they hold a to be true, and at a later time t2 — if they
measure b, say — they may see b become true and consider that
a is no longer true at t2. Thus both the notion of time and the
wave-packet reduction arise as consequences of the translation
from non-Boolean quantum logic to Boolean human logic.

### 16.6 Hint 3: Independent Reality, Empirical Reality, Potentialities, and Consciousness

In his later years Heisenberg developed a worldview in which the
notion of potentia plays a central role, and he associated wave
functions with potentialities. Heisenberg’s approach is
ontological in a subtle sense: alongside actuality it leaves room
for potentialities. He suggested that if one wants to speak about
things-in-themselves, the natural language is the quantum state,
viewed as a synthesis of actualities and potentialities.

Stated so generally, this view seems likely to contain much
truth, but Heisenberg did not precisely define the difference
between "actual" and "potential." Until that difference is
defined more sharply, explanations that rely on potentiality must
remain vague, and the transition from potential to actual remains
somewhat mysterious.

Within the conceptions developed in this book, the notion of
potentiality also arises and can be given a somewhat clearer
meaning. If one accepts independent reality and allows it to be
in principle describable by physics, then potential properties of
objects — distinct from the "actual" or ontological properties
with the same names — must be considered real. These
potentialities correspond to what one might call the system’s
"acquired aptitudes": the system’s propensity to yield certain
measurement results if the appropriate measurement is made. For
example, in the standard Bohm example: if s is measured at time
t1 and found equal to +1/2, the formal operation of wave-packet
reduction that gives V a definite spin ket (the eigenket for the
eigenvalue Sy = −1/2) corresponds to V acquiring the potentiality
of producing, with unit probability, the result −1/2 on
a z-oriented Stern–Gerlach device at any later time t > t1 that
it interacts with.

Grib’s proposal (summarized above) gives another way to
distinguish potential and actual: it identifies potentialities
with independent reality and actualities with empirical reality.
In that approach quantum objects and their properties exist as
potentialities (obeying non-Boolean logic) and constitute
independent reality; observed facts are elements of empirical
reality and thereby have actuality. The transition from potential
to actual is not, in this view, a physical phenomenon in the
usual ontological sense but instead emerges from our human modes
of looking at things. The price of this clarification is that
consciousness must be given an essential role.

That immediately raises the question highlighted by Wigner’s
friend paradox: there are many human consciousnesses (not to
mention animals). Schrödinger called this the "arithmetical
paradox" and attempted to avoid it by asserting that the
multiplicity of minds is only an appearance hiding the essential
oneness of an eternal Mind. Another way to picture the issue is
by modifying the physicalist "rope-ladder" metaphor. In the
traditional physicalist sketch, the lowest rung is elementary
particles, the next rung atoms and molecules, then macroscopic
systems like stones and brains, and finally mind and thought at
the top, each emerging from the previous rung but displaying
qualitatively new properties. The curved rope-ladder approach
suggested by contemporary physics differs in one decisive
respect: take the ladder, bend it, and join its two ends. The
picture then still shows atoms emerging from elementary
particles, macroscopic bodies from atoms, and mind from brain,
but it also shows elementary particles as emerging from thought
and so on. This "closing the circle" idea — similar in spirit to
Wheeler’s "it from bit" — makes neither mind prior to matter nor
matter prior to mind; they "generate each other" in a sense. But
it does not resolve the arithmetical paradox.

The curved rope-ladder is a natural consequence of the
veiled-reality conception, since there empirical reality and
consciousness "generate" one another — albeit timelessly — within
independent reality; the reverse need not be true. Wheeler’s
theory, for example, makes no explicit use of an
independent-reality notion and would likely regard it as
superfluous; Schrödinger rejected Kant’s "things-in-themselves"
as unnecessary duplication. But nonseparability restores to the
independent-reality notion an element of holism absent from
empirical reality, weakening Schrödinger’s objection. More
generally, arguments discussed in Chapter 14 indicate that the
notion of independent reality is indispensable. The question then
is: within the veiled-reality approach, does the arithmetical
paradox remain?

The answer is yes, but only in a qualified sense. The
arithmetical problem persists within the veiled-reality approach,
though it is not as acute as in Schrödinger’s formulation. If we
could accept time as an arena in which events-in-themselves take
place (i.e., set independent reality in time), the multiplicity
of minds would not present an insurmountable problem. As noted
earlier, all ontological theories can explain intersubjective
agreement among multiple minds. In the Bell-like version of the
pilot-wave theory, that agreement can be obtained simply by
enlarging configuration space: add as many dimensions as there
are individual consciousnesses. The representative point then
specifies, at any given time, not only the material state of the
world but also the states of individual consciousnesses of all
conscious beings. A peculiarity of this model is that, because of
contextuality (see Chapter 13), the content of one consciousness
generated by measuring some quantity may depend on whether
another compatible or incompatible measurement is performed
elsewhere, by someone else, or on details of how the measurement
is performed (order, joint measurements, etc.).

To illustrate this quantitatively, reconsider Bell’s detailed
description of how the pilot-wave theory accounts for strict
correlations in the standard Bohm two-spin example (Sections 13.3
and 15.3). There, the states of consciousness of two observers
are associated with two new variables x3 and x4, so the
configuration space of the whole system is four-dimensional. Both
x3 and x4 can take three values: 0 (nothing registered), +1
(consciousness of having seen S2 = +1), and −1 (consciousness of
having seen S2 = −1). Let t1 and t2 be the two measurement times
considered in Section 15.3. The consciousness variables are
defined by thresholds: for observer 3, for example,

x3 = 0 if −ε < x1 < ε, x3 = +1 if x1 > ε, x3 = −1 if x1 < −ε,

where ε is a small positive number, and analogous relations hold
for x4 in relation to x2. Now consider the situation where at
t = 0 both x1 and x2 are small positive numbers: 0 < x1, x2 < ε.

Case (i). t1 < t2. Then, between t1 and t2, the first measurement
at t1 drives x1 above ε so that x3 = +1, while x2 remains in (0,
ε) so x4 = 0. After t2, because of the nonlocal contextuality
encoded in the theory (see Eq. (13.20)), x2 is driven below −ε
and thus x4 = −1; x1 remains > ε so x3 = +1.

If t₂ < t₁, then between t₂ and t₁ we have x₂ > ε, hence x₄ = 1.
If 0 < x₁ < ε, then x₃ = 0. After t₁ we again have x₂ > ε, so x₄
= 1, while if x₁ < −ε then x₃ = −1. In both cases the two
observers become aware of opposite S₂ values for the spins.
However, it is not the same observer who is in the +1 awareness
state in the two cases. This remains true even though, if the two
measurement events are spacelike separated, cases (i) and (ii)
may well be the same physical process seen in two different
reference frames. In this respect the situation closely parallels
the one analyzed elsewhere, except that here we are dealing with
a deterministic theory that makes definite ontological claims.

Note also that the foregoing relations have consequences that do
not involve relativity. They show that after time t₂ the observer
A4, whose consciousness state is X, will be in an awareness state
+1 if nothing happened between times 0 and t₂, but will be in
awareness state −1 if, during that interval, someone else—perhaps
without A4 being informed—measured S(1) at some other, possibly
distant, location. Consequently, although intersubjective
agreement is guaranteed, the partial knowledge we acquire through
a given measurement or observation cannot, in general, be treated
simply as an element to be added to other such elements to yield
a genuine knowledge of the “outside world as it really is,”
including its contingent features.

A similar, and even more radical, conclusion follows from the
interpretation I proposed of the relative-state theory. The
difference between the two descriptions is this: in the first
interpretation the partial knowledge just mentioned is genuine
knowledge about an element of physical reality (the sign of x₄
corresponds to the sign of x₂), whereas in the relative-state
interpretation it is, in that respect, an illusion. Yet in
neither theory does the multiplicity of consciousnesses, nor the
correlations among their impressions, generate consistency
problems. Because these theories are “realist” models that take
the notion of an independent reality seriously and even provide
definite mathematical descriptions of its general structure,
Schrödinger’s “arithmetical paradox” does not arise in them.

These two theories share at least two important features. First,
although they are conceptually quite different, they cannot be
distinguished experimentally from each other (nor, in fact, from
other nonlocal theories). Second, as already noted, both require
that independent reality be embedded in time. The first point
suggests that they are better regarded as metaphysical than
strictly physical theories. The second point may be explained by
the fact that the notion of time is so deeply ingrained in our
thinking that none of the elaborate, quantitative metaphysical
systems humans devise can completely dispense with it.

Overall, these considerations reinforce the view that we should
be modest when speaking about independent reality. They support,
to some extent, the previously introduced notion of a veiled
reality: a reality we apprehend in space-time but that is not
itself immersed in space or time (or in space-time). Given the
substantial obstacles to making the notion more precise, the
vagueness of the idea that mind and empirical reality co-emerge
atemporally within independent reality should not be counted
against it; if anything, that vagueness argues in its favor.
Admittedly, if independent reality is not immersed in time, it
becomes difficult to avoid Schrödinger’s somewhat disconcerting
“oneness of Mind” hypothesis.

As a final remark, one of Schrödinger’s general observations
remains valid within the veiled-reality conception.
Metaphorically, the relation between the nonpersonal Mind and
individual minds can be compared to that between a hologram and
its parts: the nonpersonal Mind reappears in its entirety within
each individual mind. The mutual generation of mind and empirical
reality within a nontemporal independent reality implies that
time should be regarded merely as an element of empirical
reality, and therefore that mind creates time just as it creates
empirical reality. The power of old Chronos may well be, to
borrow Schrödinger’s image, only an intersubjective appearance.
(For a more elaborate and balanced discussion of this view, see
Section 16.5.)

### 16.7 Spinoza Revisited

Independent Reality is structured in ways we cannot actually know
and, as explained above, these structures give rise—via extended
causality—to the ordinary, observed cause-and-effect
relationships. There is no a priori reason why these same
structures should not also give rise to some form of `teleology`.
In this sense, Independent Reality plays a role analogous to
Spinoza’s God, or “Substance,” although there are differences.

To emphasize the similarities: both Independent Reality (here
capitalized) and Spinoza’s God are markedly distinct from the set
of material objects, forces, and all the things we perceive
through our senses and instruments. Thus the common claim that
Spinoza by “God” merely meant “nature” is misleading, because
today “nature” usually denotes material objects and forces;
labeling Spinoza a pantheist therefore misses the subtlety of his
view. Spinoza regarded extension and thought as basic attributes
of God on an equal footing, and this parallels the coemergence of
mind and empirical reality within and from Independent Reality
described above.

There are, however, important differences. The main one concerns
intelligibility. As Einstein interpreted him, Spinoza’s God—or
Substance—is fully intelligible: for Einstein, a reality that
cannot be understood would be meaningless. In contrast,
Independent Reality as described here is not, or at least not
entirely, intelligible—a view closer to Bonsack’s “World-2.” It
is worth noting, though, that this difference lies more between
Independent Reality and Einstein’s interpretation of Spinoza's
God, rather than between Independent Reality and Spinoza's own
conception. Spinoza actually held that God possesses infinitely
many attributes, most of which are beyond our capacity to know,
so his original philosophy already included aspects of God that
surpass human understanding.
