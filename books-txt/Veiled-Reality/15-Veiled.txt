The developments in the previous chapter support the view that
independent reality is veiled. In the first section of this
chapter I compare this conception with ideas recently developed
in the philosophy of science on purely philosophical grounds. In
the following sections the notion of empirical reality, as used
here, is made more precise.

### 15.1 Philosophical Developments; Bonsack's Approach

It is interesting that ideas resembling the veiled-reality
concept were developed independently by philosophers of science
who did not base their ideas on detailed physical knowledge.
I cannot give a balanced account of these subtle and often
sophisticated theories here; I will mention only two or three
examples and concentrate on one of them.

Two noteworthy examples are Bas van Fraassen’s Constructive
Empiricism and Hilary Putnam’s Internal Realism. Both are so
elaborate that describing either in any depth would require
developments beyond the scope of this book; here I will only give
a brief glimpse of each.

One major claim of Constructive Empiricism is that "the aim of
science is not truth as such but only empirical adequacy,"
meaning truth only with respect to observable phenomena [1].
Taken in isolation, that statement might suggest that
constructive empiricism is a form of the "pure phenomenism"
discussed in Chapter 1, but that would oversimplify van
Fraassen’s nuanced position. When applied to specific problems,
his standpoint is not equivalent to pure phenomenism. Thus there
are some affinities between constructive empiricism and the
veiled-reality concept, but significant differences remain.

Putnam’s Internal Realism is similar in this respect. Putnam
rejects the correspondence theory of truth — the idea that
concepts must map uniquely onto a mind-independent world
— arguing that many such mappings are conceivable a priori and
that to select one would already require referential access to
mind-independent things [2, p. 72]. Yet Putnam insists that
internal realism is not facile relativism: "Knowledge," he
writes, "is not a story with no constraints except internal
coherence." What he rejects in conventional realism is
essentially the view that there are inputs to knowledge that are
not themselves shaped by our concepts or vocabulary, or inputs
that admit only one description independent of conceptual choices
[2, p. 54]. At one point he even allows that "perhaps Kant is
right: perhaps we can't help thinking that there is somehow
a mind-independent 'ground' for our experience," which again
shows some resemblance to the veiled-reality idea. Still, Putnam
immediately adds that attempts to talk about this
mind-independent ground may "lead at once to nonsense," a remark
that departs in spirit from the approach of the previous chapter.

Neither van Fraassen nor Putnam (as sketched above) would
classify themselves straightforwardly as realists (Putnam was
earlier a realist but later changed his view). Because most
physicists tend toward realism and many readers of this book are
physicists, it seems useful to discuss in more detail the
affinities between the veiled-reality concept and the views of
a philosopher who explicitly considers himself, broadly speaking,
a realist. For this purpose I analyze François Bonsack’s approach
to realism [3].

Bonsack begins from the observation that many philosophers admit
that idealist arguments are hard to refute, and so feel forced to
treat our inclination to believe in an external world as, after
all, a delusion. Those among them who are sympathetic to realism
often think realism is a kind of instinct held despite
philosophical arguments to the contrary. Bonsack opposes this
attitude: either realism is correct and must be defensible
rationally, or it is wrong and holding it by instinct is a poor
excuse. Similar to analyses presented earlier in this book,
Bonsack searches for a ground that would make a realist
epistemology philosophically defensible.

Avoiding metaphysical premises, Bonsack begins with the only
secure data we have: the uninterpreted “given to consciousness.”
He observes that taking this as a starting point can lead first
to naïve realism (things are exactly as we perceive them) and,
once naive realism is transcended, to a kind of first-step
idealism: confinement to what is accessible to consciousness. He
accepts that this is a defensible option but insists that “the
job isn’t done so far.” One must still explain how, starting from
the conscious given, a subject comes to postulate a real world
and to distinguish data that refer to that world from data
(dreams, etc.) that do not. As Bonsack writes, “Even if
everything goes through representation, the distinctions between
an object and its representation and between external world and
internal subjectivity have to be restored within this
representation.” For a philosophically minded scientist, the
originality of Bonsack’s approach is that he explicitly states
this problem and studies it rationally.

Bonsack stresses that the sensory given has structure: the
different perspectives we obtain on an object are connected in
computable ways to one another and to our actions. He argues that
the aim of knowledge is to give an account of this structure;
quoting Poincaré, he notes that “objects are not only clusters of
sensations, but clusters cemented together by a persistent bond.
It is this bond and only this bond that is object in them, and
this bond is a relation (F.B. — a net of relations).” Bonsack
calls the level of sensory and motor flows and their
interconnections level-S (S for subjective or subject). This is
the phenomenological level; at it there is neither world nor
space, and therefore neither inside nor outside.

Trying to account for the structure by grasping laws directly
linking sensations and actions would be practically impracticable
(for example, trying to connect directly the muscle commands for
walking with the resulting changes in appearances, without using
concepts such as space and location). By contrast, the task
becomes manageable if, starting from perceptions, we construct
three-dimensional objects, locate them in space, and so on. In
other words, to account for the structure in question we are
practically forced to "go through the instrumentality of building
an objective world—world-O—structured in space and time and
furnished with objects."

Although Bonsack is essentially a realist, he does not invoke the
apparently obvious argument that the efficiency of this procedure
shows it must be a genuine discovery of how the world really is.
Philosophers historically have not treated this argument
seriously; in the age of quantum physics, with its difficulties
concerning localization and objects, the argument sounds less
convincing. For the sake of argument Bonsack, like other
philosophers, does not rely on it. Nevertheless he emphasizes
points that make his scheme somewhat close to conventional
realism. One is that his world-O includes the subject: not the
level-S subject itself but an objectified subject-O, which is an
object interacting with other objects and whose sensations and
experiences are interpreted by referring to the physical laws of
world-O. Moreover, the subject-O must be objectified including
his subjectivity, so that the relation of consciousness and the
external world does not lie outside ordinary causality.

In Bonsack’s model, objects-O must, for consistency, have an
existence-O that does not depend on the existence-O of the
subject-O and of that subject-O’s knowledge. This satisfies
a main condition of classical realism, and Bonsack rightly
stresses it. Yet he recognizes that traditional realists may
object: they may say that if this development happens so readily
it is a deception, for world-O is merely a representation and the
model thus lapses into idealism.

Bonsack responds carefully. He admits that, at this stage, he has
given a sense to realism within an idealist framework, but he
argues this is already significant because many idealists stop
halfway and fail to reconnect to the real. In his approach the
duality between appearance and reality is preserved, as is the
distinction between representation and what is represented. He
even claims that, unlike idealism, his scheme has a kind of
heterogeneity that gives sense to error: although its origin is
not directly accessible, world-O is not its own measure. The
sensations-O that result from objectifying the actual sensations
S can be compared with the sensations-O predicted by the
postulated world-O using the relevant scientific laws-O;
discrepancies are conceivable and should be corrected.

Bonsack admits two serious objections against his scheme. First,
the subject’s representation of the world—his world-O—is clearly
incomplete and relative to what he knows. Second, if we ask what
is represented, the classical difficulties of realism reappear.
Bonsack takes these objections seriously and seeks a reply.

He sketches two possible responses. One, briefly mentioned, is to
remain with a positive and reachable aim but add to world-O
a metaprinciple saying that world-O is incomplete and provisional
and must be modified by experience. He does not criticize this
option but treats it cursorily. The other response is to "go some
steps towards metaphysics," but to do so cautiously and with
rational justification. This second route leads him to postulate,
beyond our individual and historical worlds-O, the existence of
a kind of limit-world that he calls world-. World- is not
substantially different from our worlds-O nor in principle
inaccessible; some worlds-O might coincide with it at certain
points, but we do not know which ones or where. We have direct
access only to our worlds-O, so we cannot assess the adequacy of
our worlds-O by comparing them with world-. World- is postulated
merely as an ideal toward which we aim. Crucially for the
realist, Bonsack specifies that world- is not a representation:
it is genuinely independent of the individual subjects’
knowledge. It is an ontological whole, and because of that any
detailed discussion of its nature is “vain and metaphysical”; the
only verifiable aspect of it is the "net of relations which links
its events" (again showing Poincaré’s influence).

Bonsack’s scheme, developed from purely philosophical
considerations, is strikingly similar to the picture that
a detailed analysis of contemporary physics led to earlier in
this book: basic reality is neither a "pure X" nor something
completely and scientifically knowable. Above this was expressed
by saying that basic reality is veiled. Some philosophers might
find the term "veiled" vague; Bonsack’s more elaborate scheme can
serve as a first approximation to make that notion precise. The
correspondence is not exact, but it is close enough to be
significant. In the language used here, Bonsack’s world-
corresponds to our "independent reality," while his worlds-O
correspond to what we call "empirical" or "effective" reality.
Bonsack’s statement that "it is possible that, on certain points,
some worlds-O coincide with world- but we neither know which ones
nor where" can be read as capturing the idea, used in this book,
that independent reality is veiled. The philosophical and
physical approaches differ in origin: the philosopher begins by
doubting that sense data and science correspond to independent
reality and then narrows his doubts; the physicist begins more
optimistically about science’s ability to lift the veil but is
led by analysis to moderate that optimism. Consequently, the
physicist may be willing to conjecture that the general
structures of independent reality are well approached by physics
(as argued in Section 10.8), while the more contingent features
of phenomena are shaped by us; the philosopher has less reason to
distinguish these layers. Still, compared to the similarities,
this difference is not essential.

It is worth noting that, according to the present analysis, the
beliefs least likely to correspond to elements of independent
reality are our beliefs about contingent features we observe and
discuss in everyday life and in the laboratory. This is the
opposite of common sense, which typically treats contingent facts
as having reality per se and questions our ability to know the
basic structural features.

### 15.2 Analysis of the "Empirical Reality" Approach

The foregoing shows that critical epistemology and quantum
physics converge. Epistemology has soundly questioned the idea
that the things we encounter in daily and scientific life exist
per se, occupy a space and time that exist per se, and are fully
knowable by physics. It has shown that this idea, far from being
an obvious truth, is a mere hypothesis. Quantum physics adds the
further information that this hypothesis is most probably false
(I say "most probably," not "surely," because some ontologically
interpretable models do allow objects or events to be conceived
as existing per se; Chapter 13 explained why none of those models
can be established as scientifically definitive). This supports
the view that what physics can realistically describe is not
Reality-per-se in all its details, but primarily the phenomena as
they manifest to the human community.

This veiled-reality view is, in basic status, a realist theory
(an "open realism"), since it treats the notion of an
independently existing reality (Bonsack’s world-) as meaningful.
But according to it, independent reality is not scientifically
knowable as it is, not even in its gross contingent features.
From the definitions in Section 14.5 it follows that, although
notions of strongly objective statements and concepts are
meaningful in this view, they form a highly restricted
set—possibly empty or at best so sparsely populated that they
cannot provide an accurate scientific description of "how the
world is." In particular, this set cannot be expected to include
our detailed scientific statements and concepts about dynamical
properties of systems. Those dynamical descriptions are only
weakly objective: they describe not independent reality itself,
but independent reality as it appears through the forms the mind
imposes on sensory data — what we call "empirical reality."
Realists should carefully note that "empirical reality" is not
reality in their sense; mentally substituting "empirical view"
whenever "empirical reality" appears may help avoid confusion.

Is this shift in referent — treating physics as describing
empirical reality rather than independent reality — sufficient to
resolve the conceptual difficulties analyzed in this book about
"understanding quantum mechanics"? We now address whether this
hope is reasonable.

Such a hope would be absurd if the difficulties were internal
inconsistencies in the basic operational rules of quantum
mechanics. If that were the case, changing the referent from
independent reality to empirical reality would be a mere semantic
move with no effect. But that objection cannot be sustained: the
quantum rules are mutually consistent. The encountered
difficulties — whether about the pointer being "really lying" in
some definite interval, or about the "real factual situation" of
some systems, or other puzzles — all involve the notion of
reality as a referent. Therefore it is plausible that changing
the philosophical nature of that referent could remove or at
least alleviate some difficulties. At the same time, it seems
impossible to do without any notion of physical reality
whatsoever, as some idealists might propose. Quantum rules do not
directly relate sense data and human actions to one another. As
Bonsack also noted, constructing direct connective rules between
raw sense-data and actions would be unrealizable in practice.
When we formulate physics as a set of rules of the game, we still
picture at least the macroscopic apparatus used for preparing
systems and performing measurements as lying in three-dimensional
space, with positions, shapes, and macrostates. Thus, in accounts
of empirical reality the dynamical properties of the macroscopic
systems used as apparatus are described in terms of weakly
objective concepts (see Section 14.7).

With these points in mind, let us examine the various
difficulties we met when trying to interpret quantum mechanics as
a theory of independent reality. We first encountered them in
Chapter 4, where we had to admit that in important cases it is
impossible to treat dynamical properties of quantum systems as
having definite but unknown values. At that stage this looked
like an odd feature of quantum theory rather than a conceptual
deadlock. It became a genuine conceptual difficulty when, in
studying quantum measurement theory, we found that it prevents us
from considering an instrument pointer as generally being in some
definite macroscopic state. This issue was labeled difficulty 4a
and is associated with other conceptual difficulties (1–3 and 4b)
discussed in Section 10.1. We will consider these five
difficulties in order.

Difficulty 1

As recalled earlier, this difficulty is that in the quantum
description of a measurement process the composite system must be
split into two parts, the measured system S and the instrument A,
and it is unclear what physically defines the borderline between
them. Operationally there is no ambiguity: once a measurement
process is fully specified, it is clear what the minimal
extension of the "quantum system" must be — and including
additional parts of the instrument into the system would make no
physical difference (apart from computational complexity). Under
those conditions we are free to choose any allowable extension
and to describe the complementary part of the overall system
using weakly objective concepts. Consequently, within the program
of constructing an empirical view — that is, a description of
empirical reality — difficulty 1 does not arise. In this approach
there is no demand that the distinction between S and A be
a physical one referring to objective structure; instead the
distinction naturally refers to human features, so specifying it
poses no problem.

Difficulty 2

This difficulty concerns the determination of the so-called
"pointer basis" (Sections 10.1 and 10.6). Progress has been made
here: Zurek’s approach to decoherence and the environment has
gone a long way toward resolving this issue. As shown by a simple
model in Section 10.6, the environment theory removes the
ambiguity, provided we assume some prior split of the physical
situation into microscopic system, detector, and environment. The
same solution applies, with no change, to the corresponding
problem in the empirical-reality approach; indeed, it applies
even more cleanly there because the question of the borderline
between instrument and environment does not arise in the same
problematic way.

Difficulty 3

This difficulty concerns the von Neumann chain and the necessity
of choosing a quantum-classical cut, i.e., deciding where to end
the chain. The same argument that removes difficulty 1 also
removes difficulty 3.

Difficulties 4a and 4b

Difficulty 4a, already mentioned in Chapter 4, is a facet of the
Schrödinger cat paradox and can be regarded as the central riddle
of quantum measurement theory and, arguably, of quantum mechanics
as a whole. Difficulty 4b, the "and-or" difficulty, is often
overlooked but is also intriguing. We will analyze each in detail
with the specific aim of determining whether and to what extent
they remain problematic when the focus is on describing empirical
reality.

First, some observations valid for both difficulties. By
definition, empirical reality is the set of phenomena; its
description tells us how things appear to the human community,
not how they are in themselves. This distinction has important
consequences, especially for the notion of a wave function of
a system or an ensemble. Within the veiled-reality perspective,
an Everett-like wave function of the universe can be considered
as a candidate backbone for a conjectural description of
independent reality, perhaps giving some glimpses of it. But the
wave function of an individual atomic system or of an ensemble is
neither a strongly nor a weakly objective concept in the sense
used here. If our purpose is to describe empirical reality (the
set of phenomena), the wave function should be treated as a tool
for predicting outcomes of a given set of prospective
experiments. Viewing it this way allows us, after an observation,
to attribute a reduced wave function (the eigenfunction
corresponding to the outcome) to the system without concern for
"other branches" in Everett’s sense or for systems with which the
measured system previously interacted.

This raises the question of the completeness hypothesis: should
it be kept, and does it still have meaning? The completeness
hypothesis — that there is no finer description of quantum
systems than the state vector (ket) — goes beyond operationality.
It implies that all elements of an ensemble described by a ket
are identical, which is an ontological claim that exceeds what an
empirical description can meaningfully assert. Therefore, when
constructing a description of empirical reality based on quantum
mechanics, we should either drop the completeness assumption
altogether or restrict its meaning to operationally definable
concepts, replacing it by a weak completeness assumption (see
Section 4.2, Remark 3). In that weakened sense, completeness does
not exclude hidden (or "supplementary") variables.

Along the same operationalist lines it is consistent to assume an
additional axiom that makes sense within the empirical-reality
conception — indeed, it is a necessary ingredient (often
implicit) in many of the measurement theories discussed earlier
(the Zeh–Joos and Zurek environment-based theories, the
Gell-Mann–Hartle cosmological theory, the formalism of operations
and effects, etc.).

Axiom of empirical reality [5]. A theoretical systematization of
the empirical view must involve one or both of the following
postulates: (a) replacing very large but finite times by infinite
times, and/or replacing very large particle numbers by infinite
numbers is a valid abstraction; and (b) the possibility of
measuring observables exceeding a certain degree of complexity is
to be considered nonexistent, not even in principle; this
position must be taken even in cases where, according to quantum
mechanics, such measurements are in principle allowed.

With postulate (b), the sensitive observables discussed in
Sections 10.6 and 14.10 can be treated as lacking physical
meaning, so, as explained in Section 1.6, they can be excluded
from the set of observables to which the truth criterion of
Section 1.6 applies.

We now examine whether these two steps — weakening or dropping
the completeness assumption and adopting the axiom of empirical
reality — can help remove difficulties 4a and 4b.

Removing Difficulty 4a

As recalled earlier, difficulty 4a is that the statistical
operator describing the ensemble of the composite S + A systems
after the interaction is a pure state and therefore necessarily
differs from any statistical operator that would describe an
ensemble in which the instrument pointers each have
macroscopically well-defined positions. The environment-based
theory discussed in Section 10.6 is an important step toward
resolving this difficulty because, for all observables attached
to the S + A system that do not involve the environment — in
practice, all observables that can actually be measured — the
statistical predictions obtained from the overall state vector
(10.33) and those obtained from the density matrix describing the
proper mixture |s_+⟩ and |s_-⟩ with weights |a|^2 and |b|^2 are
practically the same. However, as noted in Section 10.6, there
nevertheless exist physical quantities — the “sensitive
observables” such as M [Eq. (10.37)] — for which one can in
principle design a measurement and for which the two methods give
vastly different statistical predictions. If part (b) of the
axiom of empirical reality is adopted, these sensitive
observables are excluded from consideration, and difficulty 4a
disappears. Incidentally, within the environment-based approach
of Section 10.6 only part (b) of the axiom is required to restore
consistency; other approaches (for example, those based on the
algebra of observables, Primas [6]) also need part (a).

Remark 1. Although this simple qualitative axiom points to the
right general strategy, it by itself does not suffice to make
quantum mechanics a fully precise, difficulty-free theory of
empirical reality. More elaborate developments in this direction
are described in Section 15.4.

Remark 2. One may ask about the time evolution of the overall
system. If we take the state operator p'(t) [Eq. (10.5a), with
the time label made explicit] to describe at time t the
“empirical reality” of the overall system (more precisely, an
ensemble of such systems), epistemic consistency appears to
require that the empirical description at a later time t1 > t be
given by the state operator p'(t1) obtained by evolving p'(t)
according to the quantum mechanical time-evolution law (that is,
evolving the proper mixture that p'(t) represents). In general,
however, there is no guarantee that situations in which the
differences between p'(t) and p(t) (both at time t) affect only
sensitive observables will remain such that the differences
between p'(t1) and p(t1) affect only sensitive observables. If
that fails, an ambiguity in the predictive rules of quantum
mechanics would arise.

One reply is that constructing a general, precise theory in which
such observable differences do not appear — at least on human
time scales — is not forbidden by any no-go theorem. Theories
that implicitly involve the empirical reality axiom, for example
the Gell-Mann and Hartle framework, can be viewed as successfully
pursuing this program. In Appendix 2 we show that, within simple
decoherence models, no differences of the problematic type occur;
this result helps answer the frequent question “what do
decoherence theories add beyond the simple Jauch-like argument
mentioned before Eq. (10.5a)?”

Remark 3. One naturally asks how to define the boundary between
non‑sensitive and sensitive observables: “How is it defined? Is
it sharp?” In Appendix 2 we in fact use a sharp boundary
— defined by the number of environment spins involved — but that
idealization was introduced only to simplify calculations. In the
empirical reality approach it is neither necessary nor desirable
to determine which physical quantities are observable by
a criterion based on the internal complexity of the systems they
belong to. This approach is intentionally centered on humanly
communicable experience and on human capabilities. It is enough
to define sensitive quantities as those that are presently beyond
our reach; that set may change with time, but, as with the von
Neumann chain analogy, this poses no real problem so long as the
set is nonempty.

Removing Difficulty 4b

By itself, the axiom of empirical reality does not remove
difficulty 4b (the “and/or” difficulty). One might initially
think it does, because von Neumann’s theorem (Section 7.4), which
rules out decomposing an ensemble described by a ket (a “pure
case”) into two or more different ensembles, assumes that every
Hermitian operator corresponds to an observable. Under the
empirical reality axiom that assumption is not made, so von
Neumann’s theorem does not straightforwardly apply. Nevertheless,
that is not enough to resolve the “and/or” difficulty, as
a simple model illustrates.

Consider the model of Section 10.1, and for simplicity suppress
the degeneracy index r and let the index m run over {1,2}. In
that model the sensitive observables are correlations between
S and instrument degrees of freedom — observables such as G′
(defined just below Eq. (10.6)) that do not commute with the
pointer coordinate G. As long as one treats measurements of these
observables as possible (and retains the completeness
hypothesis), it is inconsistent to regard the final ensemble E of
overall systems as a proper mixture of N systems in which
N |a_m|^2 of them (m = 1,2) have L = 1 and G ≈ g_m. If, instead,
these “observables” are declared physically meaningless (as we
have done for the sensitive observables in realistic situations),
the inconsistency (which is just difficulty 4a) disappears. But
that move forces us to view E as composed of elements that are
not all identical. Initially the ensemble of overall systems is
described by a state vector of the form Σ_m a_m |...⟩, and if we
adhere to the completeness hypothesis — a natural intuitive
tendency when discussing quantum problems — we would normally
regard all elements of the initial ensemble as identical. Since
they are no longer identical in the final ensemble, one would be
forced to say that a spontaneous diversification occurs among
them during the interaction, produced solely by Schrödinger-like
time evolution. This is disconcerting, especially since the only
cause we can attribute to this diversification is that the
sensitive observables (such as G′) are outside human reach. This
shows that the empirical reality axiom alone does not remove the
“and/or” difficulty so long as the completeness hypothesis is
retained.

However, as argued before, the completeness hypothesis is
actually crypto‑ontological, as is the notion that systems
possess an identity in the ontological sense. When one constructs
a theory of empirical reality, one can consistently abandon both
of these assumptions. Dropping them removes the objection just
described and hence the “and/or” difficulty as well. To see this,
note that we cannot and do not scientifically know what
independent reality is. It may involve hidden variables; perhaps
a refined pilot‑wave theory would capture it. We have no
scientific means to decide which, if any, ontologically
interpretable model actually represents independent reality, but
we may conjecture that one does. That mere possibility suffices
to remove the “and/or” difficulty because in any such ontological
model (including GRW‑type models) an individual system ends up in
one or the other of the possible final states. Thus the existence
of such models allows us to imagine that independent reality is
described by one of them and so removes the paradox. At the same
time, we are not licensed — and in my view never will be licensed
— to claim that any particular model has been proved to describe
independent reality. Our scientific discourse must therefore
stick to empirical reality, which frees us from having to
introduce hidden variables or similar ontological elements at the
foundations of our descriptions.

At the same time, abandoning the completeness hypothesis means we
accept that supplementing variables need not be banned; indeed,
we extend the idea of supplementary variables into descriptions
of empirical reality. Relative to the S + A wave function, the
pointer coordinate values g_m and the corresponding values l_m of
the measured observable are then supplementary variables that
describe the phenomena perceived by the observer.

Giving up completeness also accords with the earlier remark that,
in such a description, wave functions and state vectors should be
regarded as tools for making predictions. These tools have
limited domains of validity: the wave function of an ensemble
E of systems S is reliable only for predicting outcomes of
a specified set K of conceivable experiments, namely experiments
that involve only the elements S of E. Outside that realm the
wave function has no defined meaning.

Important remark. To understand the “veiled reality” and
“empirical reality” viewpoints, the proposal just outlined for
removing the “and/or” difficulty is significant and merits closer
examination. One natural question is this: if each of us has his
or her own mind and the supplementary variables (definite pointer
positions, etc.) are somehow created or selected by the mind, why
do these appearances — the values of the supplementary variables
— appear the same, or at least the same enough, for all
observers? Formally, quantum rules already account for
intersubjective agreement: as argued in Section 14.9, the quantum
formalism gives zero probability that two observers will obtain
different outcomes for the same physical quantity. At first sight
that formal explanation might seem unsatisfactory because, like
any quantum mechanical probability, it is a probability of
“observing” something and therefore seems to presuppose some
“superobserver” who looks at the minds of each of us as we look
at instrument pointers. That metaphysical assumption is clearly
at odds with the spirit of empirical reality.

If one insists on a “normal‑physics” style explanation of why
different minds agree, two models are available. One is an
enlarged Bell‑type pilot‑wave model in which the configuration
space gains extra dimensions, one for each distinct “mind,” and
the projection of the representative point on any such extra axis
describes the state of the corresponding mind (see Section 16.6).
The other is the amendment to Everett’s relative‑state theory
proposed in Section 12.2. In both models intersubjective
agreement is automatic, without needing a separate “rule of the
game” as in the conventional theory.

Both models, however, describe the universe (including minds) as
evolving in time and were conceived as models of independent
reality, not merely of empirical reality. As argued in Chapter
14, one should be cautious not only about believing that any
particular ontological model is “true” but also about assuming
that time is a strongly objective arena in which independent
reality necessarily evolves. These cautions make such ontological
explanations less compelling. More fundamentally, the term
“explain” itself is ambiguous: philosophers often use it to mean
“reduce to some general law” rather than “describe how things
really are.” When our interest is limited to “explaining within
the realm of appearances,” the philosophical sense is the
appropriate one. Thus the formal explanation of intersubjective
agreement given above is adequate. The practical interest of the
two models mentioned is that they demonstrate the logical
consistency of assuming an ontological basis for observed
phenomena; their mere existence dispels fears of unavoidable
paradoxes. In particular, neither the Schrödinger cat nor the
Wigner’s friend paradoxes are true paradoxes within these models,
so their existence shows that those paradoxes can vanish within
a framework that aims only to describe empirical reality.

Finally, the empirical reality approach also helps reconcile
measurement theory with relativity. Centered on Bohr‑like
conditions, this approach makes Fleming’s hyperplane argument
(discussed at the end of Section 10.12) consistent and removes
the apparent mismatch. To summarize: the “veiled reality”
conception can indeed be made to remove the measurement‑related
difficulties, provided we abandon the goal of singling out one
ontologically interpretable description as the “true” one.
Physics should be interpreted as a description of phenomena
— empirical reality — meaning intersubjective appearances
together with predictive rules that connect these appearances
statistically. The description of appearances must use realist
language, but that language is only an instrument and does not
imply commitment to an ontological interpretation. The discourse
elements borrowed from realist speech were called weakly
objective concepts in Section 14.7.

Still, a closer, more quantitative analysis of the questions
raised by this conception is necessary. The next few sections
address this.

### 15.3 Empirical Versus Ontological Senses of the Verbs 'To Have' and 'To Be'

The verbs “to have” and “to be” are essential in everyday and
scientific discourse; it is hard to imagine a text or talk that
avoids them. At the same time, both verbs carry a strong realist
connotation, so their use raises questions in the empirical
reality approach. Their use must relate closely to weakly
objective concepts, since, as noted, we use those concepts to
describe our collective experience in realist language. In the
previous section the weakly objective examples were macroscopic
notions such as pointers and pointer positions. In principle, all
of physics could be framed entirely in terms of such weakly
objective concepts and corresponding weakly objective statements
— for example, “if a synchrotron is operated in such-and-such
a way, then with such-and-such probability the instrument pointer
will be found in this graduation interval.” In practice, however,
such a description would be tedious, so it is tempting to extend
realist language (and the verbs “to have” and “to be”) into the
microscopic domain. We now examine how far this extension can
reasonably go.

To make the issue concrete, compare two types of microscopic
descriptions. One obeys strict epistemological standards and thus
seems epistemically well justified; the other is an ontologically
interpretable model, namely Bell’s version of the pilot‑wave
theory (reviewed in Sections 13.1–13.3). The strict
epistemological standard we use here requires that to ascribe any
attribute to a system (to say “A has value a”) we must be able to
specify a procedure by which we could ascertain the truth of that
ascription without disturbing the system. We discussed in Section
4.3 and Chapter 9 that a weaker condition — allowing imagined
measurements — is less satisfactory, but we will consider it here
because it is commonly used. The procedure cited effectively
defines the meaning of the attribution. For example, under this
rule the position of a particle is defined by the way we happen
to know that position in the circumstances at hand. Even this
careful epistemological definition, which might seem to guarantee
ontological interpretability, can lead to assignments very
different from those given by an ontological theory.

Consider the EPR‑type pair correlation experiment as analyzed in
the pilot‑wave theory (Section 13.3). Suppose the measurement on
particle U (coordinate x1) occurs at time t1, earlier than the
measurement on particle V (coordinate x2) at time t2. If the
outcome of the first measurement is +1, then by the strict
correlation one knows in advance, with certainty, the outcome of
the later measurement on V: it will be −1. This is a case where
the epistemological definition above (Reality Criterion 1,
Section 9.2) applies, so one would assert that immediately after
t1 the observable Sy already has the value −1. But this assertion
does not reflect what the pilot‑wave ontological model implies:
according to Eq. (13.20), the hidden variable x2 that will
determine the outcome of Sy keeps its initial (very small, either
negative or positive) value until t2, and it acquires its final
negative value only at t2. If the ontological model is correct,
then attributing the value −1 to Sy immediately after t1 is
misleading if we interpret that value as actually possessed by Sy
at that time. It is not misleading, however, if the attribution
is understood as indicating a potentiality rather than an actual
dynamical property. Thus, taking ontological considerations into
account forces us in such cases to distinguish between an actual
dynamical property and a potential dynamical property that share
the same name, and to treat both as real in their respective
senses. But within an epistemological description of empirical
reality this distinction cannot be maintained: one must abandon
the ontological description (actuality) and elevate the
potentiality to the status of an actuality (without implying
ontological reality). In the example, we therefore have no choice
but to state that Sy has the value −1 immediately after t1, but
we must add the qualification “in a weak (or epistemological)
sense” (d’Espagnat [10]). One must not assume that all the usual
ontological consequences of using the verb “to have” apply in
this epistemological sense.

Which consequences of “to have” remain valid and which do not can
be determined only within a precise, quantitative theoretical
description of empirical reality, outlined in the next section.
Even now, however, one important consequence is clear. When the
verb “to have” is used in its ordinary, implicitly ontological
sense, one is tempted to extend the domain of the proposition to
counterfactual situations. That extension is not justified when
the verb is used in the epistemological sense. This observation
bears directly on the applicability of the two reality criteria
discussed in Section 9.2. Reality Criterion 1 can be used, in
a tentative description of microscopic empirical reality, to
justify statements of the form “on system S, the physical
quantity A has value a,” provided the verb “to have” is
understood epistemologically rather than ontologically. But this
restriction rules out extending Reality Criterion 1 into Reality
Criterion 2, because the arguments that support such an extension
rely on counterfactual reasoning and on Postulate R, which
presuppose ontological thinking. Thus Reality Criterion 2 should
be reserved for systems close enough to classical ones to make an
ontological interpretation harmless — essentially, for
macroscopic systems.

A side remark on epistemology and ontology. In classical physics
it was common to use concepts defined in an epistemologically
sound way (i.e., by reference to actual or possible measurements)
and yet to attribute ontological significance to them; any
objection to that attribution was usually philosophical rather
than empirical. Quantum theory has made the need to distinguish
epistemology from ontology more pressing. Nevertheless, the
change was not always pushed far enough: physicists often kept
strict epistemological definitions but still treated the defined
quantities as if they described elements of an external
independent reality. One of the book’s recurring claims is that
the distinction must be carefully maintained: implicit
ontological interpretations should be avoided when describing
empirical reality, and if one attempts an ontological description
one must make that intent explicit.

### 15.4 Theoretical Description of Empirical Reality

A precise, quantitative description of empirical reality is
needed; here we outline a tentative one. Fortunately, an
appropriate formalism already exists: the formalism underlying
the updated version of Omnès’s theory [11], in which a deficiency
of the original version — the lack of a criterion for truth — has
been corrected by introducing a restrictive truth criterion.
Using that formalism does not imply acceptance of all of Omnès’s
conceptual commitments (see Section 11.4). Because we apply the
formalism to empirical rather than independent reality, we must
change some of the language Omnès uses. One can say either that
we are presenting a new theory based on Omnès’s formalism or that
we are putting Omnès’s formalism on a different conceptual
footing. Either way, we acknowledge Omnès’s debt to this formal
structure.

The main linguistic change concerns the word “true.” In Omnès’s
theory, values ascribed to macroscopic quantities like “pointer
localized in a given graduation interval” are treated as facts
and hence as true or false (Omnès links this to decoherence,
which he regards as generating such facts). In our present
approach those same macroscopic notions are treated only as
weakly objective concepts (see especially Section 14.10 and the
argument based on sensitive observables showing these values
cannot be full-fledged facts about independent reality).
Consequently, asserting that “in general measurement‑like
processes the pointer is always well localized” would, because of
the existence of sensitive observables, violate the truth
criterion stated in Section 1.6.

When the objective is merely a theory of empirical reality, the
difficulty noted above becomes primarily semantic and therefore
manageable. Pointers and similar macroscopic systems are such
that it is practically impossible to measure incompatible
observables on them: all observables that can realistically be
measured are compatible, and even if in principle incompatible
measurements were possible we would normally not consider
performing them. Let us therefore define macroscopic systems
simply as systems on which we are at present incapable (or
unwilling) to perform such incompatible measurements. This
definition is human‑centered and would be unacceptable if physics
aimed to describe independent reality, especially since the set
of systems so labeled may change as human capabilities evolve. In
the context of empirical reality, however, this human‑centered
definition is appropriate and consistent.

---[chunk-boundary]---

With this definition, since all practically measurable quantities
associated with macroscopic systems are compatible, we can
construct logics that are consistent in Omnès's sense (see
Section 11.4) simply by taking any collection of propositions
about those quantities. These are essentially the "sensible
logics" Omnès described. We may then define a notion of "E-truth"
(E for empirical) for a proposition by following the same
procedure Omnès used to define truth in his theory. Concretely
(see Section 11.4), this consists in asserting: (i) all
propositions about macroscopic systems mentioned above are
E-true; and (ii) given any sensible logic L and a property a, the
property a is E-true if L can be enlarged by adding a (and its
negation) to its field of propositions while preserving
consistency, and if in every such enlarged logic a is logically
equivalent to a proposition of the type mentioned in (i).

Similarly, we can define the notion of a proposition being
E-trustworthy within some consistent logic by the same procedure
Omnès used in his final formulation to specify what propositions
should be called "reliable" in a given logic.

When this is done, it turns out (see Section 11.4 and/or Ref.
[11]) that the only E-true propositions are those about the
practically measurable quantities of macroscopic systems and,
possibly, outcomes of quantum measurements considered exactly at
the time the measurement is performed. All other propositions of
quantum physics are, at best, merely E-trustworthy within some
consistent logic. This applies, in particular, to propositions
that—after a measurement on a quantum system has yielded
a definite outcome—we often feel justified in asserting about
properties of that system at times finitely different from the
measurement time. For example, suppose the z spin component S_z
of a spin-1/2 particle is measured at time t0 with outcome +1/2,
and suppose the particle is free at earlier and later times. Then
the proposition that at some earlier time t1 (finite and
different from t0) S_z already had value +1/2 is only
E-trustworthy within some consistent logic that contains the
proposition "S_z = +1/2 at t0." The same applies to the
proposition that it will have value +1/2 at some later time t1
different from t0. Similarly, in the standard Bohm example of two
spin-1/2 particles U and V in the spin-zero singlet state S = 0,
if the spin component S_u of particle U along direction a has
been measured with outcome +1/2, the proposition that S_v has
value −1/2 is not E-true but only E-trustworthy within some
consistent logics that include the propositions S = 0 and S_u
= +1/2.

As this last example shows, calling a proposition that uses the
verb "to have" merely E-trustworthy rather than E-true is
equivalent to saying—that is, using a notion introduced in
Section 15.3—that in this proposition the verb "to have" must be
taken in its weak, epistemological sense only. Consequently, the
claim made in Section 15.3—that when we have such a proposition
(inferred from Reality Criterion 1) we cannot move to Reality
Criterion 2 (i.e., we cannot infer that the proposition would
still be true if the S_u measurement had not been performed or
had been replaced by a measurement of another spin component)—is
corroborated: there is no basis for believing that a proposition
that is "E-trustworthy within some logic" is E-true independently
of whether S_u is measured or not.

### 15.5 Empirical Reality and Nonseparability

In Section 8.2 it was shown that the predictive rules of quantum
mechanics satisfy parameter independence and therefore
nonseparability does not allow faster-than-light signaling
(indeed, it cannot be used for slower-than-light signaling
either, except in very indirect senses; see, e.g., Refs. [13] and
[14]). In Section 14.11 we noted that because of this,
nonseparability—unlike empirical facts such as the nonexistence
of a universal Newtonian time—is not, by itself, likely to be
a notion required to explain experimental facts other than those
designed to test it. We stressed that, accordingly,
nonseparability does not have a scientific status as robust as
other "negative" discoveries (for example, the relativity of
simultaneity). Indeed, in highly specialized operational accounts
of basic physics (advanced textbooks) and in accessible
expositions (popular books), explicitly mentioning
nonseparability would often be inappropriate and confusing.

It is tempting to summarize this by saying that nonseparability
does not apply to empirical reality. But can we actually make
that claim, and in what sense? That is the question we now
address. Note, however, that while this question is interesting,
it is less important than the issues analyzed in Sections 15.2
and 15.3, because it concerns semantics more than the consistency
of our worldview. Put explicitly: Do our chosen (but natural)
definitions of weakly objective concepts, macroscopic systems,
empirical reality, and so on, happen to make nonseparability
irrelevant for empirical reality?

One aspect of this question is the status of local causality
within a theory of empirical reality. Let us begin there.

Empirical Reality and Local Causality

As noted in Section 15.2 (see, in particular, "Removing
Difficulty 4b"), the completeness hypothesis is essentially
ontological, so its meaning lies outside the realm of the
empirical reality concept. Within an empirical reality theory the
completeness hypothesis is therefore meaningless;
correspondingly, the notion of a full specification of the events
(or variables, etc.) in a given space-time region is also
meaningless. That notion is, however, central to the very
definition of local causality (see Section 8.4). Thus, within an
empirical reality description, local causality cannot even be
defined, and it is therefore meaningless to speak of its
violation. In other words, the theorem labeled Bell 2 in Section
9.3—important in its domain—applies only to theories that
explicitly aim to describe "reality as it really is" (Bell's
explicit purpose). Experimental data do establish a violation of
the Bell inequalities, showing that no locally causal description
of independent reality can be correct. But by itself this has no
implication for our "empirical view." For this reason, when
a brief account of the above is given, it is not inadmissible to
assert that empirical reality is separable. Strictly speaking,
however, that word is a misnomer here, since the impossibility of
defining local causality in this context means that neither
"nonseparable" nor "separable" has a well-defined sense.

Empirical Reality and Bell 1 (EPR)

The previous remarks do not completely settle the status of
nonseparability within a description of empirical reality,
because nonseparability can also be deduced via the Bell 1 or
Bell 3 theorems. Observe, however, that in both of these theorems
Bell's inequalities are derived by applying to microsystems
premises that include Reality Criterion 2. In the Bell 1 (EPR)
argument this enters at the first step in the proof, where
incompleteness is inferred using the EPR argument; as shown in
Section 9.1, the EPR argument uses Reality Criterion 2. In the
Bell 3 proof, Reality Criterion 2 or an equivalent idea is used
implicitly at the point where induction is invoked, claiming that
information gained about the reality of some attributes of the
microsystems also applies to microsystems on which no
measurements were performed. Since, as we saw in Section 15.3,
Reality Criterion 2 is not valid for microsystems in the
empirical reality description, these proofs cannot be transferred
into the realm of empirical reality.

At first sight there may appear to be a mismatch between the
argument here and the one used in Section 15.2 to remove the
"and/or" difficulty. In Section 15.2 we relied on the notion of
ontological models (noting that these models do not suffer from
that difficulty), whereas here we argue against "nonseparability
of empirical reality" by using the principle that ontological
considerations should be excluded from the empirical view. Both
arguments are correct and not mutually inconsistent. The point is
that independent reality, unless it is simply beyond rational
analysis, may be constituted as various ontological models tell
us, and these models are indeed "nonseparable." But since it
seems impossible scientifically to determine which (if any) of
these models is true, these models themselves belong more to
metaphysics than to physics, and so does their common
nonseparability. A scientific description of empirical reality
may consistently ignore that feature.

The analysis above shows that, as expected, empirical reality as
we defined it may be said to be free from nonseparability
effects.

Remark: Interesting proposals have been made (Stapp [15];
Eberhard [16]) aiming to derive the Bell inequalities without
invoking realism. The meaning and relevance of the premises used
in those derivations are not indisputable; see Refs. [17–20] for
discussion. In any case, violation of those premises could not be
interpreted as a feature of empirical reality as theoretically
described above.

### 15.6 A Quantitative Illustration

It is useful to analyze in detail, with an example, how the
general ideas above apply in a specific case. Consider [21]
a pair of systems, one S a microsystem and the other
A a macroscopic system that functions as an instrument of
observation. The interaction between S and A occurs within
a short time interval (t0, t0 + ε), following the general pattern
of quantum measurement processes described in Section 10.1.

Imagine that at a time t ≫ t0 measurements (whose details we do
not analyze) are performed on both S and A at locations far
apart. Assume further that A is a macroscopic system in our
sense: on A only a set of collective macroscopic variables can be
measured. Following Omnès [11], we define collective macroscopic
variables by reference to the 2N-dimensional phase space of the
system, where a point corresponds to the positions q_i and
momenta p_i (i = 1,2,…,N) of the particles composing the system.
Within this space we consider many nonoverlapping cells `C_α`. 

It can be shown [11] that (i) provided their shapes are simple
enough and their volumes are appreciably larger than ħ, such
cells can be associated with projectors (more precisely,
quasi-projectors) onto appropriate subspaces of the overall
Hilbert space of the system; and (ii) the commutator of two such
projectors is negligibly small as soon as the two corresponding
cells are clearly separated. By definition, the collective
macroscopic observables are then the observables represented by
such commuting projectors (together with observables whose
spectral decompositions involve only these projectors), and
a macroscopic system is one on which only such collective
variables can be measured.

To begin, note that the "pointer coordinate" G is of course one of A's collective macroscopic variables. Let {G, U, V, W, …} be a complete set of compatible observables on A, so that the other collective macroscopic variables of A are functions of G, U, V, W, …. Let H_S and H_A be the Hilbert spaces of S and A respectively, and consider the subspace E of H_A that corresponds to G = g_n. In that subspace take as a basis the eigenvectors common to G and U, V, W:
|g_n, u_i, v_j, w_k>,
and write the right-hand side of Eq. (10.4a) (the relevant expansion of the joint state) in this basis. Then one may expand, for a suitable set of states |Y_m>, as
|Y_m> = Σ_{i,j,k} C_{m,i,j,k} |g_m> ⊗ |u_i, v_j, w_k>,   (153)
with Σ_{i,j,k} |C_{m,i,j,k}|^2 = 1.  (154)
Using appropriate coefficients a_m, Eq. (10.4) then takes the form
|Ψ> = Σ_{m,i,j,k} b_{m,i,j,k} |g_m> ⊗ |u_i, v_j, w_k>,   (155)
with
b_{m,i,j,k} = a_m C_{m,i,j,k}.   (156)

Let Q be an observable of S with eigenvalue equation
Q |q_l> = q_l |q_l>.   (157)

- Imagine simultaneous measurements of Q on S and of one of the
  collective macroscopic observables of A, say U. The probability
  that the outcomes are q and u_i is

`p(q, u_i) = Σ_{m,j,k} |⟨q| ⊗ ⟨g_m, u_i, v_j, w_k | Ψ ⟩|^2    (158)`
          `= Σ_m |⟨q| ϕ_m⟩|^2 h_{m i},    (159)`
with
`h_{m i} = Σ_{j,k} |b_{m,i,j,k}|^2.    (1511)`

The probability that a measurement of G yields g_n, irrespective of what Q yields, is of course
p_n = |a_n|^2 = Σ_i h_{n i}.    (1512)

Note that for the special case that Q is the quantity L that A "measures" on S, and q is the eigenvalue λ_n that corresponds to G = g_n, Eq. (15.10) yields

`p(λ_n, u_i) = h_{n i}.    (15.12a)`

By the rules of conditional probability, the conditional probability p(u_i | λ_n) that U yields u_i given that L has yielded λ_n is h_{n i}/p_n. With these notations Eq. (15.10) can be written

p(q, u_i) = Σ_m p_m |⟨q|φ_m⟩|^2,    (15.13)
where p_m = p(G = g_m) and |φ_m⟩ are suitable normalized states of S.

For any given m define an ensemble E_m described by the (normalized) product state
|Φ_m> = p_m^{-1/2} |φ_m> ⊗ Σ_{i,j,k} b_{m,i,j,k} |g_m, u_i, v_j, w_k>.    (15.15)

The mean value ⟨Q U⟩ of the product of observable Q of S and collective macroscopic observable U of A is
⟨Q U⟩ = ⟨Ψ| Q ⊗ U |Ψ⟩
     = Σ_{m,i,j,k} ⟨φ_m| Q |φ_m⟩ u_i |b_{m,i,j,k}|^2
     = Σ_m ⟨φ_m|Q|φ_m⟩ Σ_i u_i h_{m i}.    (15.16)

Using Eq. (15.13), this can be written as
⟨Q U⟩ = Σ_m p_m ⟨Q⟩_m ⟨U⟩_m,    (15.17)
where ⟨Q⟩_m = ⟨φ_m|Q|φ_m⟩ and ⟨U⟩_m = Σ_i u_i h_{m i} are the mean values of Q and U in ensemble E_m. Equation (15.17) is identical to the expression for ⟨Q U⟩ that one would have if the overall ensemble of S+A pairs, instead of being a pure state |Ψ⟩, were a proper mixture with weights p_m of ensembles E_m. Each E_m is a pure product state, so within every E_m the observables Q and U are uncorrelated; the mean value ⟨Q U⟩_m equals the product ⟨Q⟩_m ⟨U⟩_m, and the conditional probability for outcome u_i given outcome q equals the corresponding unconditional probability. This completes the demonstration: for the kind of measurements considered here, the theory yields exactly the same predictions as would an objective local theory (see the definition in Section 8.2, Remark 3) in which the role of the parameters λ specifying "objective states" is played by the index m. Since objective local theories satisfy the Bell inequalities, it follows that if only measurements of the type considered are made on these S+A pairs, no violation of the Bell inequalities can be observed. (This was to be expected, because proofs of Bell-inequality violations typically involve, for each element of a pair, mutually incompatible observables.)

Scope and Limits of This Result

There is, of course, no contradiction between the result above
and the empirical fact that Bell-inequality violations have been
observed using instruments of the same type as those considered
here (i.e., devices on which only collective macroscopic
variables—pointer positions, etc.—are read out). The point is
that in the analysis above we considered S+A pairs whose S and
A parts play exactly the same roles as the particles U and V in
standard Bell discussions. In those developments one considers
measurements performed on U and on V; here we consider (at time
t) measurements made on A, not measurements performed by A. To
mimic the standard Bell constructions we would have to (a) split
the ensemble of S+A pairs into four subensembles on which we
assume different pairs of observables are measured, and (b) have
an experimentalist change parameters that affect the A's (such as
magnet orientations). In other words, A would, in all four
subensembles, always "measure" the same quantity on S, namely the
observable L associated with its pointer coordinate G. What
changes between subensembles is the collective macroscopic
quantity assumed to be measured on A at time t (by other
instruments). Since all these collective macroscopic quantities
are mutually compatible, it is not surprising that the Bell
inequalities are obeyed.

The conclusion is philosophically significant because it is
intrinsic to the empirical reality concept and to it alone. From
the "normal" standpoint that identifies realism with a conception
of reality coinciding with independent reality, it is impossible
to reconcile the experimentally verified predictions of quantum
mechanics with any objective local theory. That, by contrast,
this reconciliation is possible within the empirical reality
conception shows that limitations on what we can know have led us
to construct an allegory of independent reality—an effective
model in which the general ideas of objective local theories
hold, but which may differ from whatever independent reality
actually is.

Remark: Recently Peres [22] and Khalfin and Tsirelson [23] proved
theorems along the same lines as the result above. Peres showed
that although Bell inequalities remain violated in certain cases
(e.g., a pair of spin-1 particles created in a spin-zero state)
even for arbitrarily large total angular momentum values, they
are obeyed by the outcomes of any measurement in which
neighboring values of a J component are lumped together because
of limited instrumental resolution. Khalfin and Tsirelson, not
restricting attention to a particular class of macroscopic
systems, reached the same conclusion by taking dissipation of
macroscopic systems into account (following ideas of Caldeira and
Leggett [24]) and treating dissipation quantitatively; their
methods differ somewhat but are essentially of the type discussed
in Sections 10.6 and 12.3. In both approaches the "specific
position within nature" of human observers plays a crucial role,
so their descriptions refer to empirical, not to independent,
reality.

### 15.7 Outlook

The notion of veiled reality may sound hybrid and therefore
unappealing: prima facie some may view it as an awkward attempt
to combine idealism and realism. The closer look taken in this
chapter may, however, change that judgment. Many philosophers—and
most scientists—now feel that, although idealism is not
disprovable, it still lacks something both intuitively and in
strict rational terms. Bonsack's approach was partly motivated by
such a feeling. As we saw when studying the Heisenberg picture in
Chapter 10, the conceptual upheavals induced by quantum mechanics
did not render Poincaré's structural realism obsolete; on the
contrary, they provided new arguments for it. At the same time,
it seems increasingly clear that naive realism is untenable and
that even sophisticated versions of realism (for example, the
later Einstein's) encounter difficulties, even within the domain
long considered their stronghold: straightforward, matter-of-fact
physics.

A consequence is the necessity—stressed throughout—to distinguish
between what we called "independent" and "empirical" reality.
This necessity also appears from a simple reading of contemporary
physics texts. The notion of empirical reality, as defined and
made reasonably precise in Sections 15.2–15.4, removes an
ambiguity present in both scientific and popular literature. That
ambiguity is protean. One manifestation is that articles and
textbooks dealing with objects such as composite-system wave
functions or thermal-equilibrium density matrices often use
expressions like "the system wave function has such-and-such
a structure" or "the system density matrix is this or that" while
avoiding explicit statements on whether those wave functions or
density matrices are "real." A curious reader who probes this
question finds arguments (complete entanglement of wave functions
for identical particles, the "improper mixture" character of many
density matrices, etc.) that favor the latter alternative, yet
the literature gives no clear answer to the question: If those
objects are not real, then what is real or what can consistently
be considered real? Still, transition probabilities in those same
texts are interpreted without qualms as referring not to what any
given observer would see but to what "really" happens.

One may say either that in Bohr's time the ambiguity was
transparent and Bohr proposed a philosophical way out, or
equivalently that within Bohr's philosophy the ambiguity does not
arise. Bohr's writings are not always clear and, while many
physicists still pay lip service to them in textbooks, his
ideas—already far from the present scientific orthodoxy—no longer
serve as genuinely accepted references on these matters. The
ambiguity therefore persists. It has long been felt by a number
of physicists who ponder the conceptual foundations of quantum
mechanics and who could be described as being In Search of
Reality [25]. These physicists regard the obfuscation described
above as double talk; they welcomed John Bell's forceful and
witty remarks on this point [26].

Given these facts, we have limited choices. One possibility is to
follow the path favored by Bell and others: adopt ontologically
interpretable theories (pilot-wave models, GRW, CSL, etc.; see
Chapter 13 for a review). Is that the only possibility? I claim
it is not. Another way, open to physicists skeptical of any
particular ontological model, is the one explained in this
chapter: to conventionally define empirical reality so that
appearances are preserved while the conceptual difficulties that
arise when physics is identified with independent reality are
removed. The analyses in this chapter show that this path is
indeed open; if one wishes to remain as "orthodox" a physicist as
possible, one cannot consistently avoid making use of empirical
reality in some form. (A still different option would be radical
idealism; Sections 14.3 and 14.8 explain why I reject that.)

Admittedly, the notion of empirical reality is not a final answer
to all questions and enigmas in the foundations of quantum
physics. Moreover, it requires physicists to renounce some
traditional scientific goals—something some may find hard.
Nonetheless, it provides a coherent way to save the appearances
and to resolve several conceptual problems without committing to
any specific ontological model.

---[chunk-boundary]---

On the other hand, this approach seems to follow the actual
developments of physics and it alleviates several difficulties.
As argued above, it resolves problems in measurement theory
— including the Schrödinger cat paradox and the associated
"and/or" difficulty — by allowing one to regard the completeness
assumption as meaningless within the domain of empirical reality.
It also reconciles measurement theory with relativity (see the
end of Section 15.2). Moreover, it provides a coherent
qualitative picture of the phenomenal world in which, as
experience shows, nonseparability does not manifest itself.
Admittedly, this comes at the cost of restraining our natural
tendency to extend, uncritically, the range of epistemologically
defined notions of 'having' and 'being' into an ontology that may
be too naively conceived. Everyday experience has ingrained this
tendency so deeply that keeping it under control is a demanding
intellectual exercise. Still, the example of several prominent
past philosophers shows that it is not impossible. What has been
shown in this chapter is that this restraint can be exercised
without sacrificing the standards of scientific exactness.

Remark 1. To avoid a misunderstanding that a rapid reading from
Section 15.2 to the present section might produce, I insist that,
as specified at the appropriate places above, the arguments in
these sections apply only to empirical reality. Thus, omitting
the qualifier "empirical" would misrepresent my position. For
example, it would be incorrect to claim that I endorse the idea
that reality is "local" or "separable" in any general sense. For
reasons explained in Chapter 14, I am convinced that the notion
of independent reality is meaningful and logically prior to the
notion of empirical reality, which is essentially a human-made
representation of the former. One must therefore take seriously
the information provided by the Bell theorems: it is impossible
to maintain any description of independent reality (that is, the
"reality" assumed by conventional realists) that represents it as
separable or local. (The argumentation of Sections 15.4 and 15.5
cannot be transferred to independent reality, not only because it
is based on a human-centered definition of macroscopic systems,
but also because, while independent reality is not composed
exclusively of macroscopic systems, any tentative description of
it can, by the definition of what independent reality is, be made
only in terms of true statements.) Indeed, with some
hair-splitting: although nonseparability is a negatively framed
concept, any scientist who rejects pure idealism may legitimately
state, in a positive manner, that independent reality is
nonseparable (or nonlocal). Such a scientist must regard the
notion of independent reality as meaningful, and must accept as
meaningful the empirical conclusions of science, including the
feature of independent reality expressed by nonseparability.

Remark 2. If parameter dependence were actually present in
everyday life — more generally, if the world of daily experience
were nonseparable — or if quantum superpositions of
macroscopically distinct states could be observed directly, then
presumably humans (and animals) would not have been able to
develop the very notion of localized objects (and perhaps would
not even have evolved). Viewed from this perspective, the
concepts we use to describe empirical reality appear particularly
clearly as tools for organizing our collective sense-data rather
than as faithful depictions of what "really is." At the same
time, it becomes less surprising that these concepts work so well
despite not being exact pictures of independent reality.
