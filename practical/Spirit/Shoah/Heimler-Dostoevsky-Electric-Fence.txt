### Frank, J. (1983). Dostoevsky: The years of ordeal.

    - Even forced labor was not totally intolerable for the
      convicts because it made sense and could serve some
      purpose. It was part of a comprehensible world; it even had
      the hope that freedom might come to pass... because chance
      and caprice are also part of human life.

    - But what if they were knowingly and deliberately forced to
      work at something that was _totally useless_ and hence
      totally inhuman?

    - Dostoevsky answers this question, knifing to the quick of
      his religious convictions:

        - The idea occurred to me that if one wanted to crush
          a man utterly, to inflict the most terrible of
          punishments so that the most ferocious murderer would
          shudder and dread it beforehand, one need only give him
          work of a completely useless and irrational character.
        - Though the hard labor now enforced is uninteresting and
          wearisome for the prisoner, yet as work in itself it is
          rational; the convict makes bricks, digs, does
          plastering, building; there is sense and meaning in
          such work.
        - The convict worker sometimes grows keen over it, tries
          to work more skillfully, faster, better. But if he had
          to pour water from one vessel into another and back,
          over and over again, to pound sand, to move a heap of
          earth from one place to another and back again;
          I believe the convict would hang himself in a few days
          or would commit a thousand crimes, preferring rather to
          die than to endure such humiliation, shame and torture.
          [4: 20}

    - One need only transpose this passage slightly to see its
      metaphysical implications. Not to believe in God and
      immortality, for the later Dostoevsky, is to be condemned
      to live in an ultimately senseless universe; and the
      characters in his great novels who reach this level of
      self-awareness inevitably destroy themselves because,
      refusing to endure the tonnent of living without hope, they
      become monsters in their misery.  [^1]


- [^1]
   - What Dostoevsky could only imagine in the 19th century has
     actually been put to practice in the 20th.

    - His intuition of the destructive effects of _senseless
      labor_ was confirmed by an eyewitness account of Jewish
      prisoners commanded to perform such work as an 'experiment
      in mental health' by SS medical staff in a concentration
      camp.

    - Eugene Heimler, who survived to become a psychiatric social
      worker, described the incident: 

    - The prisoners had been working in a synthetic fuel factory,
      but this was bombed and destroyed in August 1944·

    - The camp commandant then "ordered a few hundred of us to
      move sand from one end of the factory to another and when
      we had completed thls task, we were ordered to move it back
      to the original place.

    - At first we thought our guards must have made a mistake,
      but it soon became clear they had not.
    - From then on, day after day, week after week, we had to
      carry sand to and fro, until gradually people's minds began
      to give way.

    - Even those who had been working steadily in the factory
      before the bombing were affected, for that work had some
      use and purpose, even if it was for the Germans, but in
      face of a completely meaningless task people started to
      _lose their sanity_. (Some IT jobs, ONS etc)

    - Some went berserk and tried to run away, only to be shot by
      the guards; others ran against the electric fence and burnt
      themselves to death."

    - The commandant remarked "jokingly" one day that "there's no
      more need to use the crematoria now..."

    - Eugene Heimler. Mental Illness and Social Work
      (Harmondsworth, 1967), pp. 107-108
