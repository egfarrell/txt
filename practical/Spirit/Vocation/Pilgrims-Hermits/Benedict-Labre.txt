### A Book of Unlikely Saints
    - Naturally, then, eighteenth-century Saints had to be all
      their century was not. They were in opposition to it, not
      from one side only but from two ; they challenge the Age of

### Forty Hours devotion
    - A favourite of Benedict.
    - The "Forty Hours Devotion" is a Catholic tradition of
      continuous prayer and adoration of the Blessed Sacrament,
      usually lasting for 40 hours. It is rooted in devotion to
      the Eucharist and commemorates the _40 hours that Christ's
      body is believed to have lain in the tomb_ before the
      Resurrection.

    - Key Features of the Forty Hours Devotion:
        1. Exposition of the Blessed Sacrament: The devotion
        centers on the Eucharist, which is placed in a monstrance
        on the altar for veneration.
        2. Continuous Prayer: Faithful are encouraged to maintain
        unbroken prayer, often through organized schedules where
        individuals or groups sign up to take turns.
        3. Symbolic Duration: The 40 hours symbolize various
        periods in Scripture, particularly:
        - The 40 hours between Jesus' death and resurrection.
        - Other significant biblical events involving the number
          40, such as the Israelites' 40 years in the desert or
          Jesus' 40 days of fasting in the wilderness.
        4. Special Liturgical Activities: The devotion typically
        begins and ends with a solemn Mass and may include
        processions, hymns, and benediction.

    - The 'Forty Hours' can take place over three days and is
      seen as an opportunity for spiritual renewal, intercession,
      and deepening faith in the real presence of Christ in the
      Eucharist. It often involves themes of atonement for sin,
      petitions for peace, and prayer for the Church and the
      world.

### Monaghan
    - Another saint being recalled at Lourdes this year is St
      Benedict Joseph Labre (1748-83), the French-born beggar
      saint, a pilgrim-hermit, who walked and died on the streets
      of Rome and is the patron saint of pilgrims, the homeless
      and the hospitality of `Our Lady of Lourdes`.
    - Statue of Saint Benedict Joseph Labre at St Macartan’s
      Cathedral, Monaghan.
        - $MY_IMAGES/Spirit/Labre-Monaghan.jpg
    - St Benedict Labre has a special connection with the Diocese
      of Clogher and, particularly, with St Macartan’s Cathedral,
      Monaghan, where he is honoured with a large statue near the
      Chapter Room and a beautiful stained-glass window in one of
      the side-chapels of the Cathedral itself.
    - When St Macartan’s was being constructed during the late
      19th century, the Bishop of Clogher, Dr James Donnelly
      (1865-93) entrusted the project to the intercession of the
      then Blessed Benedict Labre. When Dr Donnelly first visited
      Rome as bishop in 1867, he celebrated Mass at his tomb in
      the Church of Santa Maria ai Monti, near the Colosseum. 
        - He promised to honour Benedict Labre in the new
          Cathedral if he himself lived to see the project
          completed and the pilgrim-hermit canonised.
    - And so, in the boundless providence of God, it all came to
      be. Benedict Joseph Labre was canonised by Pope Leo XIII in
      `1881` and the Cathedral was completed and dedicated to the
      service of God in August 1892, before Bishop Donnelly’s
      death just over a year later.

### Guilt: Caryll Hauselander
    - Whenever great evil breaks out among men, evil which
      expresses openly Satan’s hatred of the human race, it is
      answered by great love, expressing God’s love for human
      creatures, through man’s immolation in Christ, by the
      saints.
    - The suffering which a saint seems to choose for himself
      will often be one which is a direct answer to some
      tremendous sin which is either characteristic of his own
      generation or yet to come.
    - Thus St. Benedict Joseph Labre chose the apparently useless
      life of a wandering beggar; he was destitute and he was
      dirty, he ate scraps of refuse picked from garbage heaps,
      he was homeless and verminous, he died from starvation and
      exhaustion. 
    - He was the answer to the sensuality and gluttony of his own
      generation, but he was much more, he was “another Christ”
      living through and atoning for the horrors of our
      concentration camps, Christ taking that sin upon himself,
      overcoming that torment in each one of its victims, in the
      person of the strange misunderstood beggar.
    - And the life which St. Benedict Joseph chose exactly
      foretold the suffering of those who were to come, those
      victims of the concentration camps, who would be homeless,
      destitute, half naked, verminous, starving, glad to find
      scraps of refuse to eat, and, when they were not herded
      into gas chambers, dying by hundreds and thousands of
      starvation and exhaustion. 
    - The self-inflicted suffering of a saint, which seems
      unreasonable, fanatical and even repellent, is in reality
      an absolute necessity if the whole world is not to perish,
      a necessity springing from the crucifixion.
    - I have spoken of the suffering which a saint seems to
      choose, because in fact the saint very seldom chooses
      anything: _he merely surrenders to his destiny_, which as
      often as not he understands as little as we do.
    - In every circumstance, even such a matter as his own
      temperament, he sees the will of God, and because he is “a
      Christ” he does, not his own will, but that of his Father.
    - As Christ too, he is led by the Spirit, driven on by an
      _inward compulsion_ to acts which seem _ludicrous_ to
      utilitarian Christians. There is nothing cold and
      calculating in sanctity.
    - Some of the saints have in fact prophesied, foretelling
      future events, but more often the saint is one who does not
      know the meaning of his own life; his actions are
      prophecies, but he does not interpret them.
    - He is usually unconscious of their prophetic character and,
      far from wishing to reveal the secrets of his life to the
      world, he wishes to hide them.
    - The tongue of the prophet Isaiah was cleansed by a burning
      coal; the tongues of the saints are often _incoherent_ or
      dumb, but their hearts are purified in the crucible of
      love.
    - Some of the saints, like St. Francis of Assisi and St. John
      of the Cross, have been poets in the literal sense; all the
      saints have been poets as Christ was; in everything that
      they did, in their attitude to life, in their total
      surrender to the Spirit, who is the fire and the light of
      God and the wind of Heaven.
    - Because the saints are Christ led by the Spirit, all that
      seems illogical and grotesque in their lives is in fact the
      living out of Christhood in the world. It is above all else
      a giving battle to evil, even when they seem to be
      withdrawn from the world.

### The Divine Office
    - I have always found Benedict Joseph's attachment to the
      Divine Office wonderfully compelling.
    - Deprived of choir and choir-stall, of sonorous abbey bells
      calling to prayer at regular intervals, and of the support
      of chanting in unison with others, Saint Benedict Joseph
      carried the Hours into the highways and byways of Europe,
      into the shadows of the Roman Colosseum, into humble parish
      churches, and into the occasional barns where he rested
      upon the hay.
    - His love for the Divine Office made him a worthy namesake
      of the great Patriarch who ordered, "that nothing be put
      before the Work of God."

### A Misfit
    - As a young man, Benedict Joseph made a number of
      unsuccessful attempts at monastic life.
    - He tried his vocation with the Trappists, with the
      Cistercians, and with the Carthusians, but, in every
      instance, after a few months or a few weeks, he was
      rejected as being unsuitable.
    - Benedict Joseph was endearing in his own way. He was
      a gentle young man, tortured by scruples of conscience, and
      sensitive.
    - He was completely honest, humble, candid, and open. He was
      cheerful. But, for all of that, he was a misfit.
    - There was an oddness about him. He was drawn irresistibly
      to monastic life and, at the same time, rejected from every
      monastery in which he tried his vocation.

### A puddle
    - But, austere as it was, there is something genuinely
      _extravagant_ about his life, and I suppose that is the
      part about it that has always drawn me.
    - Benedict wasn’t a spill. He was an oblation. Unsparing and
      lavish, he poured himself out. He made of himself a sort of
      puddle, the sort of puddle made by the anonymous woman
      memorialized by Christ in the Gospels (Mark 14.9).
    - Obscure and extravagant, a waste, a life poured out for the
      love of Christ. A costly ointment and a fragrant perfume
      for the Lord, this reeking homeless man by any other name
        — but then... what must I smell like?

### Benedict decisive meeting
    - Benedict went away with complete submission to the rules of
      piety which opened up an adventure whose fortune nothing
      could foresee.
    - It is probable that he went first to Gray and then to
      Besançon, where he had a decisive meeting with this unknown
      priest. Nothing is known of such a meeting except that it
      took place, for Benedict was to mention it more than once
      in the course of his life, and the sequence of events
      indicates quite well what it was.
    - Once again Benedict had to recount his life without going
      into the diffuse details, according to the formula of the
      confession. His life and his thoughts were easily
      summarized.
    - He had always wanted to leave the world. It was in him
      a lively inspiration. He had solemnly abandoned his own
      people to obey the call of Christ. His only vow was the
      convent, and yet he could not remain in the convent itself,
      where he had most wished to live and first found happiness.
      His fate was that he could neither return to the world nor
      leave it.
    - The man to whom he confided with candid despair this
      impossible situation had no business ordering him to join
      his family. Benedict had vowed to leave her forever, and
      the Trappist Fathers had not thought that he should
      renounce that vow either.
    - So everything became simple. One could easily invent the
      words that were said to Benedict: "My son, there are other
      convents and other deserts. Look for a place in the world
      that welcomes you. Do not ask for anything, but wait to be
      asked. Go to the holy cities. It may be that one day you
      will not find what you have so desired."
    - Thus the road opened before Benedict, and it was a road to
      which this time no end was assigned. He was a pilgrim, but
      also a wanderer, because beyond one pilgrimage there was
      another, and the place of rest would only be indicated to
      him by an unexpected light.
    - In what country? Under what sky? To understand the sky one
      must already measure the greatness of this low world which
      is so beautiful with all the men, animals, birds, fish,
      mountains, valleys, plants, trees, meadows, fields,
      streams, rivers, seas together, as Granada said.

### Chapter VI The Unattainable
    - Benedict in this misfortune remained the same Benedict he
      had always been. Remarkable for his piety, absent-minded in
      his work, uneducated and unclean, in all things as clumsy
      as possible, and, with his persuasive voice, confessing his

    - He was certainly not without thinking of his dream, as
      tenacious as it was fragile, of living in a desert with
      only herbs and roots for food. However, he rigorously
      rejected all thoughts and never wanted to give in to his
      torment or leave the Trappist monastery.
    - He fell ill. Working in the swampy shallows of the land
      with increasing fatigue, he contracted a malignant fever.

      to the desire to keep the discipline of the Trappist, not
      without the fear of being unduly lost. He was taken to the
      hospice outside the walls, where the poor were received.
      There he could be given more comforting food. It was almost
      in unconsciousness that he left the Trappe enclosure.

        > ...and if at any time you see (God forbid!) your soul
