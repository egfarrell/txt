# Walking in God's name 
- Rob Amen

Editor's note: This is the second part of a three-day series
chronicling Pilgrim George Walter on his recent 1,350-mile
journey on foot from his home in Hampton to Chicago and back.
Part three will appear Tuesday.

Sixty-three-year-old George Walter has envisioned himself as
a priest since before he was 14 years old, when, having just
completed the eighth grade, he decided to leave home and attend
seminary school in Cincinnati.

Eleven years later, in 1966, a year removed from finishing his
theology studies at Saint Vincent College in Latrobe, George
found himself at a crossroads.

Despite almost 20 years of religious education and preparation,
George did not feel the connection, the calling, the deep
relationship with God that he believed were necessary to become
a priest.

Today, he is a pilgrim, walking across the country, sometimes
across the world, to serve God.

"There was a lot of questioning of things going on," George said.
"This was the 1960s. We had the Vietnam War, civil rights, the
hippie thing, Vatican Council changes. Everything was changing,
so everything was up for grabs. I became very confused about what
I believed, what I was committing myself to. You can't make
a permanent commitment to something that keeps changing.

"I wasn't able to make a commitment to priesthood in that state
of mind."

The priesthood was considered a noble vocation but, according to
George, was going through an identity crisis in the 1960s. Was
a priest a counselor, a social worker, a community organizer,
a teacher?

"A priest is usually defined as one who serves other people,"
George said. "That's so ambiguous. You can serve people being
a social worker or a nurse. The church has lost the sharp focus
of a priest as one who offers sacrifice to God. That's what's
causing a lot of confusion and the trickling away from
priesthood. Priests are leaving.

"The church still hasn't recovered from that."

George, studying in the Diocese of Pittsburgh at the time,
addressed his concerns with then-Bishop John J. Wright. He told
Wright that something was missing. Intellectually, he was ready.
Spiritually, he was not. His faith was weak.

Wright told George to finish the school year, then take
a pilgrimage. Pray. Seek the Lord.

In 1967, George graduated from Saint Vincent, was ordained
a deacon and immediately dropped everything to explore the West
Coast. He hitched a ride to Los Angeles and rented a small room
at the downtown YMCA and looked for a job.

He never found one. Instead of settling for being a bank teller
or swimming instructor at a boys club, George joined a group
called "The Diggers" and began providing food, shelter and
clothing for hippies.
    - Aside: The Diggers were a radical, anarchist
      community-action group based in San Francisco’s
      Haight-Ashbury from 1966 to 1968, not Los Angeles, known
      for using street theater to promote a "Free" society. They
      provided free food, clothes, and medical care to challenge
      consumerism and private property, influencing the 1960s
      counterculture.


He lived among a hodgepodge of loose individuals who were devoted
to nothing more than sex, drugs and a good time. He invested what
little money he had in helping runaway teens, organizing free
meals and managing an underground newspaper.

He stopped attending Mass and receiving the sacraments.

Some considered George as much a part of the southern California
scene as any of the individuals among whom he lived.

After a month and a half, George had failed to find answers. He
still felt disconnected with God, with the priesthood. So he
packed his bags and headed toward San Diego.

There, he delved into reading the works of Karl Marx, JRR Tolkien
and Aldous Huxley ("Brave New World"). He applied unsuccessfully
for a three-month job on a commercial fishing boat. He searched
with a friend for a month for a lost goldmine in Mexico. Mostly,
he sat and listened to the mighty Pacific Ocean's waves crash
along the shore.

His hair long, his beard unshaven, George happened upon a blue
Navy pea coat and long tassel cap, which he adopted. It would be
a precursor to the denim-patched robe he wears today. But he
never found answers.

Having exhausted 18 months in southern California, George
returned to his parents' home in Hampton Township. It was not
a warm reception. His appearance foreign, George was cast as an
outsider among friends and family.

By spring 1968, with an invitation to stay at a friend's farm
near Boulder, Colo., George again headed west. Only he never
found the farm. Instead, he found a group of about 30 men and
women gathered to await the end of the world.

By the end of June, when disaster was averted and time ticked on,
the group dissolved. George hiked higher, spending four weeks in
the Rocky Mountains, baking bread in a makeshift stone oven,
reading books.

Then, one day during the four weeks he spent in the Rockies,
George explained, his heart was opened, the darkness of confusion
erased and his spirit lifted.

"It wasn't a voice or a vision. It was an understanding in my
heart," George said. "I was ordained a deacon, but I felt that
wasn't the road the Lord wanted me to pursue, that he wanted me
in the seminary to have the training, but he was asking me to
take up a more radical way of life, a life of complete trust and
faith, having no security.

"As a pilgrim, you don't have anything. You don't have
respectability. Everyone knows what a priest is. No one knows
what a pilgrim is. And so it's just trusting that God will take
care and provide. When He calls you to a way of life, He gives
you the desire for it and the ability. So if anyone would have
told me when I was in the seminary about the vocation of
a pilgrim, I wouldn't have understood it. It's not the kind of
thing you read about in the vocation directory, but He formed it
in me over the years.

"It's a gift of faith. So then I realized the purpose of my life.
If God made me, then I belong to Him. The purpose of my life is
to return home to Him. So I thought to myself, 'How do I get home
to heaven?' "

Initially, George believed that the path to heaven traveled
through the Catholic Church. He trimmed his hair, returned to
Pittsburgh and accepted an invitation from Bishop Wright to
become a deacon at Sts. Peter and Paul Catholic Church in Beaver.
Six months later, Wright was made a cardinal and ordered to Rome.
Before leaving, he confronted George.

Either be ordained a priest or be laicized. Laicization declares
a priest's or deacon's ordination or solemn religious profession
as null and void, meaning that he never held any clerical or
religious rank in the church.

"I said, 'I can't ask for priesthood. There are still some
questions I have,' " George said. "So I asked for the
dispensation."

Still, George made a commitment to remain celibate and recite the
Prayers of the Breviary, prayers to be said at specific intervals
during the day.

Without a parish, without direction, he spent time praying for
answers to questions that plagued him. Then a revelation came to
him.

"Jesus said, 'I am the way. No one comes to the Father except
through me,' " George remembers thinking. "So I thought, 'How do
I get to know Jesus?' "

What better place to learn about Jesus than Jerusalem, the Holy
Land itself, he thought. So he charted a course for a pilgrimage
on foot from Barcelona, Spain, to Jerusalem.

He hitchhiked to New Orleans, caught a freighter that would carry
him across the Atlantic Ocean to Barcelona and began a one-year,
4,000-mile pilgrimage that would lead him not only to the answer
he so desperately sought but also to a life that few would dare
accept.

* * * * * * *

7:35 p.m.

The day is dwindling. Another hour of daylight before the 10th
day sets on George's pilgrimage from Hampton to Chicago, and
still no sign of possible sites where he can set up camp for the
night.

George has walked about 130 miles of this journey, almost 15
today from North Canton, Ohio, to the western fringe of the city
limits of Massillon, Ohio, a small town an hour south of
Cleveland that is best known for its high school football team
but could be famous for the breathtaking architecture and
aesthetics of its many churches.

George is tiring. The weather cooperated today -- a steady breeze
complemented a near-flawless sky -- but temperatures reached the
80s, well above the normal high for early May. George's sweat has
permeated every layer of his clothes and can be seen spreading
across the back of his denim-patched robe.

He knew when he left Saint George Romanian Church in North Canton
at about 8 a.m. that he would have to reach at least the western
side of Massillon to have any hope of finding a quiet,
country-like setting to pitch his tent and fall in for the night.

By 7:30 p.m., almost 12 hours later, George finds himself still
surrounded by concrete, street lights, traffic signals and
fast-food restaurants.

Part of being a pilgrim is that he never knows where he'll lay
his head each night. Maybe under the protection of leaf-laden oak
trees. Or in the middle of a meadow. Perhaps at a friend's or
stranger's house, or in a church stable.

On Thursday, May 6, just when it looks as though George might
have to walk into the evening to find the seclusion he seeks at
night, Carla and Kyle Gay of Manchester Avenue appear at the
corner of 32nd Street and Lincoln Way West, along the sidewalk
near "Your" Pizza Shop.

Carla is standing outside the family's emerald green Pontiac
Montana minivan. "Would you say hello to my children," she asks
George, smiling broadly. "We took a pilgrimage to Russia last
year and adopted these children."

The Gays' pilgrimage, though, included planes and automobiles.

Carla opens the passenger's side sliding door. Four beautiful
children -- Oksana, 15; Elena, 12; Lydia, 10; and Alex, 5 -- sit
anxiously inside, able to watch their mother's dialogue with this
stranger from behind tinted windows.

The Gays were returning from enjoying ice cream after Oksana and
Elena's soccer team's win, its first of the season, when they saw
George walking slowly along Lincoln Way.

Carla and Kyle, both teachers, homeschool Oksana and Elena. Alex
attends public preschool, and Lydia, who suffers from a minor
learning disability, attends the second grade in public school.

Kyle shoulders much of the responsibility teaching Oksana and
Elena while Carla maintains her job with the Ohio Virtual
Academy. The children could not speak English when they left
Russia a year ago. Tonight, they carry a conversation with
a pilgrim from Pittsburgh.

As they do, Carla sneaks to the driver's side of the mini-van,
where Kyle mans the wheel.

"Do you want to invite him to stay for the night?" she whispers
to her husband.

"I don't know," Kyle says. "That's up to you."

"This is a once-in-a-lifetime opportunity," Carla persuades.

"You're right," Kyle says. He pauses for a moment. "Go ahead."

Carla circles the mini-van. She approaches George but turns to
her children.

"Do you know of any place where he could pitch his tent?" she
asks them. "Do you want to ask him to stay with us?"

The question obviously is posed to George as well, who, after
Carla looks at him with an inquiring stare, graciously accepts.
On one condition.

"Can you drop me off back here tomorrow morning when I leave?" he
asks.

"Of course," Carla says.

He piles into the mini-van with his three satchels, and Kyle
heads to the Gays' 120-acre farm that they share with Carla's
parents and her sister's family.

* * * * * * *

If a pilgrim isn't predictable, at least he's consistent.

He never turns a cheek to a helping hand. He can't. He holds few
belongings and relies on the generosity of strangers to see him
through.

"Faith," George says.

Still, it's a risky proposition, especially considering that
George left St. Mary of the Assumption in Hampton Township with
one day's worth of food and water.

He often relies on nearby streams to renew his water supply. He
carries a small pump that filters water before emptying it into
a white, one-quart, squeezable water bottle. He repeats this
exercise four or five times each day.

Food is another matter. It is not uncommon for George to walk
hours without so much as a granola bar to silence the growls of
his empty belly. And that's on a good day.

"Whatever God provides, I eat," he says. "I don't plan my menu.
I let Him do it.

"I pick up things along the road, too, that are edible,
especially later (during the pilgrimage) when the berries are in
(season) and the fruits. But even (early on), I eat the
dandelions, and you can eat the cattails. You can make a nice
salad just from what you find along the road."

This particular day has been especially rewarding.

A young woman in her 30s passes George along Lincoln Way West at
about 6 p.m. Moments later, at the corner of 24th and Lincoln
Way, near the parking lot of a pizza/sandwich shop, the woman
waits in her mini-van. George, his head down, approaches. The
woman begins pulling out of the parking lot, stops and lowers the
passenger-side window. She leans across the passenger seat,
extends a bottle of water and offers pizza, her family's dinner.

George accepts. He takes one piece of pizza, thanks her and
quickly devours it. Tomato sauce sticks in his mustache and
beard.

Two hours earlier, while resting on a wooden park bench in the
shade at the corner of First Street and Lincoln Way East, just
outside the Massillon Museum, two elderly women slowly walk
toward him.

Margaret Shoemaker, who lives in an apartment not far from this
park bench, was having her hair styled earlier in the afternoon.
Dorothy Cox, a good friend of Shoemaker's who also is from
Massillon, joined her for the day.

Almost on cue, bells from a nearby church begin chiming.

The women exchange small talk with George, then part ways but not
before reaching into their purses and handing him a fistful of
dollar bills. Without counting the money, he stuffs it into
a pocket and thanks the women.

"We wish you a lot of luck," Shoemaker says.

"Pray for me," George says.

"We will," Shoemaker says. "The Lord will get you where you're
going."

"I count on it," George says.

Yet the day is growing long, the road lonely, by the time 7:30
p.m. ticks away.

George acknowledges that he may have to walk into the dark, past
sunset, to find a quiet, safe, secluded location to camp for the
night.

Carla and Kyle Gay -- and a higher power, George would insist --
ensure that he does not need to go to such measures.

After arriving at their home, the Gays offer George a room in
which to sleep. He thanks them but declines, explaining that he's
more comfortable in his tent.

Carla and Kyle escort George across the yard to a nearby house,
where Carla's parents, Gene and Nancy Hawk, sit on green, plastic
chairs on a cement patio. They're enjoying one of countless
breathtaking, bright orange sunsets in their back yard.

Rusty, Carla's husky golden retriever, trots over, wagging its
tail, as does a dark Tiger cat aptly named Tiger. Carla's niece,
Kelli Kurzen, just 8 years old yet donning playfully colorful
makeup, sprints across an adjacent plot of grass from her house.

Carla explains to the family details of George's pilgrimage and
the fact that this is one of many for him in the past 35 years.

"Don't you think he ought to get there soon?" Gene laughs,
addressing his grandchildren. "I've got just what you need,
George -- a motor home."

"Don't try to civilize him, Dad," Carla says, serving as
a cushion between her father's remarks and George's feelings.

Elena emerges from her grandparents' home with a glass of water,
which she hands to George, who now also rests on a green, plastic
chair.

"Praise the Lord for water," he says.

* * * * * * *

He later meticulously assembles his tent under a large tree
behind the Hawks' home, across the driveway from the Gays' home.
Kelli Kurzen runs into her house, grabs one of her pillows and
returns, handing it to George.

After George is done, Carla and her children lead him into their
home and to a seat at the head of their kitchen table. Carla
begins heating homemade macaroni and cheese and potato soup with
large chunks of ham. She cleans salad, then pours him a glass of
orange juice.

George begins eating. First carrot sticks. Then soup. Then salad
with ranch dressing, croutons and tomatoes.

He talks of his travels, of religion.

When he is finished eating, Carla asks for his thick and colorful
woven socks and tosses them in the washing machine. Kyle appears
from another room with a basin of warm, soapy water. George
gingerly toes the water, then submerges his large, bony, bumpy
feet.

It's 9 p.m., George's bedtime. He eases back into the wooden
kitchen chair. It's been a long day. He'll endure an even longer
one tomorrow. So many stops, so many conversations with so many
curious passers-by have left George a mile or two short of his
goal.

Those miles must be accounted for. And they will. Tomorrow.

* * * * * * *

Two weeks after securing a cabin on a cargo freighter and
shipping off from a port in New Orleans, George landed in
Barcelona, Spain.

It was 1970.

George's original plans called for him to walk through Madrid en
route to Jerusalem. But when he landed in Barcelona, he learned
of the Shrine of Saint James on the western side of the country
and the Camino de Santiago, a road leading from Barcelona to the
shrine.

With no timetable to guide him, George opted to walk across Spain
to see the shrine.

From the time he left his job as an orderly at Mercy Hospital and
his Locust Street apartment in the uptown section of Pittsburgh
on Thursday, Feb. 5, 1970, to the time he walked into Jerusalem
on Friday, May 14, 1971, George traveled through eight countries.

He spent a month in Paris, traveled through Switzerland, Rome and
Athens, then crossed the Mediterranean Sea from Turkey onto the
island of Cyprus and then into Israel.

"After I got to Jerusalem, I prayed and asked the Lord, 'Where
I should go next?' " George said. "I felt him say, 'This
pilgrimage is over, but you'll remain a pilgrim the rest of your
life.' "

George said he had no apprehension about embracing his newfound
profession. He did not worry about not having a home or money or
a job.

He was in God's hands.

"The walk from Barcelona to Jerusalem proved He could take care
of me," George said. "I landed in Barcelona with $150. One year
later, I still had $10 of that left in Jerusalem. That set me
free from the thing most people say, 'Well, you need money to
live.' No, you don't. I proved it. And I've been living without
it for 35 years."

On Friday, May 28, 1971, George caught a flight home to
Pittsburgh.

He moved back into his parents' house, the home in which he grew
up. He began volunteering at Saint Mary of the Assumption, his
home parish, the place that fostered his interest in his faith
and prompted his journey that ended short of the priesthood but
landed him squarely as a pilgrim.

For the next three years, George lived this life.

In 1974, he moved. He left his parents for the final time and
took up shelter in an old underground pump house 6 feet wide,
6 feet long and 6 feet high at the bottom of a hill from the
church. Amenities were lacking, to say the least -- no running
water, no electricity. Only a couple of feet of the top of the
pump house were exposed, keeping it cool and damp throughout the
year.

"The Lord told me to use it as a place for prayer," George said.

It was, and still is, in violation of the Allegheny County
Housing Code to live in buildings without utilities. George did
not care. And no one from the parish reported him.

George put a wood stove inside for heat and to cook. He
cultivated a small garden outside. He made the pump house his
home.

He met the Rev. Gus Milon after Milon gave a speech at Saint Mary
of the Assumption in early 1973. George began working with Milon
at a teen ministry in McKees Rocks shortly thereafter. George
often rallied teenagers for pilgrimages, albeit much shorter,
most lasting only one week. When Milon left the Pittsburgh area
for Chicago, George was hired as a parish secretary, a job he
held from 1981 to 1988.

He helped as best he could with children at Saint Mary's School.
According to Rose Stegman, director of Confraternity of Christian
Doctrine (CCD) classes at Saint Mary parish, George played guitar
at the school when asked and taught physical education classes on
a part-time basis for a short time.

In 1988, George abandoned everything to pursue what would be
a 13-year pilgrimage around the world that ended in Jerusalem
during the Catholic Church's Jubilee celebration of 2000.

George set out from Saint Mary with a female donkey named Unita
that a friend of his from the church bought to keep George
company. "It's the closest thing I've had to having a wife,"
George said with a laugh.

He walked west, then south, eventually reaching Mexico City in
six months. He left Unita behind, as the donkey only slowed him,
and headed toward California. Upon reaching the Monterey
Peninsula, George decided to establish a poustinia, or hermitage,
overlooking the ocean. He stayed for three years before walking
up the West Coast, through Canada and into Alaska.

He flew across the Bering Straight into Asia, where he exhausted
a year and a half walking across the continent. He spent the
winter of 1993 at a church in a small Siberian village near Lake
Baikal, a breathtaking spectacle in nature that is one mile deep
and holds 20 percent of the world's fresh water.

He worked his way into India and eventually flew to Rome because
he did not have visas for Iraq and Iran. He trekked to Croatia
and eventually into Poland, walked through England and Ireland
and back through England before reaching Norway. He traveled by
foot through Sweden, Estonia, Russia, Finland, back through
Estonia, and across Latvia, Belarus and Ukraine.

"Then," George said, "I was ready for Jerusalem."

For 17 months, from August 1999 to January 2001, he explored the
Holy Land.

When he returned to Saint Mary of the Assumption, he learned that
his home, the cramped pump house, had been covered over with dirt
when the parish expanded its cemetery. That prompted the Rev.
John Marcucci, pastor of Saint Mary, to permit George to bunk in
a small basement room in the old rectory adjacent to the church.

* * * * * * *

George's was -- and remains -- a dangerous path to follow.

While in California in the late 1980s, an 18-year-old man who had
been drinking approached George from behind, grabbed his staff
and began hitting him over the head with it. Police drove George
to a nearby emergency room, where he received nine stitches.

The man was later arrested and ordered to perform work to pay
George's $500 hospital bill.

In Turkey in 1999, he was accosted by Muslims who broke his staff
into three pieces and shouted, "This land belongs to Mohammed!
The cross does not belong here!" He walked without a staff for
about a year until he cut the one he currently uses from a tree
outside what is believed to be the prophet Elijah's cave on Mount
Carmel.

He was harassed and beat up by angry Pakistanis for not putting
away his crucifix while walking through their country during
a pilgrimage.

"He's happy because he's getting persecuted for the Lord," friend
Mary Sonnett said.

George commonly is stopped and questioned by police wherever he
travels. Some require identification -- he carries a passport --
and run background checks on him. That was particularly true last
summer, when he walked through New Jersey and New York.

En route to Alaska via Canada in the early 1990s, he was stopped
at the U.S.-Canadian border by the Royal Canadian Mounted Police
and questioned. When the police did not receive the answers they
wanted, George called former Diocese of Greensburg Bishop Anthony
Bosco, a good friend of George's, who vouched for him.

"I assured them he comes in peace," Bosco said.

George has witnessed events and met people that most only read
about.

The most bizarre, he said, came while spending six months in
India. Upon arriving at a shrine, George met a man who had
devoted his right arm to his god.

"He never used his right arm, and it was all shriveled up,"
George said. "He always had it up (in the air). Even when he
slept, he had a rope to hold it up."

He has had rocks and beer cans thrown at him, been verbally
abused across the world and been attacked by strangers who viewed
him either as a freak or religious threat.

Yet George keeps walking, without protection, without hesitation.

"The Lord," he said, "is my defender."
