
### They Followed His Call

- From now on, change in him will _never follow_ the pattern of
  his dreams and desires. It will always move in the direction
  marked out by the call and its sober effects. In the time
  before admission, a kind of instruction begins, introducing him
  to the life he will later find in an order and already shaping
  him for what is to come. 

- But it may also be that what he undergoes corresponds to _no
  specific order or spiritual direction_, and consists only in
  God’s naked work in the soul. 

- This change is first of all interior and hidden. It concerns
  his prayer, his inner life, his view of the world. He must
  consider certain details _almost painstakingly_: whether this
  or that is still appropriate or possible. Other things he can
  renounce or accept generously

- If someone responds to a call by pouring out emotion—turning
  what began simply into something dramatic or showy—that’s
  a sign the call is no longer alive in them. This doesn’t mean
  the original call wasn’t real, but it does raise serious doubts
  about the sincerity of the response and about the person’s
  current relationship with God. The call has been mixed with
  self-display, and in that mixture it has been smothered.

- God calls people in freedom. He lowers himself enough to be
  heard in an _ordinary human way_—and that also means he can be
  ignored. Like a plant overun with parasites, a call can be
  choked if not cared for. That’s why the one called has to
  protect it and let it grow. But it can only grow if _self-will
  gives way_. The person answered freely; whether that freedom
  remains alive is up to them. The call they accepted now has the
  right to shape their life, cutting away what doesn’t belong and
  strengthening what does, without regard for personal
  attachments or previous plans.

- "Stamped": One can often tell at once to which order certain
  older religious belong, since the spirit of their community has
  fully taken shape in them: in character and bearing, speech and
  attitudes. Something of this “embodiment” must begin
  immediately after consent, even if it becomes complete only
  slowly and organically. The person must accept being “stamped”
  in a certain way by the Spirit, even if he cannot clearly
  define what this Spirit is.

- Slow details:

- We often say that God has determined our path from all
  eternity, and we claim that we want to follow it. But when
  God’s call becomes concrete—when his will stands clearly before
  us in his Word as the shape of our life—that is when obedience
  becomes hardest. We must learn to accept the _unexpected_ as
  the only real option, and to receive what God freely gives as
  what truly matters.

- It means nothing to speak of surrender if we ignore the
  _specific, slow, and demanding ways_ God’s will unfolds.

- True surrender must combine generosity with careful attention
  to details. This attitude does not come naturally; it can only
  be received as a response formed by God himself.

- God’s answer is always a new, active, living demand. But its
  activity and liveliness are very different from what man
  imagines. The reality is at once harder and gentler, because it
  is life. And because it comes from God’s own life, it cannot be
  measured, assigned to a place, or predetermined. The dialogue
  of God the Father with the Son who remains on earth is perhaps
  the most living reality one could ever conceive...

- Many personal factors are involved in choosing a way of life.
  A person has to come to terms not only with himself, but also
  with the wider setting in which he lives, including the Church.
  What once looked like asceticism directed only toward the call
  itself must now become a lived discipline that takes in the
  person’s whole concrete situation.

- Because he is only human, much of what he thinks remains purely
  human. Concern for comfort, reputation, and doing what is
  “right” is placed on the scale and, perhaps for the first time,
  truly counts for him. He sees a humanly prescribed path before
  him that could bring him honor. He may still be too young to
  see how questionable plans can be when they are made by an
  individual alone. To find a counterweight against God’s call,
  he will cling to anything at all—to every plausible _word of
  warning_, to every _counsel to be sober_. He will probably take
  it all as an omen, a sign that he was about to do something
  _foolishly rash_.

- Although the Gospels first tell how the apostles immediately
  left everything to follow the Lord and only later speak of the
  difficulties of their chosen path, we must not conclude that
  the first step was easy for them. Following Christ is always
  a painful break in life. The renunciation is real, but the
  weight of the call outweighs everything else. Whoever is called
  must do everything to remain within reach of the voice. If the
  voice falls silent, everything is lost. The one called would
  rather lose everything than have the voice grow muted. The
  voice brings about discipleship. It is at once the Yes and the
  inability to say No. The thought of a life without this voice
  appears as unbearable solitude. And so one must follow: who
  knows whether the voice will ever be heard again?

- The silencing of the voice is such a terrifying event, and it
  so destabilizes a person, that anyone who has said No—or tried
  to put God off by postponing a decision—is permanently marked.
  He is and remains recognizable. He has pushed aside the
  decisive experience of his life. In the future he remains
  bitter, dissatisfied, sarcastic, and fault-finding. He never
  tires of laying out his reasons, concealing only a sense of
  “knowing better,” and trying to prove that discipleship is
  impossible. But he is already marked; his words are
  unnecessary. The No imprints itself and remains, and it can
  sometimes transform a person’s spiritual physiognomy more
  deeply than a Yes would have done.

- The one who consents knows that much struggle awaits him. He
  will not always have joy. Hours of doubt will come upon him.
  His mistakes and weaknesses will not leave him. He is in danger
  of joining the ranks of the lukewarm. He does not feel that the
  monastery, seminary, or community receiving him gains much by
  him. He is a questionable gift, one they may be able to do less
  with than with most others who could have applied. Yet he has
  no choice, because God has pointed precisely to him. God wants
  him, and God therefore vouches for him. If God gives him
  strength to consent, he can also give him strength to persevere
  in allowing himself to be changed.

- Perfect surrender to the Lord is an act of discipline that is
  meant to contain everything in God’s plan for a person. Its
  first and essential quality is readiness: an openness that
  stops calculating what seems possible, difficult, or
  impossible. This readiness has the courage to leave what
  belongs to God in his hands.

- The moment of hearing God’s call is not the time to experiment
  with practices of self-denial or to push the limits of fasting,
  vigils, or other austerities. True readiness keeps everything
  open—spirit, body, and time—and this openness is the basis of
  all genuine discipline.

- Key:

- In everyday life, however, this self-surrender does not mean
  passivity. On the contrary, it means doing everything as well
  as possible, so that half-measures and imprudent activism do
  not become obstacles to complete obedience. Surrender keeps
  one’s relationship with God free from the dross of a bad
  conscience.

- A person may be willing to give up things he once held dear,
  yet God may instead ask him to keep them. In that case, God’s
  will is discovered _not by renouncing_, but by remaining
  faithful. This changes how he relates to those things: he now
  loves them because God asks him to, and what was once worldly
  affection becomes shaped by Christian love.

- He stays ready to offer them back to God if asked, but without
  pushing or insisting—as if he needed to prove his willingness,
  or as if he somehow knew better than God.


- Prayer should shape a person’s whole way of living. It should
  make the one who is called kind and loving, so that others
  genuinely feel that love. Ideally, it even awakens questions in
  them: What kind of love changes someone like this? Where does
  it come from? How could I learn it myself? Such questions can
  become paths toward God.

- Everything about the one who is called should point to God, not
  to the self. Witness comes more from the way life is lived than
  from words. Speech is used only when necessary, without
  preaching or lecturing. Joy is simply allowed to show. Though
  there is an inner letting-go of a former way of life, that life
  still continues for others and should not be diminished by the
  decision, but given deeper meaning and stronger reasons to be
  affirmed.
