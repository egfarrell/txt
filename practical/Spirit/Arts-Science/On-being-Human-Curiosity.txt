### Kristin Lin Editor, The On Being Project

- The “Epic of Gilgamesh” is considered the earliest work of
  literature in human history. As novelist Marilynne Robinson
  observes, it’s a story that engages with questions that still
  resonate today, like “Who are the gods?” and “What is the
  purpose of life?”

- "I think these are questions that people need, crave, and they
  take them right back to primal origins," she observes in
  conversation with theoretical physicist Marcelo Gleiser in this
  week’s On Being.

- That one of human history’s oldest texts is anchored in
  questions of meaning points toward how powerful and enduring
  our curiosity is: It appears that asking "Why?" is a practice
  as ancient as it is emergent. We’ve simply found new ways of
  expressing it, expanding from literature and art to science and
  technology. 

- Marcelo Gleiser suggests that this curiosity spans not just
  traditions and cultures, but also fields of study. "The mythic
  narratives and the scientific narratives, they’re both asking
  the same question: Where did everything come from?" he says.

- Imagining the 4,000 years of questioning that have taken place
  since the "Epic of Gilgamesh" paints a beautiful picture of
  human curiosity. In asking the same question "Why?", we’ve
  arrived at many different answers: from the the "Odyssey" to
  Beloved, heliocentrism to the Big Bang theory.

- And so long as we continue asking, we’ll continue to discover
  and create. As technologist Kevin Kelly argues in his
  conversation with Krista, even as the technology for artificial
  intelligence and robotics advances, "there’s plenty of room for
  us to explore, curate, invent, innovate, love, chat, experience
  things, all of which are inherently inefficient and not things
  that machines are good at."

- And Robinson says this continued creativity will only continue
  to shape us, ad infinitum. "Every life is a new description of
  what human beings are. Every work of science, every object of
  art is new information. And it is inconceivable at this point
  that we could say anything final about what the human mind is,
  because it is demonstrating, in beautiful ways and terrifying
  ways, that it will surprise us over and over and over again."

- Understanding humanity’s creative pursuits as new articulations
  of who we are adds a new dimension to our work today — whether
  that means penning universes into existence like Robinson or
  contemplating the theory of the universe like Gleiser.
