### Are Mount Everest climbers dreamers, or just cultists?
Coleman Spilde

- commentary
    - "Everest: The Other Side" is a moving, maddening look at
      humanity's need to defy the odds


- In the title of his 1997 bestselling adventure tale, “Into Thin
  Air,” mountaineer and writer Jon Krakauer employs a double
  entendre so clever that its effect is extraordinary. Throughout
  the book, Krakauer recounts his attempt to summit Mount Everest
  the year earlier in what would become, at the time, the
  mountain’s deadliest season. I’ve been momentarily halted by
  that title each time I’ve picked up my tattered $5 thrift-store
  copy over the last few weeks. It’s witty, but not grim;
  accurate but also imaginative. In three words, “Into Thin Air”
  conveys not only the literal effect of Everest’s high altitude
  and how quickly the mountain can take a life, but also the
  ardent, almost fatuous confidence of those who choose to climb
  it.

- The dissonance between reason and ambition is what makes “Into
  Thin Air” such a compelling read. For someone like myself, who
  knows next to nothing about climbing, Krakauer powerfully
  elucidates the pull of a mountain like Everest and the
  trance-like state in which it ensnares its adversaries. Unlike
  some climbers, Krakauer can wax poetic while staying honest
  about the stakes. He writes about mountaineering as a spiritual
  practice, good for the mind and body alike, while questioning
  why some find that clarity only by staring death in the face.
  In that respect, extreme climbing is an incredibly selfish
  endeavor. Before he begins his push for the summit, Krakauer
  remembers his wife, Linda, telling him before he left for
  Nepal: “If you get killed, it’s not just you who will pay the
  price. I’ll have to pay, too. For the rest of my life.”

- That sentiment echoed in my mind throughout “Everest: The Other
  Side,” the remarkable new documentary from National Geographic,
  helmed by “Free Solo” and “Nyad” co-directors Elizabeth Chai
  Vasarhelyi and Jimmy Chin. The film is brutally honest in its
  depictions of cold human narcissism and the relentless pursuit
  of glory in the name of nebulous spiritual fulfillment. Yet
  it’s just as candid about humanity’s collective desire for all
  those things in one form or another.

- Such a wanton, helpless display of pride makes for a character
  study as gripping as it is frustrating. From that disharmony,
  “Everest: The Other Side” forges a uniquely human story of
  actualization at all costs, revealing the true price of a life
  spent looking up and not around.”

- We are an arrogant species, frustrated by our humble place in
  this ecosystem. And yet, we feel everything intensely. When we
  love, we want to take that love to the ends of the Earth. We
  want physical proof of emotions we cannot put into words. Maybe
  then all of this will mean something.

- As “Everest: The Other Side” asserts, nothing in this already
  mystifying life is ever so simple. The film’s subjects,
  mountaineer Jim Morrison and his partner Hilaree Nelson, found
  love at the edge of the world and made a life together, with
  Morrison becoming co-parent to Nelson’s two young sons. But
  their shared lust for adventure created a prickly tension
  within their family unit, a strain between their passion and
  parentage that sits at the documentary’s emotional center.

- Like those pulled by devout religion or extreme acts of faith,
  Morrison and Nelson are powerless before their distinct higher
  calling — especially with the literal highest calling of all,
  Everest, in their sights. They don’t just want to summit the
  mountain from its treacherous north face; they want to be the
  first people ever to ski down it. In their quest, personal
  glory and public vanity are forever at odds. Such a wanton,
  helpless display of pride makes for a character study as
  gripping as it is frustrating. From that disharmony, “Everest:
  The Other Side” forges a uniquely human story of actualization
  at all costs, revealing the true price of a life spent looking
  up and not around.

- Because this is National Geographic, the film is only partially
  concerned with relaying that price. This documentary is made
  for IMAX-loving, secondhand adventure seekers. Those who want
  sweeping shots of impossible sights won’t be disappointed.

- But while “Everest: The Other Side” includes countless
  breathtaking sequences of Earth’s tallest mountain and plenty
  of nail-biting anxiety in the eventual summit push and ski
  attempt, those moments are merely the film’s selling points.
  The crux of this story is on Nelson and Morrison’s shoulders,
  and their sincerity flies in the face of good judgment and
  self-editing, giving viewers a palpable sense of their egos.
  This isn’t to say that either of them is a raging narcissist
  who cares only about themselves, but there’s no doubt that
  their sport’s seismic, life-altering dangers are on full
  display, warts and all.

- Early in the film, Nelson tries to communicate the draw of
  climbing, and how fundamentally at odds it is with her role as
  a mother. “People ask me, ‘How do you find balance?’” she says.
  “There isn’t. It’s just chaos. You’re just hanging on. You want
  to be with your family, but you have a passion, and you want to
  work, and you need to make money. You’re all over the map.”

- It would be easy to dismiss Nelson as selfish, or, at the worst
  and most damning, a bad parent. How can someone have young
  children and decide to part with them for two months for
  a climb that is about, at face value, little more than bragging
  rights? How can a parent let their children spend two months
  wondering if their mother is dead or alive?

- These are easy questions to ask from the comfort of a living
  room or the cushion of a theater seat, but much harder to
  answer when you’re living the life. It’s important to remember
  that, in life, most of us have desires that would yank us in
  severe directions. It reminds me that cult experts will tell
  you that any of us could fall into the clutches of a cult, and
  those of us who argue otherwise are likely among the most
  vulnerable. Anyone who insists they are too self-aware to be
  pulled into something that goes against their own interests is
  admitting that they don’t know themselves or humanity very
  well. That’s one of life’s great mysteries: Why do we do things
  that we know are wrong, or bad for us? Death and taxes might be
  certain, but so are mistakes and regret, no matter how much
  we’d like to believe that we’re big enough to let go.

- Conflict is ever-present in Morrison, who chooses to climb
  Everest despite its perils. For him, the adventure is about
  more than pride or titles; it’s about honor and love. The
  journey is intended both to strengthen his bond with Nelson and
  honor the memory of his late wife and their two children, who
  died in a horrific commuter plane crash in 2011, a decade
  before he and Nelson decide to attempt Everest. Recalling the
  aftermath, Morrison tells how climbing brought him clarity.
  Unable to move from the edge of his driveway, consumed by
  grief, he eventually found the strength to simply walk up the
  slight incline and into his home — a microscopic example of the
  human spirit’s extraordinary tenacity he hopes to commemorate
  in the Everest climb.

- We are an arrogant species, frustrated by our humble place in
  this ecosystem. And yet, we feel everything intensely. When we
  love, we want to take that love to the ends of the Earth. We
  want physical proof of emotions we cannot put into words. Maybe
  then all of this will mean something.

- But more tests lie ahead. The route to Everest proves demanding
  in heart and health. And while training together for the big
  summit push by climbing a lower peak in Nepal in 2022, Nelson
  loses her footing in an avalanche the size of a small snow
  drift. On a ski slope, the drift would be minor. But on
  a mountain, every movement is measured. Nelson is swept away
  and under the snow, toward a cliff. We see it happen in real
  time, captured on a helmet GoPro. The moment is almost
  unassuming in its speed, but it permanently shapes everything
  that follows. Nelson is lost amid a series of avalanches, and
  the clock starts ticking.

- Counting the seconds seems futile. But climbing is futile, too.
  For Morrison, left to search for his partner amid the snow,
  hazardous ridges and cliffs, it all seems like a waste. More
  pain, more trauma, more memories that are left for only one
  person to hold onto. I was immediately reminded of what
  Krakauer’s wife told him by satellite phone before he began his
  push from Everest’s base camp. “Saying goodbye to you was one
  of the saddest things I’ve ever done,” he remembers her saying.
  “I guess I knew on some level that you might not be coming
  back, and it seemed like such a waste. It seemed so f**king
  stupid and pointless.”

- It’s hard not to dwell on that sentiment, already knowing what
  and who Nelson would leave behind if disaster struck. On some
  level, and to some people, it is all stupid and pointless. But
  that split second fortifies Morrison, confirming that his quest
  to Everest is for everyone that he’s lost, and every
  heartstopping moment along the way. It’s a spiritual pursuit,
  the kind that we make when we travel to a family member or
  friend’s favorite place to scatter their ashes; the kind that
  we make when we give eulogies, or just sit in the grass,
  staring into space, thinking about everything and nothing,
  everyone and no one.

- Some may be driven to climb Everest just to say that they did
  it, while others are looking for something else, something with
  no name. They want to pierce the heavens and reach through the
  atmosphere to grasp the indescribable truth that unites us all.
  And if they don’t make it back down to share that peace with
  the rest of us, it might be enough to experience it just once.
