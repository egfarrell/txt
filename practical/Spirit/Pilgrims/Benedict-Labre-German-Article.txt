The Literary Wren No. 3/2014

# Unacceptable for today’s Christians? A portrait, questions and answers

### Beggar, pilgrim, penitent — the life of St Benedict Joseph Labre
by Ildefons M. Fux OSB

- Not everyone who begins reading this article will finish it.
  The things reported here are simply too unbelievable — in more
  than one respect. There is hardly anything comparable. How is
  one to classify this strange figure? The “French saint” was not
  the founder of an order. He was not a bishop, not a preacher,
  not a martyr. But what was he then? He was a beggar and
  a pilgrim, hidden in the darkness of mystery to this day and
  still provoking our generation. The Church canonized him
  a century after his death and is not ashamed of him, even if
  more than a few voices of outrage accuse his life of being
  completely unreasonable for the Christian of today. Love, too,
  has its follies, and it will always be difficult to explain
  them. “Give me someone who loves, and he will understand what
  I mean” (Augustine).

- From: Joachim Schäfer – Ecumenical Lexicon of Saints.

- At the beginning of 1994, Arturo Jelmucci died, the last
  “barbone” (street vagrant) of St Peter’s Square in Rome.
  Countless pilgrims and tourists had seen him sleeping on
  cardboard under Bernini’s colonnades or cooking an egg on an
  old camping stove. All attempts to turn this homeless drifter
  into a settled and industrious citizen failed, because Arturo
  loved his freedom too much to buy comfort at the price of
  dependence. He spoke rarely and never drank alcohol. Two
  cardinals and several bishops attended his Requiem Mass. In
  some ways he recalled St Benedict Joseph Labre (1748–1783).

### Portrait of an outsider

- It was not unusual that the boy born on 26 March 1748 in
  Amettes in northern France, the eldest of many siblings, was
  destined for the clerical state, especially since his parents
  are described as deeply devout people. But his studies soon
  stalled, because he showed a pronounced aversion to the
  classical authors and to logical thinking in general — an
  obstacle he could not overcome. In an age of enlightened
  rationality, his mind was structured quite differently. He felt
  no call to the priesthood, but he did feel called to be a monk.

- His first attempt to become a Trappist failed because of his
  parents’ resistance. At most he might enter a Carthusian
  monastery, they said — but the monks of Val-Sainte-Aldegonde
  wanted nothing to do with him, and those of Neuville told him
  he was too young and insufficiently educated. When he was
  finally accepted somewhere, the prior told the distressed young
  man after six weeks that he was not called to be a Carthusian
  and dismissed him again. Perhaps God wanted him as a Trappist
  after all? But at La Trappe he was rejected because he was not
  yet 24 years old.

- The Cistercian abbey of Sept-Fons did accept him; Labre
  received the habit and was given the name Brother Urban.

- But his happiness did not last long. After six months, the
  abbot and the doctor declared that his weak constitution was
  not suited to the demands of monastic life. He was sent away.
  All attempts at entry had failed miserably. The disaster was
  complete, and Benedict Joseph stood there as one unfit for use.
  His long and painful period of searching ended with the
  unavoidable insight that he was not called to the monastic
  state. He had not found the place he longed for; from now on,
  homeless on earth, he would be a continual pilgrim toward
  eternity.

### Perfect surrender to the divine will

- His path now led him across Europe. Unrecognized and unnoticed,
  he was only very rarely perceived for who he truly was, and so
  his routes can be reconstructed only with difficulty. No one
  has ever counted the kilometers he covered on foot along dusty
  country roads. He never begged in advance for supplies. In his
  shoulder bag he carried a New Testament, The Imitation of
  Christ, and four volumes of the breviary.

- In this way he reached Loreto, Einsiedeln, Paray-le-Monial,
  Assisi, and Monte Cassino, but also Absam in Tyrol — and not in
  an air-conditioned luxury bus like today, but in the
  “classical” way: on foot. The beauty of nature, the wonder of
  creation, he found first and foremost in the Creator himself,
  and that was enough for him. The isolated self-glorification of
  created things was as foreign to him as it was to St Francis.
  He was also deeply convinced of the necessity of doing penance
  — we will return to this later. “The poor too,” he said, “must,
  like everyone else, mortify and master their flesh.”

- On 31 August 1770 he wrote in one of his few letters to his
  parents: “I have taken the road to Rome.” He walked this road
  without resignation or bitterness, in an even more perfect
  surrender to the divine will. “One must fulfill the will of
  God” — this was all he would answer when pressed with
  questions.

- In Rome he merged into the ever-present and very large crowd of
  beggars. He slept in a niche of the Colosseum when he was not
  on a nighttime pilgrimage to one of the seven principal
  churches of the Eternal City. During the day he was usually
  found in a church where the Blessed Sacrament was exposed.
  Benedict Joseph Labre is one of the Eucharistic saints; in Rome
  he was therefore called the poverello delle quarant’ore — the
  “poor man of the Forty Hours devotion.” There one could see him
  kneeling from early morning until sunset, upright and
  motionless, his eyes fixed on the Eucharistic Savior. His knees
  had long since become swollen and deformed. That is how he is
  still depicted today: the rosary around his neck, hands crossed
  over his chest, as if sunk in ecstatic rigidity — while fleas
  jumped about on his body and lice made their way through the
  thicket of his hair.

### Penance as a purpose of life?

- We read correctly, and the truth allows no softening
  reinterpretation: Benedict Labre never washed, he never changed
  his rags, and accordingly he smelled. He was covered with
  vermin, and more than once a church rector, worried for his
  congregation, drove him out of the church with many words and
  gestures. For the street boys he was a favorite target of
  mockery and stone-throwing.

- Across the centuries we too feel this enormous provocation,
  which attacks our civilizational achievements and offends our
  sense of hygiene. Not every key fits every lock, and since the
  penitential spirit of earlier times has largely been lost to
  us, and the confession of guilt at the beginning of Mass has
  often become mere formula, we find ourselves in almost
  insurmountable perplexity. We tend to assign hair-shirts,
  penitential belts, and instruments of penance of every kind to
  the “darkness of the Middle Ages” and to think about following
  Christ in categories of wellness and well-being. As the
  awareness of standing as a sinner in need of reparation has
  faded, insight into the necessity of penance has also become
  rare.

- When asked once how one might come to great love of God,
  Benedict Labre answered that three hearts must be fused into
  one: “The first must be entirely love and tenderness for God,
  the second full of kindness toward one’s neighbor, the third
  hard in penitential spirit toward oneself.” Our saint possessed
  this way of thinking and willing to a high degree. “He wanted
  to be regarded as low and common in everyone’s eyes; he tried
  to be thought a sinner, a hypocrite, an idler, a fool or
  a madman.” Walter Nigg gives perhaps the most accurate
  interpretation when he understands the repulsive vermin on the
  beggar’s body and clothes as a “living penitential garment.” It
  was a penitential belt of unconventional kind, more dreadful
  than others.

- Only from the perspective of penance can one understand that
  when the little creatures tried to crawl away, he carefully
  took them between his fingers and put them back into his
  sleeve. In a similar way St Rose of Lima once made peace with
  the mosquitoes that tormented her. No wonder Benedict Labre
  grew weaker and weaker. Once his confessor watched him walk
  away and thought to himself: “He is going to die — a martyr of
  penance.”

### A martyr of penance

- On the steps of a church he was overcome by deep faintness.
  When people lifted the gasping man to carry him into the house
  of a nearby butcher, they noticed that he weighed no more than
  a child. There, behind the butcher’s door, he gave his soul
  back to his Creator on 16 April 1783, without having received
  the last sacraments. The children in the street interrupted
  their games and began shouting loudly: “The saint has died, the
  saint has died!” In the following days a stream of people moved
  toward his coffin, and since the burial of St Philip Neri, Rome
  had not seen such a triumphant funeral as this one. At the
  death of the French beggar, all of Rome without exception
  declared that this poor man was a saint. Cardinal de Bernis,
  the French ambassador, reported this to his government in
  Paris, and Madame Élisabeth, the sister of Louis XVI, began
  invoking him as an intercessor in her prayers. Minister
  Vergennes judged that the deceased deserved closer
  investigation. The Church later carried this out and canonized
  Benedict Joseph Labre on 8 December 1881.

- When the French painter André Bley was traveling through Rome
  in 1777 looking for a model for a depiction of Christ, he
  noticed the beggar with the small red beard and painted his
  portrait. Another artist, A. Cavallucci, was so moved by
  Labre’s posture in prayer that he too portrayed him. His death
  mask lets us recognize his “true face.”

### “My thoughts are not your thoughts …”

- What God does always has meaning, yet the path that should lead
  us out of our perplexity is not easy to find. Of course one may
  recall, as a kind of first aid principle: admirandum, non
  imitandum — the saints are to be admired, not imitated in every
  detail — but that alone does not say very much. One must also
  listen to the prophet Isaiah, who has God say: “My thoughts are
  not your thoughts, nor are your ways my ways” (Isaiah 55:8).
  This prevents us from measuring God’s actions by our own
  coordinate system.

- A reference to that period of European intellectual history in
  which our saint lived may help us further — the age of the
  Enlightenment, the time of Voltaire and the encyclopedists
  — whose “closed” worldview Labre simply breaks open without
  compromise. Today’s systems of practical living (and their
  ideological justifications) are no less closed in their
  forgetfulness of God, their economic absolutism, and their
  all-encompassing immanence.

- Was it misguided that Paul called himself a “fool” (see
  2 Corinthians 11:16–21), and that others followed his path as
  “fools for Christ”? Behind the highly unpleasant exterior of
  our French saint lay something highly precious and admirable:
  the peace of a soul that knew no earthly sources — whereas for
  many of his contemporaries it is the reverse: the exterior
  shines while hiding something poor and questionable.

- Again: one must see Benedict Labre kneeling motionless for
  hours before the Eucharistic mystery. Then the windows toward
  divine transcendence open again.

### Questions and Answers

- The article on Benedict Joseph Labre shows how worthwhile it
  can be to reflect on unconventional “paths to salvation.” But
  it also raises — quite apart from the value judgments shaped by
  the spirit of each age — essential theological questions.

- Wren (interviewer): According to common understanding, the
  purpose of canonization in the Catholic Church is to present
  the saint as a model. You convincingly refer to the principle
  admirandum, non imitandum — we should admire every person
  seriously striving to follow Christ, but we do not have to
  imitate every detail of his life. But concretely — in what does
  the holiness of this praying and penitent beggar consist, who
  apparently had nothing in mind but his own salvation, who did
  nothing for others, did not even speak as a wandering preacher
  about God’s love, but instead allowed his talents to waste away
  rather than invest them? You write of the “unacceptability for
  the Christian of today,” but how could earlier times so
  completely ignore the parable of the talents?

- Fux: I think this difficulty can be resolved if one looks in
  the life of this devout man for what is exemplary — the point
  in which the striving of all saints converges: love. The love
  of the saints for God, and concretely Labre’s yes to Jesus
  Christ, is our model — a love that held fast even under the
  most adverse circumstances and expressed: You are worth it to
  me.

- Wren: Of the three marks of great love of God that Labre
  himself formulated, the first — to be entirely love and
  tenderness toward God — clearly applies to him. But regarding
  “kindness toward one’s neighbor,” as a beggar he seems more to
  have benefited from it than radiated it. In this context, the
  parable of the talents seems decisive, since it calls for
  active use of our abilities in human relationships. And if, in
  his “hard penitential spirit toward himself,” Labre burdened
  his fellow humans with fleas, lice, and stench, does that not
  show a lack of compassion? Any other form of mortification (for
  example self-scourging) would have been more altruistic.

- Fux: The difficulties you mention seem to arise from viewing
  the saint in personal isolation, detached from the ecclesial
  dimension. What the saints do in their life of prayer and
  sacrifice — and of course also in their apostolic efforts
  — they always do with and for the Church. They can never detach
  themselves from their integration into the total structure of
  the Church, the Mystical Body of Christ.
  A “self-sanctification” in the strict sense cannot exist — that
  would be a reprehensible egoism. The saint who seeks to love
  Jesus Christ with his whole heart wants what Jesus wants and
  loves the Church.

- Regarding the talents, one must avoid an overly narrow
  interpretation. There is also a “talent of prayer,” and the one
  who prays will always bring benefit to the Church and to
  individual members. And there is certainly also a “talent of
  suffering,” a special calling to carry the cross — and this
  talent too is directed toward helping the Church and its many
  members who are in need.

- Wren: You say “The saint wants what Jesus wants,” and you write
  “What God does always has meaning.” Do such formulations not
  overlook the fact that here on earth very often what happens is
  not what God wants, but evil — which God does not want? And is
  what Jesus wants not already made sufficiently clear in the Ten
  Commandments and the Sermon on the Mount? One finds no
  justification there for a penance consisting of the slow
  destruction of one’s own body.

- Fux: You are right — concise formulations always carry the
  danger of oversimplification. The question of God’s permitting
  evil is the hard core of theodicy. It seems to me it must be
  approached from theological anthropology: God created the human
  being as image and likeness. God not only has love — he is love
  through and through. The likeness of the human being therefore
  lies in the calling to love. If God wants the human being to
  become entirely love like himself, then he must also will human
  freedom — with all its risks.

- A love without freedom would abolish itself and become
  impossible; the result would be puppets (as behaviorists and
  materialists like to see us). That would amount to destroying
  the very concept of love, just as it would destroy marital love
  between persons. Moral evil cannot be willed by God in itself
  and as such. It is permitted and tolerated for the sake of the
  freedom required for love. In this sense, even saints — people
  always seeking union with God — are not immune from error.
