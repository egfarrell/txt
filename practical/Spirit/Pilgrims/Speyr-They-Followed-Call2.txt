- Saying No leaves a `spiritual wound`.
    - Her reflections here are severe but compassionate. She
      believes that deeply hearing God’s call and refusing it
      leaves a person inwardly divided — often embittered or
      restless. Not because God punishes, but because one has
      turned away from one’s deepest truth

- Von Speyr’s treatment of `refusal` is unusually penetrating
  because she does not describe it mainly as a moral failure or
  “breaking a rule.” She describes it existentially:
    - as a person turning away from the deepest truth ever spoken
      to him.

- The wound comes because the call was personal.
    - The call is not generic religious advice. It is not merely
      “be a good Christian.” It is God addressing this concrete
      person. Once someone has truly sensed that — even dimly
      — he can never entirely return to the previous innocence of
      not knowing.

- Refusal creates division within the self.
    - The person continues living outwardly, perhaps even
      successfully, but inwardly something remains unresolved.
      Part of him knows he stepped away from what was most real.
      Von Speyr suggests this unresolvedness can later appear as
      bitterness, sarcasm, restlessness, cynicism, or perpetual
      criticism of radical discipleship in others.

- The “No” keeps echoing.
    - Just as a faithful Yes reverberates through life, so can
      a refusal. She almost portrays it sacramentally: the soul
      becomes marked by the refusal. Not damned, not abandoned
      — but marked. A person may spend years constructing
      explanations around the refusal, yet still secretly revolve
      around it.

- She is especially alert to rationalisation.
    - One of her sharpest observations is that people often hide
      from the call beneath perfectly reasonable arguments:
        - duty,
        - prudence,
        - realism,
        - fear of rashness,
        - concern for reputation,
        - “balance,”
        - or exaggerated humility.
    - None of these are necessarily false. But they can become
      shields against surrender.

- Refusal shrinks the soul’s horizon.
    - Earlier she says the call introduces the person into
      “infinity and eternity.” To refuse is therefore not merely
      to decline a task but to retreat back into self-defined
      limits — “this far and no further.”

- She believes people instinctively recognise the wound in one
  another.
    - There is a haunting line where she says the man who has
      silenced the voice “remains recognizable.” He becomes
      someone perpetually explaining why discipleship is
      impossible. She does not mean this cruelly. I think she
      means that unoffered love hardens.

- Yet her emphasis is ultimately not fear but truth.
    - She is not trying to terrify the reader into obedience. The
      deeper point is that vocation corresponds to reality. God’s
      call is not arbitrary; it reveals who the person most truly
      is. Refusing the call wounds because it wounds communion
      with one’s own deepest identity in God.

- There is also an implicit tenderness in her severity.
    - She repeatedly insists that God is patient, especially with
      the late-called.
    - Even the frightening possibility of refusal exists because
      God calls in freedom. Love does not coerce. The very pain
      of refusal reveals how serious and dignifying the call was.

- For someone discerning in his 50s, one particularly moving
  aspect of her thought is this:
    - She does not imply that the decisive question is “Am
      I capable of this radical life?”
    - The more decisive question is: “Can I bear living outside
      the truth of what I have heard?”

- That is a very different kind of discernment.
