- It is always the same story. One must live. Abbé François’s
  concerns were the same as his brother’s about Benoît. Faith is
  the essential matter, but it is nonetheless true that we have
  a role in the world, and that if one follows a religious
  vocation one must pursue it with human means. Learn at last to
  compose Latin speeches, since this is required by custom, train
  oneself in theological reasons, know men and the life around
  one. What does Benoît want? It does not suffice to read, to
  pray, to attend the elementary catechism as he does under the
  pretext of ignorance, it does not suffice to be helpful and
  ready to sacrifice everything. One must also show oneself
  a man. It was certainly not without good will that the curé
  often repeated such advice to Benoît. Benoît did not know what
  to answer. He tried to please his uncle more. And then one fine
  day he declared that he could not reconcile himself to the idea
  of being a parish priest. He did not understand any theology,
  nor art of living. To succeed his uncle, to direct
  parishioners, was beyond his mind and strength. Then what? He
  took up his old refrain and began saying at random that he was
  thinking only of withdrawing into a desert.


- During the five years he had spent with his uncle, Benoît had
  returned to Amettes only rarely for the holidays. His brothers
  and sisters were growing up and admired their elder brother who
  was destined for the priesthood. If his progress in studies was
  rather slow, his piety was beyond all praise. The parents too
  were proud of Benoît. The request he made to them as soon as he
  arrived was not taken in the least into consideration. It was
  obvious that they would never authorize Benoît to follow
  a decision that went against all reason. They had already made
  sacrifices to prepare him for the priesthood instead of putting
  farm work in his hands as befitted the eldest son. Benoît
  remained the sole heir to the patrimony and in that capacity he
  owed himself to his brothers and sisters. Jacques, the second,
  was a health concern. They could in no case rely on him. His
  mother finally showed him how distressed she would be if he
  were far away from her and in such difficult living conditions.
  All the usual family reasons were put forward. But he, always
  with his idea that the supernatural, the Virgin and Christ,
  reign, and that nothing else prevails, persisted in begging his
  parents to let him go. Whatever they decided, he would obey,
  but nothing could interrupt the pleas he made to them.
  According to the letter, he renewed his request in refusing any
  discussion. Absolutely submissive, and from the depths of his
  heart opposing the family will. His very gentleness seemed an
  inadmissible resistance.

- Then there came, in the words of a witness, the harsh and
  stinging words. One can only imagine, but it seems clear they
  sought the weak point, and so it would be for all the stays
  Benoît was to make thereafter at home. His puny look. His
  naivety. He knew nothing of life, nor of any life. If he said
  there was no other life than that devoted to Christ, it was not
  difficult to answer that in turning away from the priesthood
  for a vocation of which no one in the world can be sure, he was
  not much in agreement with a sacred will that parents have the
  role of representing. He was led to say that he was responding
  to a call that no one could gainsay. But what, after all, of
  obedience, if he refused reasons and discussion? Was he not
  devoured by _the worst pride if he believed only himself_?

- One can only imagine. It is certain that these remonstrances
  were at the heart of the drama, he having for stake only an
  uncontrollable call (as the poet has poetry), while his parents
  spoke in the name of the simplest piety, which commands the
  acceptance of the condition allotted to us. Everything turned
  against Benoît, and he was reduced to offering himself to the
  torment of the rules set against him. He no longer even had
  recourse to grace, to which he himself and by law had no right
  to lay claim. There remained to him only a few words, like
  those he had repeated since earliest childhood about a desert:
  “I want to go to Our Lady of La Trappe.” They shrugged their
  shoulders. Childish stubbornness, the parents said to
  themselves. After a few days, Benoît left the house, stammering
  and confounded.

He took again the road to Érin by that path, at the bottom of the
valley, which had once led him to the school of Nédon. The peace
he had been so good at maintaining in his family was now broken.

Affection still existed between his parents and him, but there
was no longer any understanding. And it was his fault, speaking
in the name of Christ.

The days that followed his return to Erin were very dark. He
increased his prayers and austerities, but could not regain his
calm. The situation seemed inextricable to him. In spite of his
resolve to be gentle, and the gentleness of his whole life within
him, the contradiction he had learned to accept was becoming more
and more intolerable. He had to believe at last that his faith
was not great enough. He went back to the routines of the
presbytery.


People told him they hoped to see him one day succeed his uncle.
He replied that he would never dare, that he was not capable of
it, having no such vocation. As soon as the curé’s brothers had
settled everything and the village affairs had returned to a more
normal course, Benoît went back to Amettes, no doubt with his
father. He was about to turn nineteen. The recent trials had made
him more wild. He did not know what would become of him. He felt

At last, as everything was going badly, he declared again that he
thought only of entering La Trappe, and that he would wait for
his parents’ agreement. They once more opposed him with
a categorical refusal, and the discussions resumed. Benoît spoke
of fleeing into the desert. He was not capable, he said, of
living among other men, except in the silence of La Trappe, and
since that solitude was denied him, he must look for any other
solitude, as the anchorites had done.

But people of former times were of a stronger temper, everyone
pointed out to him, and in those days there were miracles that no
longer happen today. Benoît, who had just escaped the plague
against all expectation, replied that trust calls forth all
miracles at any time.

These modest country chatterings were nonetheless the vivid
expression of a period in which even devout people believed
little in the reality of miracles, and a great deal in the
efficacy of work and social standing. What place did Benoît give
to work? He himself would be obliged (whether he liked it or not)
to seek a position one day. But without miracles, there was only
boredom.


Benoît had never had a friend with whom to share his thoughts.
The fraternal affection of his uncle came as a surprise to him,
this uncle who remembered the time when he played with young
Benoît, taught him hymns, and imposed penances on him. The vicar
found again a young man just like himself at that age, tormented
by the impossible desire to enter a religious order. Abbé Vincent
had wanted to become a Franciscan, and had had to give it up to
obey his family. But Benoît, reading the books of Father Lejeune,
had kept in his memory the words that the Missionary reported
about Saint Francis of Assisi: Christ said that whoever left for
love of Him and of the Gospel his father, his mother, and his
possessions would receive a hundredfold in this world.
