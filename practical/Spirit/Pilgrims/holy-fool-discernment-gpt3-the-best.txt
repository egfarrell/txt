
# Block trials

I’ll describe the structure first, then the daily practice, then how to read the results.

### THE STRUCTURE (4–6 WEEKS)

- You will not “decide” during this period. You are only observing.

- Divide the time into two equal blocks.
    - Block A: live as if Physics is your `obedience`
    - Block B: live as if Pilgrimage is your obedience

- The key rule: 
    - during each block, you do not treat the other path as an
      option. It is bracketed, not rejected, but unavailable.

- If possible, start with the one you feel less emotionally drawn
  to. That reduces bias.

### BLOCK A: PHYSICS AS OBEDIENCE

- For 2–3 weeks, you live as if your life direction is settled:
  Physics is the path you have accepted.

- This does not mean romantic study. It means discipline.

- Concrete commitments:
    - Fixed daily study hours (even if short, e.g. 90 minutes).
    - Work on material that is difficult, not exciting.
    - Keep regular sleep, meals, and ordinary social contact.
    - No pilgrimage planning, reading, or fantasy. When it arises, gently note it and return.

- Interior stance:
    - Each morning, say plainly (not emotionally):
    - “For these weeks, I accept this as my obedience.”

- What you observe (write briefly each evening):
    - Resistance: does it soften or harden over time?
    - Energy: does fatigue feel empty or meaningful?
    - Peace: does it deepen quietly or remain flat?
    - Temptation: does pilgrimage appear as escape or as background longing?
    - Self-image: do you feel more real or more performed?

- Important: do not judge days `individually`. Look for `trends`.

### BLOCK B: PILGRIMAGE AS OBEDIENCE

- For 2–3 weeks, live as if your life direction is settled:
  long-term pilgrimage is your path.

- You do not need to be physically on the road, but you must live
  under its logic.

- Concrete commitments:
    - Daily long walks in all weather.
    - Simple meals, limited comfort.
    - Fixed daily prayer rhythm (even when dry).
    - No academic study “just in case”.
    - No planning physics as a fallback.

- Interior stance:
    - Each morning:
    - “For these weeks, I accept this as my obedience.”

- What you observe:
    - Loneliness: does it hollow or deepen you?
    - Repetition: does it ground or irritate you?
    - Body: does weakness feel meaningful or threatening?
    - Dependency: does reliance on others feel truthful or humiliating?
    - Self-image: do you feel seen by God or invisible to the world?

- Again, watch `trends`, not moments.

### THE CRITICAL DISTINCTION

- You are not looking for:
    - Excitement
    - Tears
    - Consolation
    - Clarity

- You are looking for:
    - A peace that survives boredom
    - A willingness to continue without reassurance
    - A sense of “this costs me, but it fits”
    - A quiet reduction of inner argument

- One path will likely produce intensity that spikes and collapses.
- The other will produce something flatter but sturdier.

- The right path often feels:
    - Less dramatic
    - Slightly disappointing to the ego
    - More sustainable in dryness
    - Harder to justify to others
    - Easier to return to the next day

### AFTER THE EXERCISE

- Only after both blocks are complete do you sit with the
  question:
- “Which obedience made me more whole, even when it stripped me?”

- Not which felt holier.
- Not which moved you more.
- But which reduced self-deception and increased truthfulness.

- You may still need courage after this. Discernment does not
  remove fear; it clarifies what you are afraid of losing.

### Last word

- One last word, given your history of burnout and the humility
  gained from it.

- Whatever you choose must include structure not of your own
  making. If you choose pilgrimage, consider binding yourself to
  real accountability (a spiritual director, a rule, a community
  that knows where you are). If you choose physics, bind yourself
  to deadlines and service rather than pure contemplation.

- Freedom without form will re-create the split.

- If you want, after you try Block A and Block B for even one
  week each, you can come back and describe only the `resistances`
  (not the consolations). That is often where God speaks most
  clearly.

# Background on this approach

- Yes—what I gave you is not invented from scratch. It draws from
  several traditions, but they converge on the same wisdom, which
  is why it feels “clean” rather than ideological.

I’ll name the strands briefly, not academically, but so you can see the lineage.

First and most strongly: Ignatian discernment, but stripped of sentimentality.
St. Ignatius of Loyola was very suspicious of decisions made at the peak of consolation. His core insight was that God’s will is often revealed not by intensity but by what remains livable when consolation withdraws. The block method echoes his idea of testing a “state of life” by imaginatively and practically inhabiting it, then watching the movement of consolation and desolation over time—not moments. The key Ignatian move here is attention to durability rather than feeling.

Second: Benedictine and Cistercian stability.
The emphasis on obedience, repetition, boredom, and the slow revelation of truth through daily fidelity comes straight from monastic discernment. Benedict’s genius was realizing that holiness emerges less from choosing the “right” thing and more from staying. That’s why I framed both paths as obediences rather than projects. The question is not “Which is higher?” but “Which can I vow myself to without becoming unreal?” That is deeply Benedictine.

Third: the Desert Fathers’ distrust of self-directed asceticism.
They warned constantly that a life that looks radical but remains self-interpreted is more dangerous than an ordinary life lived under authority. That’s why I emphasized form, limits, accountability, and the risk that pilgrimage—precisely because it is holy—can remain subtly self-authored unless bound. Their rule was simple: whatever path increases humility, patience, and truthfulness is the safer one.

Fourth: a very old Thomistic distinction (even if unnamed).
Aquinas distinguishes between movements of appetite (what draws us) and judgments of reason informed by virtue (what fits our nature and end). Tears tell you what matters deeply; they do not yet tell you what you are called to sustain. The block method lets reason observe appetite over time without repressing it.

Finally—and this matters for you—there is something specifically post-burnout in this approach.
After illusion collapses, God often stops speaking through fire and starts speaking through weight. What can be carried? What does not inflate or hollow you? What makes you simpler rather than more interesting? This is not a punishment; it is a refinement.

So the method is not trying to “figure God out”.
It is trying to see where your life becomes less performative and more truthful.

One last reassurance.

If pilgrimage truly is your obedience, this exercise will not weaken the call—it will sober it. And if physics is your obedience, the longing for pilgrimage will not vanish—it will become integrated, no longer competing for ultimacy.

Either way, you are not choosing between God and God.
You are choosing between two ways God has already visited you—and asking which one you are meant to consent to without reserve.

Take your time. Return when you’re ready.
