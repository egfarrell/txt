# A never-ending quest
Rob Amen

Editor's note: This is the final installment of a three-day
series chronicling Pilgrim George Walter on his recent 1,350-mile
journey on foot from his home in Hampton to Chicago and back.
George Walter does not belong here.

Lake Forest, Ill., with its tree-lined residential streets,
mansion-like homes, pristine gardens and manicured lawns, is many
things. A town in which a pilgrim could be found typically is not
one of them.

Residents of this quaint town about 30 miles north of Chicago
take pride in their luxurious possessions, judging by the houses
and cars parked in their driveways. Money appears to be of little
concern or much of an obstacle. It is not uncommon to see people
jogging, biking, walking their dogs and pushing their children's
strollers along the sidewalks, seemingly without a care. It's
a scene that would make a Norman Rockwell painting envious.

By 20 minutes till noon on June 14, George has weaved his way
through this striking section of the state, where rumor holds
that celebrities such as Michael Jordan and Mr. T own mansions.
He wears his tattered, denim-patched robe with pride. He has only
pennies to his name. And he believes that he is the richest man
in the world.

George has walked almost 700 miles since leaving St. Mary of the
Assumption Roman Catholic Church in Hampton on April 26. By
nightfall, he will reach his destination -- Marytown,
a conventual Franciscan friary and retreat center that serves as
the national shrine to St. Maximilian Kolbe and where Eucharistic
adoration is available 24 hours a day, seven days a week. This
friary is George's northern-most destination on his pilgrimage
before he makes a wide southwest turn through Illinois and heads
home.

Water from heavy rains that fell an hour ago begins evaporating
under George's sandals as he navigates the sidewalks. The sky
slowly clears. The sun's rays emerge from the lingering clouds
and warm George's face.

Despite being mere miles from Marytown, George does not alter his
schedule. Rather than trudging on in hopes of arriving early, he
stops at West Park at about noon.

Picnic tables with benches are scattered across the grounds.
Tennis courts, a baseball field and playground serve as the main
attractions. Mothers and children begin filling the park as
George takes off his purple Diocese of Pittsburgh baseball cap
and rain poncho. The passers-by take long, hard stares at him.

Every step George takes changes him. He is a different man, more
weathered, than the one who left St. Mary seven weeks ago.

He sits on a bench, drinks from a bottle of water and smiles. He
says he is no more affected emotionally by being so close to
Marytown than he was by camping in the cornfields of Indiana with
nary a stream of clean water nearby.

"I just take it a day at a time. I never know if I'll make it to
the next day," George says. "I don't have that sense of having to
get somewhere. The present moment is what's important.

"Arriving at a shrine isn't my great accomplishment," he adds.
"It just gives me some direction."

Finding direction, seeking a closer relationship with God, is
what started George on these pilgrimages 35 years ago. It's what
continues to drive him.

"My calling," George says, "is to walk and to pray."

He will go just about anywhere to reach his next destination.

* * * * * * *

South Chicago, much like Pittsburgh and the Alle-Kiski Valley,
was once a bustling industrial hub, with steel mills providing
a large economic boon there. And, like Pittsburgh, the closing of
the mills, in particular the United States Steel South Works in
1992, proved to be devastating to South Chicago.

The community crumbled. Poverty struck. Crime followed.

Locals know better than to venture into certain parts of the
city. The Rev. Gus Milon knew better, but he didn't care.

Milon was no stranger to tough neighborhoods. He operated a teen
ministry in McKees Rocks for years during the 1970s and early
'80s. George began working with Milon, and, during that time, the
two developed a close relationship.

In the early 1980s, though, Milon was moved to North Chicago by
his Franciscan order, the Assumption Province of Franklin, Wis.
A few years later, he moved on his own to South Chicago and began
working with the poor.

He opened a soup kitchen. The first day, George helped his friend
serve about 30 meals. Within a month, Milon was feeding 300
mouths.

Today, he operates The Port Ministries on South Hermitage Avenue
between 50th and 51st streets in South Chicago. The Port consists
of five buildings, including an old school that serves as
a shelter for 65 women and children of Hispanic descent.

Another building holds a free clinic where the women and children
come to learn English and get an education. A bank vacated one of
the buildings that Milon now uses and regularly donates $20,000
each year to the ministry.

It had been years since George last saw Milon. Seeing his old
friend was a priority on this pilgrimage. Having George speak at
his annual fund-raiser was a must for Milon.

To get there, George walked through neighborhoods that make parts
of the Valley look like Sesame Street.

"Those streets go on forever," George said with a smile. "One
black fella selling T-shirts asked me to bless his store."

George did. He gave the man one of his Miraculous Medals,
a silver coin on which the Virgin Mary's face is engraved. They
were blessed at Marytown and given to George to hand out to
strangers at his discretion on his walk.

The ongoing joke between George and Milon was that if George
hadn't arrived by nightfall June 5, Milon was sending out
a search party to find him.

It wasn't necessary. George, with his uncanny knack, arrived half
an hour early.

* * * * * * *

George finishes the remaining water in his bottle, fishes through
one of his three sacks, pulls out a map and business card and
begins eyeing the distance to Marytown.

The business card has markings on the back, with a line every
half an inch or so and writing.

One mile. Two miles. Three miles. Four miles.

It's about noon on June 14.

"Only a few more hours," George says. "I should get to Marytown
around 4 o'clock."

The suburban sidewalks under the canopy of trees in Lake Forest
give way to a gravel bike path in Libertyville. Countless bikers
and walkers pass George. Only Valerie Goranson of Libertyville
stops him.

"Is this something religious?" the red-haired Goranson says,
straddling her mountain bike and wearing sunglasses, shorts,
a tank top and hat.

The two engage in a short conversation. Goranson fires question
after question at George. He offers answers and little more in
return.

Moments later, they part ways, but not before George hands her
a Miraculous Medal.

Goranson, a fourth-grade teacher in the Waukegan School District
in nearby Waukegan, Ill., puts it in her pocket with a skeptical
look. It turns out she is an atheist.

"But I'm open to all kinds of stories," she says. "I talk to
everyone I meet."

"Maybe with Mary's help," George says later, "she'll be led to
the truth."

By 4:30 p.m., George is still walking, still more than a mile
away, having made perhaps the first estimation error of his trip.
But he is close. And by 5:50 p.m., George makes a right turn onto
Marytown's driveway.

Marytown's friary resembles buildings found on a college campus:
red brick, white accents, professionally manicured grounds. The
red-brick chapel, with its towering white pillars at the
entrance, is attached to the friary.

A white statue of Mary sits at the foot of the chapel, situated
slightly to the right, near an entrance to the friary.

Concrete above the chapel's pillars is engraved in Latin script:
Christvs Regnat Venite Adoremvs -- Christ reigns, come let us
adore him.

George gently scales each step leading to the entrance, enters
the chapel's foyer, where hundreds of candles illuminate and heat
the room, and immediately removes his hat. He walks respectfully
into the chapel, sets his staff against the back wall and places
his three sacks on a pew below. He unbuckles the straps of his
sandals, steps out of them and removes his socks.

Barefoot, George looks around. He is carrying his Bible, still in
its black leather case. He begins walking down the aisle.

Behind him, above the entrance, more Latin script reads: Ex
Altari Tvo Domine, Christvm Svmimvs in Qvem cor et caro nostra
exvltant -- From your altar, oh Lord, highest Christ, in whom our
heart and flesh rejoice.

Before him, above the altar, near the ceiling, Latin engraving
reads: Regem Adoremvs Dominantem Gentibvs -- We adore Christ the
Lord of Nations.

Directly below is the centerpiece of the chapel. The perpetual
adoration monstrance, a 5-foot, 2-inch mostly gold object, holds
a large Eucharistic host. Catholics believe that the Eucharist,
which they receive during the sacrament of communion, represents
the body of Jesus Christ. It is one of the holiest aspects of
Catholicism.

Three steps with Sanctvs, or holy, engraved on each lead to
a shrine-like monument with eight marble pillars that encompasses
the monstrance. Inside the pillars is written Eternvm Sanctissima
Caro Christi -- Most holy body of Christ.

To each of George's sides are towering white marble pillars that
reach out to form arches between them. Ten-foot-high mosaics
comprised of tiny bits of smooth, painted stone decorate the
walls outside the pillars. Above the pillars are 15 more murals,
many depicting scenes of Jesus. Arched stained-glass windows
above those murals light the top half of the spectacular chapel.

George walks halfway down the aisle. He stops, kneels, then bows,
lowering his head toward the blue and salmon marble floor. He
stays there for about a minute.

Several people in the chapel are praying. All is silent.

George rises, shuffles into a pew on the left and sits. Still
sweating from walking in intense heat, he wipes his eyes, ears
and balding head.

After five minutes, bells chime signifying six o'clock. George
stands, exits the pew and opens a door leading to a hallway to
the friary.

Eighteen friars make Marytown their home, as do five live-in
volunteers who work at the retreat center for about a year before
returning to their everyday lives.

George is in search of Brother Hugo Lamm, a friar who once served
at a friary in Carey, Ohio, which George visited earlier this
trip. Lamm, George hopes, will welcome him with a warm meal and
comfy room with all the amenities that he usually goes without.

After Renee Lesko, an engaging receptionist at Marytown, finds
Lamm, George and the friar exchange greetings. George briefly
explains his pilgrimage. Lamm says there is enough room for
George to stay at the friary for two days, one shy of what George
had hoped for, and he leads the weary pilgrim to his third-floor
quarters, Room 339. It has two beds, carpeted floors and
a private bathroom.

"You have your choice of beds," Lamm jokes.

"Oh, I'll just sleep on the floor," George says. "I'm more
comfortable there."

Lamm looks at George curiously. He fingers a schedule detailing
Masses and meals, then leads George downstairs to the cafeteria,
where George devours a trayful of ham, potatoes, broccoli, bread
slices, cheese chunks and strawberry yogurt.

Afterward, he tours a museum dedicated to St. Maximilian Kolbe in
the basement of the retreat center. Two and a half hours after
arriving at Marytown, George is ready to collapse.

He leaves the museum, heads down a dark hallway and boards an
elevator that eventually leads him to his sanctuary and a good
night's rest on its carpeted floors.

* * * * * * *

George most identifies with John the Baptist in the Bible, but he
patterns his life as a pilgrim after St. Francis of Assisi.

St. Francis was a pilgrim. Founder of the Franciscan Order, he
devoted much of his life to rebuilding dilapidated churches and
caring for the poor. He raised the poverty of Christ as an
example by which his followers should live.

John the Baptist preached the message of repentance in an attempt
to prepare people for the coming of Jesus.

"People had to leave their homes in the city to go out into the
desert to hear him," George says of John the Baptist. "He was
pointing the way to Jesus. He eventually said, 'There goes the
Lamb of God, who takes away the sins of the world.' That's what
I'm doing. I'm lifting up Jesus on the cross."

It's a life not many can identify with. Even those who make their
faith their profession.

"I'm not saying it's wrong; it's not my way of (serving God),"
Lamm said. "It's a different lifestyle. It's his way."

George is something of an enigma, a man who views the world in
a way that not even the religious always comprehend. Ask him
a question, and he will answer. But look deep into his eyes, and
it is difficult to believe there is not more behind his response.

He goes hours without saying a word, only serving to further the
mystery that is George Walter. During pilgrimages, he rests in
the shade cast by large trees, sometimes staring at leaves
blowing in the wind, other times gazing into the distance.

It's impossible not to wonder what he is thinking, to wonder if
he sees the same trees, the same leaves, as others, to question
if he holds a greater appreciation for such things in nature than
common folks do.

"A lot of people are very critical of him because of the way he
looks," said Rose Stegman, a long-time friend who works at St.
Mary's School. "I've heard people around here call him a bum.

"They don't understand the heart and soul of his commitment to
God."

That includes priests. George has been invited to speak at many
church functions across the country, but he knows priests and
religious types who don't approve of him or his ways.

"I would speculate that one of the reasons for that is when we
see people doing holy things, we get a little intimidated and
feel guilty," said former Diocese of Greensburg Bishop Anthony
Bosco, a good friend of George's. " 'Look at what he's doing and
look at me.' I think it makes them uncomfortable.

"He found his niche. Not everyone can live this way, nor should
they. He does this because he is sure this is what God wants him
to do. He doesn't do this because it is neat."

But he will continue doing it. Many of his friends, even his
brother Tom, believe that George's body eventually will not allow
him to continue walking, forcing him to adopt a seminary-type
life. But George remains unwavered in his calling.

"I promised the Lord I would walk until he comes again or until
he takes me home," he said. "Whichever comes first."

"He was meant to stop here"

Linda Randles knows pain. She knows suffering.

She has been lost, floating aimlessly while dealing with the
tragedy of her teenage daughter's death from an automobile
accident in 1996.

It has taken her all of the eight years since the crash to put
her life back together, to cope with the what ifs, to find
solace.

Hours before the accident, her daughter avoided a near accident
along Routes 22 and 40 a few miles outside of Zanesville, Ohio.
She was shaken. She was so scared that she convinced herself that
she could not continue driving. And so she did the only thing she
could thing of -- she called her mother.

But for all her persuading, Linda could not talk her daughter
into getting back behind the wheel. And moments later, when her
daughter's friends, who had been drinking earlier in the evening,
arrived, Linda could not talk her daughter out of getting in
their car.

Everything would be fine, Linda was told. Don't worry, her
daughter insisted.

It was the last time she talked to her daughter.

Linda slipped into depression, endured a divorce and fought to
keep her sanity.

Then, she says, she found Jesus. And in her heart, Linda knows --
she just knows -- that she was meant to meet George.

At about 6 p.m. on Aug. 19, along Routes 22 and 40, about three
miles east of Zanesville, Linda passes George, makes a left turn
into a gas station parking lot and motions for him to approach.

Having reached his intended destination, the Sonora area of Ohio,
George is ready for a rest and a meal. He had been given
McDonald's gift certificates days earlier, and, with a McDonald's
just ahead on the left, he planned to make good use of them.

But with a friendly face beckoning, George sets aside his hunger
and aching feet to strike up a conversation.

Linda is a meek-looking woman with long, blond hair and dark
glasses. She looks even meeker as she sits in her car and
explains to George that she recently found Jesus. She has taken
to writing and singing songs in his name, Linda says, and that
when she saw George, she knew he was a deeply religious man and
just had to stop.

George smiles warmly and offers a brief account of his
pilgrimages. After a few minutes, Linda pulls out a $20 bill and
hands it to George. He graciously accepts. As George is about to
move on, Linda extends an offer for him to stay the night at her
mobile home. With dark clouds rolling in overhead and tornado
watches issued for the region, George accepts.

Linda's home rests atop a small hill across the street from
a cornfield not far from where she approached George. It's
a relatively secluded section of Sonora. Upon arriving at her
home, Linda questions where George normally sleeps. He explains
that he carries a tent and that he is quite comfortable in it.

As the sky darkens and winds pick up, Linda's internal tug-of-war
weighing the risks of inviting a stranger into her house for the
night against her Christian intuition to help a seemingly
peaceful man in need boils to the surface.

"Are you sure you'll be all right out there?" Linda asks. "I
would like to invite you inside but ..."

Her voice trails off.

George insists on pitching his tent in the front yard. With
spectacular lightning flashing in the distance, he quickly erects
his fragile shelter before shuffling off to McDonald's to silence
the thunder in his belly.

The expected rain still has not begun falling by the time George
pulls open the door and enters McDonald's.

The restaurant is almost empty. George approaches the counter,
stares overhead at a lighted menu and orders a Caesar's salad,
large French fries, orange juice and a large hot tea.

The teenage boy entering George's order does not appear to be
fazed by the man who stands before him.

"That all?" he asks.

"That's all," George answers as he sets his gift certificates on
the shiny, silver counter.

Traffic through the restaurant picks up slightly after George
sits at a booth to eat. Several young children, hell-bent on
beating the others inside, escape their mothers' grasps at the
entrance and dart for the counter. En route, they catch a glimpse
of George and snap their heads around to get another look. But,
like children do, they continue on, undeterred, toward the
counter to win the race.

George returns to Linda Randles' home, pulls out a book that she
had given him to read and sits on a wooden bench facing the home.
He begins flipping through the pages when Linda emerges from the
home.

"You're back," she says gleefully.

She walks over to George, who makes room for her on the bench.
She sits. George attempts to persuade her to sing one of the
songs she has written, and after a short debate, she agrees. She
enters her home and moments later returns with a radio and
cassette. She inserts the cassette, pushes play and soft music
begins playing.

Linda begins swaying back and forth to the gentle beat. George
rises from the wooden bench and walks toward Linda.

She begins singing.

It's a song about waiting for Jesus. The song, Linda says, earned
her an opportunity at a national recording contract. But the
record company told Linda that it wanted only her, not her
husband, who sang backup. Before Linda could even discuss the
issue with her husband, he became irate. Their relationship
quickly deteriorated, and then their daughter died.

Despite efforts to reconcile, Linda says, they finally divorced.

The music still plays, though Linda no longer is singing or
swaying. Sorrow fills her face. George's presence has brought her
so much joy, but it's also caused plenty of heartache.

It's nearing 8:30 p.m. It's dark. Thunder begins rolling in the
distance.

Linda works the overnight shift at the nearby Wal-Mart in
Zanesville and must be going.

"Could I ask you to pray over me before I leave?" Linda asks
George.

"Yes, of course," he answers.

George steps to Linda. She bows her head, forcing her hair over
her face. George places both of his hands on her head and begins
praying.

He prays mostly for peace to envelop Linda.

When he finishes, Linda appears calmer. She hugs George, turns
and leaves. George retreats to his tent, pulls down the zipper
and retires for the night, just two weeks and 140 miles away from
home. His timing could not have been better.

"I think," Linda says, "he was meant to stop here."

* * * * * * *

All is quiet at Mount St. Macrina.

The Sisters of the Order of St. Basil the Great, who maintain the
Shrine of Our Lady of Perpetual Help at Mount St. Macrina,
scramble to complete final preparations for the 70th annual
pilgrimage here outside of Uniontown.

In a few hours, the vast grounds will serve as home to thousands
of weekend pilgrims who will converge to celebrate Mary. They
will come by all kinds of transportation: airplane, automobile,
motor home and, in George's case, by foot.

George has made this three-day festival the final destination of
his pilgrimages since returning from the Jubilee celebration in
Jerusalem in 2001, and he will do so again today.

It's nearing noon on Sept. 3. The sun shines brightly as
temperatures reach into the low 80s. Labor Day, the unofficial
close to summer, is just three days away, as is the official end
to George's pilgrimage.

George, who planned to arrive at Mount St. Macrina at about noon,
is right on time. He is followed by his good friend, Connie
Beatty of Oakmont, who is filming George's trek from Footedale,
a few miles away, to Mount St. Macrina.

George climbs the twisting, inclined, black-topped driveway that
leads to various shrines, three of which he visits briefly.

Despite walking for the past 131 days, despite traveling about
1,350 miles, George is refreshed. He built more rest days into
this pilgrimage than he typically does, and having turned 63 on
July 25, his body approves of the decision.

Still, the road has been long, and today's heat is taking a toll
on George, as evidenced by the beads of sweat that form on his
face and head just moments after he wipes others away. As has
become customary, the first night of this celebration figures to
be a late one. George has become known around these parts, and
it's not unusual for dozens of people to wait in line for him to
pray over them.

It proves to be a draining task, one that George embraces, but he
knows he will need a nap to complete it.

So he weaves his way through Mount St. Macrina, still on the
driveway, en route to his modest living quarters: a cot in
a small room that he will share with another man above a large
garage.

He is a 50 yards away when he spots a familiar face outside
a cottage.

An elderly nun, Sister Martha Elizabeth Moyta, of St. Petersburg,
Fla., smiles and greets George in Slavonic, the liturgical
language of the Russian Orthodox Church. Unfazed, and without
hesitation to translate, George answers in Slavonic.

Later, George explains that Sister Elizabeth, as he knows her,
said, "Praise be Jesus Christ," to which he answered, "Forever
and ever. Amen."

The two hug.

Another woman, Susan Collis, who flew from Anaheim, Calif., to
attend the festival, emerges from the cottage and also welcomes
George. The two met last year during Collis' initial visit to
Mount St. Macrina. Collis immediately took to George and remains
fascinated by him.

"Some people will flock to him, and some people will not look at
him for fear of exposing their inner selves," Collis says after
George moves onto his room. "When you see someone like George,
you're forced to look at yourself."

George says he is simply the tool that God uses to help
disseminate the Holy Spirit in people's lives.

"It's not me," he says. "It's God's love that touches their
hearts."

George walks behind the garage and removes his robe, fanning it
out to dry. A cozy cot and afternoon nap await. After awaking and
showering, he will go to confession, the ceremonial start to the
pilgrimage celebration.

Three days later, the celebration, and George's journey, will
end.

George has been here before. Middle Road. Hampton Township. It's
home.

It's become tradition for George to end his pilgrimages by
receiving a ride from Mount St. Macrina to the intersection of
Route 8 and Middle Road. From there, he walks 3.5 miles up the
steep, snaking road to St. Mary of the Assumption, where he rests
during the fall and winter months in preparation for his next
pilgrimage. Next year, he plans to walk the British Isles.

For now, he'll be content climbing this narrow section of Middle
Road.

Many drivers turning the corners don't see George until the last
moment before braking and swerving around him. Some yell rude
comments as they pass. John Crooks is not one of them.

The Hampton resident, who has seen George because his wife and
children attend Mass at St. Mary, is parked at the entrance to
a housing plan a half-mile from Route 8. He passed George once,
turned around and now is waiting for him.

Crooks, who recently finished work, usually drives a Christian
co-worker around town. Today, he left without him. "I'm feeling
guilty all the way home," he says. "I can't pass up two
Christians in the same day."

When George slowly approaches, Crooks calls for him. "Pilgrim
George!"

George turns and smiles. He walks toward Crooks' truck.

Crooks explains that he has seen George before and wants to offer
him a ride.

"What do you think?" George says with a smile.

"I would think yes, but ..." Crooks says.

George thanks him and wishes him well, then returns to his climb.
It's much less taxing than it was last year, when he suffered
a hernia during his pilgrimage but completed it anyway.

An hour and a half later, after passing his maternal
grandparents' old house, the one in which his mother was raised,
George spies the sign for St. Mary.

Ray and Joanne Stein, who were the last ones to leave George's
side when he left on his pilgrimage 134 days ago, welcome him
home on a large lawn by the church's entrance.

"Praise the Lord, Raymond," George says enthusiastically.

"Now and forever, George," Ray answers.

They hug.

"It seems like just a few days ago you said goodbye," George
says.

"It seems longer than that for me," Ray says.

"Praise the Lord, Joanne," George says as Joanne walks toward
him.

"Praise the Lord, George," she says. "Welcome back. Safe
journey?"

"One piece," George says.

The Steins and Connie Beatty, who drove George from Mount St.
Macrina to Middle Road and still is filming him, escort him to
the church and detail the demolition of George's home, a small
basement room in St. Mary's old rectory, a few weeks earlier.

George knew that he might return from his pilgrimage to nothing.
After a short while, staring at the site of his old home, he
begins assembling his tent over a hill from the church.

It matters little to him where he stays, what belongings he
carries, what others think of him. He commonly -- unemotionally
-- says that pilgrims like himself have no home on Earth, that
his true home is in heaven with God.

Night has fallen. The sound of chirping crickets fills the air as
George finishes assembling his tent.

He always will feel a deep, unconditional commitment to God, to
plan and complete pilgrimages every summer in the name of Jesus.
He will continue doing what he does as long as he is able.

His pilgrimage is over, but his journey is never ending.

He is a lifelong pilgrim on an eternal pilgrimage to God.
Additional Information: After the pilgrimage ...

George remains in his tent, albeit a larger one, over the hill
from St. Mary of the Assumption, still without concrete plans for
the approaching winter.

The possibility exists that George could stay at St. Mary's
rectory, but the parish that he has called home since he was
a child is waiting to receive permission from the Diocese of
Pittsburgh to extend such an invitation.

He weathered the Sept. 17 storm that dumped record rainfall over
western Pennsylvania in his now four-person tent before staying
the night in the rectory at the request of the pastor.

He came about the new tent when his friend, Rose Stegman, who
works at St. Mary, saw an advertisement selling it for $50. Asked
how he purchased it, George says, 'The Lord took care of it.'

Since returning, George calculated that he covered 1,378 miles
and walked 121 of the 134-day pilgrimage. He was approached by
strangers 481 times and stopped by police on 22 occasions. He
attended Mass in 22 different churches and visited nine libraries
to check his e-mail account, pilgrimgeorge@yahoo.com.

For now, he waits patiently. He has received invitations to stay
at friends' houses but prefers to stay in his tent and sleeping
bag, which offers protection down to 40 degrees.

'Whenever the snow comes, that's the timetable (to find
a different shelter),' George says. 'It's in (God's) hands now.'

-- Rob Amen
