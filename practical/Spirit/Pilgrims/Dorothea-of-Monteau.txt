

### Dorota z Mątowów
    - Dorothea of Montau
    - CELTA help

### Life
    - Feast Day June 25
    - Born: Mątowy Wielkie (Groß Montau). 
    - Died: Kwidzyn        (Marienwerder)

### Pilgrimages
    - Made Pilgrimages to: Cologne, Aachen, Einsiedlen, Rome
    - also: Koszalin
    - Aachen: Together with her husband, Dorota visited Aachen in
      1384 to commemorate the relics of the Lord's Passion.
    - Einsiedeln: famous shrine of Our Lady in Einsiedeln. Their
      stay made such a strong impression on them that they sold
      the workshop and house and went on a new pilgrimage
      (1385-1387). 
        - It lasted two years and ran amid great inconveniences
          and dangers from highwaymen. They arrived exhausted and
          robbed of their possessions.
    - Rome: Finally, in the years 1389-1390 she made a pilgrimage
      to Rome. Hundreds of kilometers, nights spent watching and
      contemplating, and the ecstasy experienced in the shrines
      she visited left her in the Eternal City exhausted. 
        - She spent several weeks there, plagued by a mysterious
          disease, spiritual and happy. When she returned to
          Gdańsk in May 1390, her husband was already dead.

### Eucharist
    - Dorothy told her confessors about visions she had. About
      the words Jesus spoke to her. She spoke in such a way that
      she delighted eminent theologians. 
    - She found them only among the canons in Kwidzyn. When she
      lived in Gdańsk, priests shouted at her from the pulpit.
      Most Gdańsk priests did not like the fact that Dorota
      wanted _Holy Communion every day_. 
    - And he refers to the wish of the Lord Jesus. Back then,
      people received Communion rarely: according to the Teutonic
      tradition, the most pious seven times a year.

    - No wonder then that after her husband's death she settled
      in Kwidzyn and submitted to the spiritual direction of Jan
      of Kwidzyn, who had previously been a professor of theology
      at the University of Prague. 
    - She revealed her revelations and mystical experiences,
      ecstasies. He then described an event called a change of
      heart: It took place in 1385, during prayers at the Church
      of the Blessed Virgin Mary in Gdańsk. 
    - During the church ceremony, Dorota, kneeling in the crowd,
      saw Christ who approached her and took her heart, and gave
      her a new, hot and filling her with great joy and love for
      God. 
    - "In the description of Jan of Kwidzyn, this fact is treated
      as a miraculous event, consisting in the physical exchange
      of heart. Undoubtedly, the change of heart in Blessed
      Dorota was the beginning of even more intense mystical
      experiences.

### Cell
    - There were three windows in Dorota's cell. 
    - One towards the church through which she received Communion
      daily. 
    - The other towards the sky, through which she folded her
      hands. 
    - And the third, towards the cemetery, through which she
      talked to people who came to her. 
    - They traveled far to see her to consult them on difficult
      matters. And they walked away deeply impressed with what
      they heard. By closing herself to people in a cell, she
      inexplicably opened herself to them. 

### Free Spirit
    - In 1391, Dorothea was accused of being a part of the Free
      Spirit movement. Her irregular behavior at Mass attracted
      attention because she was taken up in ecstasy in
      anticipation of receiving the Eucharist, and she often
      failed to stand during the elevation of the host. 
    - Although the charge of heresy was dropped, it became
      obvious that she needed a confessor to guide and counsel
      her. With the help of Johannes Marienwerder, she secured
      permission to build and dwell in an anchoress' cell.

### Mass
    - ... it suggests that, for Dorothea, singing is not just
      a devotional exercise like praying or fasting, but becomes
      a hallmark of her raptures: the fact that she breaks into
      loud song and _laughter_ during mass, especially during the
      canon, causes offence and distaste to those observing her.
    - We are not told what these songs are, only that they
      constitute an expression of the joy of connubial rapture,
      so that Johannes Marienwerder again constructs an implied
      contrast between the response of the historical ‘audience’
      in gdańsk, who condemn Dorothea’s disruptive behaviour, and
      the initiated who are able to see her singing as a sign of
      special grace

### Traveller
    - As well early examples of Dorothea’s retreat into a corner
      and withdrawal from society being signs of her special
      grace, the Vita offers a different paradigm in recording
      her activity as a traveller. 
    - When Dorothea defies convention by setting out on several
      journeys of pilgrimage across Europe, these accounts again
      highlight a tension: as others, especially Margery Kempe,
      observe, Dorothea is unusual in being able to swap the life
      of a well-to-do townswoman for that of a pilgrim.
    - Economically, these journeys are possible only because she
      is able to gain the support of her husband. However, this
      greater freedom is also reinterpreted as the need to follow
      a spiritual calling, despite the conflict which this causes
      between the social expectations which others, in particular
      her husband, have of her and her desire to follow G-d even
      at the risk of social exclusion.


### Communion
    - It is this focus on the Eucharist and, equally
      characteristically, on confession as preparation necessary
      to prevent unworthy reception of the eucharist, which may
      be the key to the special status of Johannes
      Marien-werder’s accounts of Dorothea’s spiritual life. 
    - By highlighting her particular devotion to the eucharist,
      he bridges the seemingly unbridgeable gap between the
      charismatic mystic and an everyday lay reader, because
      communion and a reflection on the significance of communion
      form a prominent feature of lay spirituality in the towns
      of east-central europe.
