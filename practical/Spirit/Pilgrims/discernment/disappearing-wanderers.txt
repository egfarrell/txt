The wandering eccentric, the holy fool, or the traveling scholar

You're right, they have largely vanished. The reasons are
a complex tapestry of social, technological, economic, and
philosophical changes.

Here’s a breakdown of why this tradition has faded:

### 1. The Rise of the Modern State and Bureaucracy

In the past, a person's identity was local and relational. You
were "John from the village of Oakhaven." There were no
passports, social security numbers, or credit scores. The world
was more porous.

Identification and Control: Today, to exist in society, you need
to be documented. You need a fixed address for a bank account,
a job, a driver's license, and social services. A true wanderer
is "off the grid," a status that is now seen as suspicious and is
actively difficult to maintain. Vagrancy laws, while less common
now, historically criminalized this very lifestyle.

Borders and Sovereignty: A wandering monk in 800 AD could walk
from Ireland to Italy, passing through dozens of petty kingdoms,
often finding welcome in monasteries along the way. Today, that
journey requires crossing multiple heavily-controlled
international borders, each requiring a passport and a legitimate
reason for entry.

### 2. The Transformation of the Economy

The economic logic of the past was entirely different.
Hospitality, barter, and patronage were viable means of survival
for a wanderer.

Cashless to Cash-Based: A wandering pilgrim could often rely on
the Christian duty of hospitality, receiving a meal and a bed in
a monastery or a village home in exchange for stories, news, or
blessings. Today's world is overwhelmingly transactional and
cash-based. You can't easily barter for a motel room or a bus
ticket.

The End of Patronage: Wandering scholars were often supported by
a network of patrons—nobles, bishops, or wealthy merchants—who
valued their knowledge and skills. This system has been replaced
by institutionalized academia and research grants, which require
affiliation and stability.

The Stigma of Non-Productivity: In our hyper-capitalist society,
a person's value is often tied to their economic productivity and
career. Someone who intentionally eschews work and possessions to
wander is often viewed not as holy or eccentric, but as lazy,
irresponsible, or a "drain on the system."

### 3. The Information Revolution

Much of the wanderer's social function was to be a conduit for
information. They were the original internet.

Knowledge Was Geographic: Before the printing press, and even for
centuries after, knowledge was physically located in specific
places. A scholar had to go to the library in Alexandria or the
university in Bologna. A monk had to travel to a specific
monastery to study under a famous abbot. Today, virtually the
entire store of human knowledge is accessible on a phone.

News Traveled by Foot: Pilgrims and travelers were a primary
source of news from the outside world. They brought tales from
distant cities, warnings of war or famine, and new ideas. This
function has been made utterly obsolete by 24-hour news and
instant global communication.

### 4. Changes in Social and Psychological Norms

Our understanding of and tolerance for eccentricity has
fundamentally changed.

Medicalization of Eccentricity: A figure like a "Fool for
Christ," who engages in bizarre public behavior to shake people
from their complacency, would today likely be met with a mental
health intervention. What was once seen as a spiritual state is
now often viewed through a clinical lens.

The Cult of Stability: Modern life venerates stability: a steady
job, a mortgage, a family, a retirement plan. The wanderer
represents the antithesis of this ideal. They are a figure of
instability, and our society is deeply uncomfortable with that.

Decline in Communal Trust: In a more tightly-knit, religious
society, a stranger might be viewed with caution, but also as
a potential guest or messenger from God. In our more atomized,
secular, and "stranger danger" conscious society, an unknown
wanderer is far more likely to be met with fear and suspicion
than an open door.

### So, Where Have the Wanderers Gone?

The impulse hasn't vanished entirely, but it has been
transformed, tamed, and channeled into new, more socially
acceptable forms. The modern "wanderers" are:

The Through-Hiker: Hiking the Appalachian Trail or the Pacific
Crest Trail is a kind of secular pilgrimage. It's a temporary,
highly structured, and equipment-intensive journey with a clear
beginning and end.

The Backpacker and Digital Nomad: These are often young people
exploring the world between life stages or knowledge-workers who
have monetized a wandering lifestyle. They are still tied to the
grid through bank accounts, visas, and Wi-Fi.

The "Van Life" Community: This is a modern counter-cultural
movement rejecting fixed housing, but it's heavily reliant on
technology, vehicles, and often, a source of remote income. It's
a curated, Instagram-friendly version of wandering.

The Modern Pilgrim: Pilgrimages like the Camino de Santiago in
Spain are more popular than ever. But they are now well-organized
experiences with guidebooks, apps, and a network of designated
hostels (albergues). It's wandering with a safety net.

The Tragically Unhoused: This is the dark, un-romantic side of
the coin. Many of the people who would have been wandering
beggars or eccentrics in the past are now the homeless, often
struggling with mental illness and addiction, and trapped by the
very bureaucratic systems that have no place for them. They are
the wanderers stripped of all spiritual or romantic gloss.

In conclusion, the world that created and sustained the wandering
holy fool or scholar is gone. We have traded a world of porous
borders, communal hospitality, and localized knowledge for one of
globalized information, bureaucratic control, and transactional
relationships. While we have gained immense convenience and
safety, we may have lost a space for a certain kind of radical,
untethered human experience.
