post-gazette.com Obituary: George F. Walter, solo pilgrim who
walked through 40 countries Janice Crompton

- George F. Walter was a beautiful soul with an angelic spirit.

- How else could you describe a man who spent most of his adult
  life trekking more than 40,000 miles through 40 countries on
  a Christian pilgrimage, spreading a message of peace and love?

- He eschewed worldly possessions and the trappings of modern
  life, choosing instead to live off the land without a paying
  job, depending on the kindness of others.

- "I have no regular income. People are led to give me money. But
  I would rather be given a sandwich or a place to sleep, " he
  said in a 1982 story in The Pittsburgh Press.

- Mr. Walter cut a distinct, unforgettable figure along the
  roadside with his long white beard and flowing robe of patched
  denim, replete with sandals cobbled together with rubber from
  old tires and discarded nylon straps and a staff topped with
  a crucifix.

- “People ask me if I'm walking for my health and I say, ‘No, I'm
  walking for the Lord,’” Mr. Walter said in The Press story.

- The Hampton native even constructed his own coffin of cherry,
  hickory and black walnut, which will no doubt serve as
  a fitting final resting place for Mr. Walter, who died Sept. 17
  after declining dialysis for kidney failure. He was 82.

- The oldest of three boys, he attended grade school at St. Mary
  Church of the Assumption school, which straddles the border of
  Indiana and Hampton townships.

- At 14, he left home for St. Gregory Seminary High School in
  Cincinnati.

- As a young man, those around him already sensed that Mr. Walter
  was special — perhaps meant for a different life.

- “Even as a young person, there was something about him. He was
  very calm, very peaceful,” said his longtime friend Arlene
  Nath, of Indiana Township, in a family obituary written
  recently by former Pittsburgh Press reporter Deborah Deasy at
  Mr. Walter’s request. “The love of the Lord shown through him
  even before I knew what that meant.”

- “I always remember him being religious,” said his brother
  Thomas, of Albany, N.Y. “In the summertime, he worked as
  a counselor at a stayover camp for the [Catholic Diocese of
  Pittsburgh].”

- After graduating from the Athenaeum of Ohio: Mount St. Mary’s
  Seminary & School of Theology in Cincinnati in 1963 with
  a bachelor 's degree in philosophy, Mr. Walter studied for four
  years at St. Vincent Seminary in Latrobe, where he earned
  a divinity degree.

- On the brink of ordination into the priesthood in 1967, he had
  second thoughts — finding himself hungering for “a real
  experience of God,” and took a leave of absence for a year.

- “I just dropped out…and went to the West Coast,” he explained
  in the 1982 Press story.

- The following year, he was in Colorado, looking up at the
  majestic Rocky Mountains when he was struck by the natural
  beauty that surrounded him.

- “I realized one day that man didn’t make these mountains or
  these trees, God made this,” he recalled in a March 2001 story
  in the Post-Gazette. “That was the first time in my life that
  God was real to me.”

- By 1970, Mr. Walter took his first walk “with the Lord,” in
  a pilgrimage from Barcelona, Spain, to the Holy Land in
  Jerusalem, with $150 in his pocket. When he returned a year
  later, he still had $10 left.

- During the decades that followed, he found himself welcomed in
  some quarters — such as when he was invited to stay at
  a Turkish peasant’s hut — and faced hardship in others,
  including threats with a knife and shotgun.

- Mr. Walter didn’t wander aimlessly through the world, said Ms.
  Deasy, who also wrote the 1982 Press story about him.

- “He always had a destination,” she said. “He would map a route
  and figure out where there were monasteries and shrines along
  the way where he could probably find shelter. He was very
  organized in his routes.”

- Mr. Walter inspired love from strangers and sometimes pity from
  those who mistakenly assumed he was mentally ill.

- Others aimed their rage and pent-up resentment towards religion
  — and Christianity in particular — shouting obscenities and
  worse.

- “People are confronted in a way they're not used to. Some are
  turned off. Some curse, scorn. They do all kinds of things,”
  Mr. Walter said about his effect on people.

- One driver told Mr. Walter he would have shot him if he’d had
  a gun with him. Young men attacked him in Istanbul and broke
  his walking staff.

- “They yelled, ‘This land belongs to Mohammed. The cross has no
  place here,’” he recalled in 2001.

- His roadside experiences varied by culture. In America, for
  example, a handful of people may stop him along the way to
  talk, while the majority blew by in their speeding vehicles.

- In Mexico, people would sometimes join him, singing and praying
  along the way.

- In India, he was largely ignored. But, in Siberia — the vast,
  frozen tundra in Russia — Mr. Walter could meet as many as two
  dozen people in a day.

- “That’s the most hospitable country I have walked in,” he said.

- Mr. Walter typically walked every year from May through August,
  and carried about 40 pounds of gear, including a tent, soap and
  a filter to purify the water he drank from streams.

- He secured sleeping accommodations where he found them.  In
  Norway, he stayed in a mountain cabin. In Ukraine, he was given
  a trailer in a city.

- Longtime friend Rose Stegman of O'Hara briefly joined Mr.
  Walter on several of his pilgrimages along U.S. highways.

- “He has been a great inspiration to me,” she said in the
  obituary written by Ms. Deasy. “I'm just always amazed by his
  total trust in God for everything.”

- Back home in Hampton, Mr. Walter spent many winters living in
  a 6-square-foot abandoned stone pumphouse in the woods behind
  St. Mary of the Assumption Church, spending time in prayer,
  fasting and writing about his experiences.

- But, it wasn’t an escape. It was a place to pray and better
  concentrate on his faith, Mr. Walter explained in 1982.

- “I pray for the people I’ll be meeting,” he said. “I don’t go
  into the [hut] to get away from the world.”

- Mr. Walter wrote two books: “A Pilgrim Finds the Way,” in 1988
  and “The 40 Thousand Mile Man: Odyssey of a Pilgrim,” published
  in 2019.

- Mr. Walter’s final pilgrimage came in 2013, through Ohio.

- That year, he spoke about his journeys with Dave Malarkey of
  the Butler radio station WISR.

- “He kind of struck me as a modern John the Baptist,” Mr.
  Malarkey said in the obituary by Ms. Deasy. “There’s
  a tremendous joy about him. He’s not preachy at all … I would
  say that he impresses more by example, than anything else.”

- In her obituary, Ms. Deasy wrote about how Mr. Walter spent the
  last 10 years surrounded by cornfields in a small house on the
  grounds of the former Holy Trinity Byzantine Monastery in
  Jefferson Township, Butler County.

- “He's probably the most holy man I've ever met,” said longtime
  friend the Rev. Andrew Marko of Avon Lake, Ohio, a Byzantine
  priest and World War II veteran who attended Walter's 75th
  birthday party. “He's a man of God and a man of prayer.”

- Mr. Walter’s funeral last month drew friends and well-wishers
  from across the globe, all of whom had been touched in some way
  by Mr. Walter during his travels.

- “There were people all over the world who admired him,” Ms.
  Deasy said.

- “They came from far and wide,” his brother said. “My brother
  marched to the beat of a different drummer. In his case, the
  drummer was the Lord.”

- Mr. Walter best summed up his life as a faithful wayfarer in
  the 2001 PG story about him.

- “People know I’m walking for the Lord,” he said. “Even if it’s
  a fraction of a second, if they see the cross on my staff,
  maybe that’s the only time that day they think about God.”

- Along with his brother, Mr. Walter is survived by many nieces
  and nephews.

- He was preceded in death by his brother Paul.

- Memorial donations are suggested to Blessed Trinity Academy,
  2510 Middle Road, Glenshaw PA 15116.
