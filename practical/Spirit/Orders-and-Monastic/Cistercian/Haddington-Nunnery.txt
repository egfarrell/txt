### Abbey Mill Farm - (Regional Newsletter – Spring edition 2023)
- Br Seamus Conway OCSO

- Relative to their male counterparts, not a lot is known about
  medieval female Cistercian foundations in Scotland. That is why
  it was a pleasant surprise for Sr Margaret Ann Minards HHS (a
  friend who lives near by) and I when we saw a write-up in our
  local paper the East Lothian Courier last November, about Abbey
  Mill Farm, just east of our local town, Haddington. Near the
  present-day farm buildings was once a Cistercian Nunnery, known
  in its day as St Marys; the place is the origins of our
  Cistercian Abbey here at Nunraw; we are about six miles from
  Haddington, near the village of Garvald. Nothing remains above
  ground of the nunnery that stood there from 1152 to 1579, it
  did not survive the Reformation. The only indication that
  something like it was there is its name and a large mound of
  earth, where its church was, and later a cemetery (the stones
  in the near-by buildings could be examined for previous use).

- The East Lothian Courier has it's own in-house/resident story
  teller, Tim Porteus. He told the story of the abbey in a weekly
  article he wrote in 2018. He began by saying: “Everywhere you
  go in East Lothian there are ancient monuments to the county's
  past. Whether it is prehistoric fort on top of hill,
  a tooth-embedded stone cairn from the Bronze Age or a burial
  ground, evidence of those who came before us is easy to
  uncover. Sometimes there is nothing evident to the naked eye
  and what looks like an arable field or woodland was actually
  once a site of huge importance. One such fascinating site is
  the former St Mary's establishment near Abbey Mill Farm”.

- I first got to hear about the place from our Br Barry, here, at
  Nunraw Abbey. He gave me a print-out with information about
  work a medieval historian had done on the site and presented it
  to a conference at Leeds University in 2012, on the subject of
  medieval nunneries in Britain – Dr Kimm Curran. She calculated,
  by the tax that was paid on the property, that it was up there
  with the biggest of its kind in Britain (which later became
  Britain). Kimm Curran begins by saying: “A farm in East Lothian
  could hold the secrets of one of Europe's most important
  Cistercian nunneries. Investigating the site would contribute
  to the wider understanding of the role of Scottish convents,
  but also put Scotland's nunneries in league with those on the
  continent and beyond”. Sr Margaret Ann and I have more than
  a passing interest in the place. To draw attention to the
  place, among other things, we meet once a year in a cafe in
  Haddington - we call ourselves Friends of Haddington Nunnery.
  The last get-together was extra special as we read the article
  to those who joined us.

- The article in the Courier say that Haddington's Historic
  Society have erected an information panel on the grounds of the
  farm, and are quoted saying: “The monument is of national
  importance because it has an inherent potential to make
  a significant contribution to our understanding of the past, in
  particular to medieval ecclesiastical and medieval nunneries”.
  The origins of the abbeys foundation was made possible by
  a gift of land from Countess Ada of Warenne, a Scottish
  princess, who was mother to two of Scotland's Kings. She was
  married to Prince Henry a brother of King David I. When he
  died, her two sons, in turn, became King; William the Lion, the
  youngest of the two reigned for 48 years. Margaret Tudor, aged
  13, and sister of King Henry VIII of England stayed in the
  abbey in1503 on her way to marry James IV of Scotland (she had
  her first child at 17). Her grandson, James VI of Scotland
  became the first monarch to rule over both Scotland and England
  after the death of Queen Elizabeth I. The place was involved in
  some of the most dramatic periods in the countries history,
  which is not very well known. One of those periods is known as
  A Rough Wooing – Henry VIII style!! He wanted Mary, Queen of
  Scots to be promised in marriage to his son. Henry's “request”
  was refused, he was not happy, and sent an army to enforce it.
  The French came to Mary's aid with the Scottish parliament
  meeting in the abbey, where the Treaty of Haddington was
  signed. The treaty guaranteed Mary safe passage to France and
  promised her in marriage to their King. The treaty begins by
  saying: “In the Parliament of a most excellent princess Mary,
  Queen of Scots, held in the abbey of Haddington on 7 July
  1548”. Mary was only six days old when she became queen – five
  when she fled to France – fifteen when she married the King of
  France - eighteen when she returned to Scotland – and forty
  four when she died. She was held prisoner for nearly twenty
  years before being executed by her cousin Elizabeth I in 1587
  – which resulted in the Spanish Armada attacking England a year
  later. Before her death Mary predicted, correctly, that: “In my
  end will be my beginning”.

- It would be nice to think that the work the society has begun
  will continue and that a proper survey/excavation of the sit
  can be carried out – making its history better known, locally
  and beyond. It would also be nice to think such work will
  contribute to a renewed interest in medieval Cistercian history
  - male and female. Kimm Curran has called for this to happen,
  with medieval female foundations generally; she said when this
  kind of work has been carried out in other places, local people
  have shown a lot of interest - meaning Haddington should be no
  different. Anyone wishing to visit the place and see the
  information panel will find it by going to the car park which
  is opposite the entrance to Abbey Mill Farm, from there go on
  to the river bank, the panel is a few minutes walk towards the
  buildings that are to the left.

- Historic Scotland also make mention of the fact that there
  would have been close connections between St Mary's nunnery in
  Haddington and the nearby St Martin's Kirk, saying that the
  road between the two was called Nungate – its possible even
  that the nunnery founded the Kirk? The society also says that
  the town of Haddington grew up around the Kirk. A similar story
  could apply to the church in our local village Garvald, to St
  Mary's and, now, Nunraw Tower. Without doubt there would have
  been close links between all three places – one/two being the
  cause of the other? The whole issue, topic points to an
  incredible span of time, history, which is all the more reason
  we should do all we can to draw attention to such places. If
  the church could be kept as a church, and attention be drawn to
  the links between all four places (including St Martin's). then
  that would be a good start.

- The above brings us to look further a-field, to look out the
  way from the Nunnery, to look for places that might have
  connections to the place.


### Newspaper eastlothiancourier.com

EVERY
Marie Sharp


- EVERYWHERE you go in East Lothian there are ancient monuments
  to the county’s past.

- Whether it is a prehistoric fort on top of a hill,
  a tooth-embedded stone cairn from the Bronze Age or a burial
  ground, evidence of those who came before us is easy to
  uncover.

- Sometimes there is nothing evident to the naked eye and what
  looks like an arable field or woodland was actually once a site
  of huge importance.

- One such fascinating site is the former St Mary’s Nunnery,
  established near Abbey Mill Farm, Haddington.

- The nunnery was founded by Countess Ada de Warenne, a Scottish
  princess who produced two of the nation’s kings.

- Countess Ada was married to Henry of Scotland and granted the
  privileges of Haddington and other areas in East Lothian by her
  father-in-law King David I.

- Henry died the year before his father, who made arrangements
  for the couple’s son Malcolm to take the throne when he died.

- As a result, Ada found herself ruled by her 12-year-old son,
  King Malcolm IV; then, after his untimely death at the age of
  just 24, by her younger son William the Lion, who would be
  Scotland’s longest-serving king prior to the Union of the
  Crowns in 1603.

- No longer needed in the maternal role, the still-young dowager
  countess Ada retired to her lands in Haddington and devoted her
  time to good works.

- She gave land to the Cistercian Nuns and helped found their
  convent, which was dedicated to St Mary, and is said to have
  inspired the name Nungate.

- It was one of the largest nunneries in Scotland and was
  established between 1152 and 1159. At its peak it is thought to
  have had fish ponds as well as gardens.

- The nunnery would be burned to the ground twice in the next
  four centuries by the English and closed in the early 17th
  century after the Reformation.

- Even after surviving two blazes, the nuns were forced to seek
  royal protection in 1471 amid claims the Lairds of Yester and
  Makerston “ungallantly seized their lands”.

- The last prioress was called Elizabeth Hepburn and stood her
  ground in 1548 when the English invaders came.

- Although they took the town of Haddington, the nunnery remained
  in Scottish hands and was where the Treaty of Haddington was
  signed in July that year, promising Mary Queen of Scots to
  Dauphin Francis in marriage in return for French help.

- In a twist, the nunnery was built about a kilometre east of St
  Martin’s Kirk and the road which connected the two was known as
  Nun Gait, later becoming Nungate, where John Knox, the
  architect of the Scottish Protestant Reformation, lived in the
  16th century.

- A survey of the site in April 2013 reported that, although
  there were no obvious remains of the nunnery, there was an
  L-shaped anomoly on the land south of the graveyard which still
  sits there and traces of outlying buildings.

- In 2016, Historic Environment Scotland reported it as
  a scheduled monument which it said was partly visible as
  a burial ground with a large mound next to it.

- The agency described the site as a monument of national
  importance.

- Its statement of scheduling said: 
    > The monument is of national importance because it has an
    > inherent potential to make a significant contribution to
    > our understanding of the past, in particular of medieval
    > ecclesiastical foundations and, more specifically,
    > Cistercian establishments and medieval nunneries.

    > The monument was one of the richest nunneries in Scotland,
    > with connections to lands across central Scotland and
    > pioneering land management and exploitation activities.

    > The monument is a rare survival, with high potential for
    > the good preservation of buried features and deposits,
    > including architectural remains and burials.

    > The monument is directly associated with Ada de Warenne and
    > with significant historical events such as the Rough
    > Wooing.

    > It has the potential to make a significant contribution to
    > our knowledge of the role of women in medieval religious
    > life in Scotland, particularly in relation to monasticism.

    > The loss of the monument would significantly diminish our
    > future ability to appreciate and understand nunneries in
    > Scotland and their role in respect of their local
    > economies.”
