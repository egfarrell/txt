# UNIT 1: LIFE AND TIMES

- Like any person, the time and place in which we live has
  enormous effects on our activities, attitudes, life choices,
  personality, etc.
- To understand Teresa of Avila, one of the most remarkable women
  of any century, we need to have an awareness of her life and
  times.
- Therefore we will:
    - Look at Spain in the time of St. Teresa.
    - Obtain an overview of her life and main writings.

### SPAIN IN THE TIME OF ST. TERESA

- Two monarchs, called sometimes the “Catholic Sovereigns,”
  Ferdinand (d. 1517) and Isabella (d. 1504), succeeded in
  uniting the Iberian Peninsula between 1474 and 1517. The crowns
  of Aragon and Castile were united in 1492 and Pope Alexander VI
  conferred the title “Catholic Majesty” on the king.  They were
  religious reformers and profoundly influenced political,
  religious and cultural life.  Spain developed an extensive
  empire which would last until the 19th century.  They
  instigated a profound ecclesiastical reform in which they had
  the support of popes. There was reform of dioceses and of
  religious life.  They prepared for a Golden Age of Spanish
  literature, culture, universities and spirituality which would
  flourish under their successors, the Emperor Charles V (King of
  Spain 1516-1556) and his son Philip II (1556-1598).  The term
  “Golden Age” (Siglo de Oro) is used of many aspects of Spanish
  achievement; historians do not agree on an exact dating, but
  a broad delimitation would be 1500-1700. Spain at this time
  reached some of its highest achievements, influence, power and
  prestige.  We had the achievements in art by El Greco,
  Velásquez, Murillo; in literature and theatre there are
  important figures like Cervantes author of Don Quixote; two
  religious' poets, Fray Luis de León and John of the Cross
  belong to the group of six lyric poets of the 16th and 17th
  century recognised for supreme achievement in Spanish
  literature.

- In terms of Catholic life there was much relief that the
  influence of Jews and Muslims was reduced by royal decree and
  the Inquisition established by the monarchs in 1480.    The
  Reformation, initiated by Luther’s challenge to Church
  authority on the door of Wittenberg castle church on 31 October
  1517, had little direct effect in Spain.  Teresa is quite vague
  about it, but she speaks very negatively about heretics. The
  prohibition of Philip II against Spaniards studying in
  universities abroad (1559, 1568) helped to keep heresy out but
  isolated Spain from currents of European thought until the
  eighteenth century.

- The `Alumbrados` (also known as `Illuminati` or “enlightened
  ones), were much more troublesome; they were loosely knit
  groups of spiritual persons.
    - Many were orthodox, but there were some who were
      spiritually unbalanced and had an unhealthy interest in
      visions and revelations—many of which were false.
    - The Inquisition treated them severely and many saints were
      at one time or another under suspicion, notably St.
      Ignatius Loyola in 1527, who was charged with showing them
      sympathy.

- Socially there was much poverty in Spain and uneven
  distribution of wealth and opportunities.

- A key thing to remember is that this was an exciting time for
  spirituality and Church life; there was great enthusiasm and
  energy for religious affairs.

- As you study this module keep an eye out for things Spanish;
  you may be surprised that there will be items that will tie in
  and enlighten you.

### MAIN EVENTS IN HER LIFE

- As you will have seen in the reading list, the very important
  research principle which is to try and find something short and
  simple on whatever topic you are beginning to study, before
  moving on to longer and more detailed study.
- As already noted there, in the case of Teresa you might find
  a simple pamphlet, a religious dictionary, a history of
  spirituality, a book of saints’ lives.
- This will give an initial feel and basic information. You may
  then move to something longer, such as an introduction to her
  works. What follows are the main facts about the saint.

- She was born on 28 March 1515 to Alonso and Beatriz and given
  her grandmother’s name Teresa de Ahumada.  Her grandfather was
  a merchant, a converso, that is, a Christianized Jew; he was
  publicly humiliated before the Inquisition.  At the time to
  have such a grandparent was very much to have a skeleton in the
  family cupboard.  It may well have contributed to Teresa’s
  lifelong aversion to honor and social dignity. Biographers
  speak of her pleasant features and her figure which tended to
  being plumper that thin.  She was extrovert, cheerful, friendly
  and a good conversationalist.

- She had much early piety, enjoying games with a religious
  theme. After her mother’s death in 1528 she showed a tendency
  to frivolity.  From about the age of ten she had been more and
  more drawn to tales of chivalry and romance. Her father became
  worried and sent her to the Augustinian Sisters at Avila.
  Without his permission she entered the Carmelite Monastery of
  the Incarnation at Avila at the age of 20 (1535).

- Teresa soon became ill and left the convent for treatment with
  a 'pseudo=healer' or 'charlatan' whose treatment was painful
  and nearly killed her and left her with ongoing problems of
  poor health for the rest of her life. After three years she was
  cured through the intercession of St. Joseph, who was
  a lifelong favourite saint of hers.

- In her early years of Carmelite life, she was not very fervent.
  She says that for eighteen years she was lax. In 1556-1557 she
  had a profound conversion, central to which was an image of
  Jesus being scourged at a pillar during the Passion. From then
  she made rapid progress and various mystical experiences began
  in 1558. Around that time too the question of reform arose and
  in 1562 the first convent of the Reform was opened, the
  Monastery of St. Joseph at Avila.  Seventeen more foundations
  would follow.

### MAIN WRITINGS

- The `four` main writings of the saint have different origins.

- Her confessors found her spiritual experiences difficult to
  understand and she was asked to write an account of her life in
  1561-1562; she finished its second draft in 1565.

- Books on prayer were difficult to access; many books that
  Teresa felt helpful, like Osuna’s Spiritual Alphabet, were put
  on the Index of Prohibited Books.

- She wrote her `Way of Perfection` for her sisters. Because it
  is not dated, there have been different claims concerning the
  dates on which the first and second version of this work were
  written.
    - Modern scholarship, however, is generally in agreement that
      the first version was written in 1565 or early 1566 and the
      second version not long afterwards, during 1556.

- She began an account of her reform in 1573, called `The
  Foundations`, probably at the instigation of her confessor, the
  Jesuit Jerónimo Ripalda.
    - Three years earlier in 1570 she had an apparition of the
      Lord who encouraged her to make as many foundations as
      possible and to write about them; Ripalda may just have
      encouraged her to follow this instruction.
    - She wrote perhaps nine chapters from August 1573 to March
      1574.  The rest was written sporadically until her death in
      1582.

- Her greatest work is `The Interior Castle`, begun on the feast
  of the Holy Trinity 1577 and completed on 29 November of that
  year, though she was able to work on it only for two months of
  that period.
    - It is an account of the spiritual journey to the highest
      union with God described as seven “mansions” or dwelling
      places. In the final section, Mansions 7, we find that she
      describes a state more elevated than she knew when writing
      her Life (ch. 22).

- There are also some minor or shorter works such as her
  Constitutions for the nuns, a commentary on `The Song of Songs`
  and poems.

- Teresa died in 1582.  She was canonised forty years later and
  proclaimed a doctor of the Church in 1970.

# UNIT 2: EARLY LIFE

- Now we'll look at the life of Teresa up to her conversion in
  1554. In this period, we can already see the beginnings of
  a phrase that she would make her own:
    - “`a determined determination`” (determinata determinación).
    - We also see evidence of Teresa’s human weakness and her
      long struggle to give herself totally to God’s invitations
      to love.

- Some key expressions in Spanish that you are likely to meet in

  as obligatory as in Irish or French.

- As you work through the unit be on the lookout for evidence of
  God’s grace at work in Teresa, and also indications of her
  weakness.

### EARLY CHILDHOOD

- The very first words of her autobiography set the tone of her
  childhood:
    - To have had virtuous and God-fearing parents along with the
      graces the Lord granted me should have been enough for me
      to have led a good life, if I had not been so wretched.[^1]

- Already we see a key issue in Teresian spirituality. Teresa
  often speaks of the sinfulness of her early years and of her
  offending God.
    - Scholars are not unanimous on the question of how sinful
      Teresa was during her early life. There was certainly some
      sin, but was she ever totally alienated from God—what in
      traditional theology would be called “mortal sin.” [^2]
    - When saints talk about their sin, they are not necessarily
      being scrupulous.  [Scrupulosity is a psychological
      disorder, acute anxiety about moral behaviour.]
        - But for the saints, who love God so much and are
          conscious of receiving overwhelming love and munificent
          gifts, minor failings will often be seen by them as
          a real or serious _betrayal_.
    - At the beginning she was very pious and liked playing
      religious games. Her brother Rodrigo de Cepeda and she at
      one time wanted to go off to be martyred by the Moors. The
      plan was frustrated, and Teresa wryly remarked, “Having
      parents seemed to us the greatest obstacle.” (3)  She liked
      to play at being hermits or with her playmates pretended to
      be nuns in a monastery. [^4] She was quite pious as
      a child.
    - A change began which she outlines in the second chapter of
      her life. She became distracted by books on chivalry and
      was drawn into various forms of vanity and frivolity.
        - Her half-sister, María de Cepeda, was not a good
          influence on her.  There was a period in which she went
          astray, but her father took the opportunity of her
          sister’s marriage to send her to a boarding school in
          Avila, run by Augustinian sisters. She was about
          sixteen then.

### VOCATION

- In her youth Teresa vacillated on the issue of vocation. At the
  boarding school she turned against the idea of a religious
  vocation; she did, however, greatly admire the nuns who taught
  her, [^5] in particular Doňa María Briceňo who influenced her
  greatly especially by speaking about prayer. [^6]

- She was quite confused at this time, when she was between her
  sixteenth and eighteenth years:
    > After a year and a half at the convent school I was much
    > better [spiritually]. I began to recite many vocal prayers
    > and to seek that all commend me to God so that He might
    > show me the state in which I was to serve him.  But still
    > I had no desire to be a nun, and I asked God not to give me
    > a vocation, although I also feared marriage.
    - [^7]

- In those years of struggle good people influenced her. Her
  uncle Don Pedro Sánchez de Cepeda had her read spiritual books
  to him. Being a natural 'pleaser', she pretended to like such
  books, and she admitted that they influenced her. [^8]

- An internal battle ensued. The idea of vocation battled with
  her fear of hell:
    > And although my will did not completely incline to being
    > a nun, I saw that the religious life was the best and
    > safest state, and so, little by little I decided to force
    > myself to accept it. I was engaged in this battle within
    > myself for three months, forcing myself with this
    > reasoning:
        > that the trials and hardships of being a nun could not
        > be greater than those of purgatory and that I really
        > merited hell; that it would not be so great a thing
        > while alive to live as though in purgatory; and that
        > afterward I would go directly to heaven, for that was
        > my desire.

    > And in this business of choosing a state, it seems to me
    > I was moved more by servile fear than by love. The devil
    > was suggesting that I would not be able to suffer the
    > trials of religious life, because I was too pampered.
    > I resisted this with the thought of the trials Christ
    > suffered…
            - [^9]

- As she could not get her father’s permission to enter, she
  `absconded` to the Carmelite Convent of the Incarnation in
  Avila, where a good friend of hers, Juana Suárez, had already
  entered.
    - Her motivation at this stage was clearly mixed; friendship
      and affective relationships meant a lot to her.
    - On later reflection she saw that God was employing many
      ways to turn her to himself. [^10]
    - She experienced great fulfilment in the convent. After
      taking the habit, she wrote:
        > Within an hour, He gave me such great happiness about
        > being in the religious state of life that it has never
        > left me up to this day.
        - [^11]

- And so, she would say that the vocation as a nun was a great
  divine favour.[^12] She was professed 2 November 1536.

  Teresa’s personality and temperament.

### DECLINE

- Her health soon declined, and she left the monastery for
  treatment. She was ill for three years until, as we noted, she
  healed through her intercession of St. Joseph.[^13]
    - She had great difficulty with prayer, largely perhaps
      because she had not been given appropriate help such,
      method or way or proceeding.
    - In particular she states that she did not have a talent for
      discursive thought, or for profitable use of the
      imagination:
        > God didn’t give me talent for discursive thought or for
        > a profitable use of the imagination. In fact, my
        > imagination is so dull that I never succeeded even to
        > think about or represent in my mind—as hard as
        > I tried—the humanity of the Lord.
        - [^14]

- She goes on to speak of the implications for praying for
  people, like herself (who are numerous), that cannot pray
  reflectively.
    - She gave in to a very common temptation which was to give
      up mental prayer because of her unworthiness.[^15]
        - At this time, she indulged in a dangerous friendship;
          an early special grace Teresa received was the sense
          that Jesus was reproving her for this.
        > While I was with a person, the Lord at the outset of
        > our acquaintance desired to make me understand that
        > those friendships were not proper for me and to counsel
        > me and give me advice in the midst of such thorough
        > blindness. With great severity Christ appeared before
        > me, making me understand what he regretted about the
        > friendship. I saw him with the eyes of my soul more
        > clearly than I could have with the eyes of my body.
        - [^16]

- She did not at the time understand that there could be seeing
  that was not with the eyes of her body.

- In `chapter seven` of the Life we see how various people were
  a good influences on her and tried to make her more careful and
  fervent.
    - She speaks with great clarity about the conflict within her
      when she began to pray again, but without abandoning her
      sinful actions. [^17]

- She was about eighteen years in the convent before she was
  given the grace of profound conversion in Lent 1554, when she
  was thirty-nine.
    - The occasion was prayer before an image of the wounded
      Christ (an ecce homo [see John 19:5] rather than Christ at
      the pillar being scourged).
    - She was at the time reading the Confessions of St.
      Augustine with great empathy. [^18]

### GROWTH

- After this episode, she made extremely rapid spiritual growth.
  She began to receive many mystical graces. Her term for these
  is “supernatural.”

- Note: an English dictionary is rarely helpful when one is
  reading spirituality.  Writers from different centuries,
  countries and spiritualities will use words like “supernatural,
  “mystical, “contemplation”, “meditation” etc. in quite diverse
  senses.
    - We have to be guided by the text and context of the author.
    - In practice this will mean using introductions to works,
      commentaries, notes.

- The account in `chapter nine` of initial graces prepares for
  a long discourse on prayer.
