# UNIT 7: TERESA AND PRAYER IV: THE FOUR WATERS

### Unit 7; The Aqueduct (3)

- With the third image of the Aqueduct, we very definitely enter
  into the mystical way.
    - It is the situation described by Teresa in the `fifth
      mansions` of The Interior Castle.
    - The point of this comparison is that, once the initial work
      is done in making the aqueduct, there is afterwards only
      _miniscule labour_ concerned with maintenance,

- Aqueducts are of all kinds. As the word suggests they are
  passages not for traffic but for water.
    - In Rome two thousand years ago, there were aqueducts that
      in some places were cut into the ground and that in other
      places took the form of water bridges - all depending on
      gravity as the water flowed from the Alban hills southeast
      of the city to the city itself where the users obtained
      it—with a charge, for Roman water was at one time metered.

- Teresa makes her point immediately:
    - Let us come to speak of the third water by which this
      garden is irrigated, that is, the water flowing from
      a river or spring.
    - By this means the garden is irrigated with much less
      labour, although some labour is required to direct the flow
      of the water.
    - The Lord so desires to help the gardener here that he
      himself becomes practically the gardener and the one who
      does everything. (L 16:1)

- The Waterwheel and the Aqueduct form a continuum; they are not
  clearly demarcated or totally separate.
    - The central issue in both is the role of the faculties of
      _intellect, will and memory_.
    - The prayer is asleep of the faculties: the faculties
      neither fail entirely to function nor understand how they
      function. (L 16:1)

- Here the faculties are mainly intellect and will:
    - they are not active as in the first water by reasoning,
      gathering thoughts and looking for ways to express desires,
      praise, and thanksgiving.
    - God is even more active now than in the second phase of the
      Waterwheel, and so the intellect and will are furthered
      quietened. Teresa describes their situation as “sleep.”

- Though there is not a distinct boundary between Waters Two and
  Three, Teresa gives an important indication of the difference
  when she writes:
    - The consolation, the sweetness and the delight (el gusto
      y suavidad y deleite) are incomparably greater than in the
      previous prayer…
    - This experience doesn’t seem to me to be anything else but
      an almost complete death to all earthly things, and an
      enjoyment of God. (L 16:1)

- The Spanish words, especially `gusto`, will become important
  when we come to the Fourth Mansions in The Interior Castle.

- Are the ideas in this section somehow familiar?
    - Recall the second aim of the Carmelite life in the
      Institutes of the First Monks, which you will meet in other
      modules. (mystical)
    - Here there is a totally new kind of experience that makes
      other attachments and pleasures irrelevant and at the time
      of this prayer they are, as it were, almost dead.
        - The “almost” is an important qualification.

- In what follows, be ready to mark ideas that seem new or difficult to grasp.

- Teresa returns to a most significant theme:
    - ... she is taught by God:
        - In fact, five or six years ago, the Lord gave me this
          prayer in abundance, and I didn’t understand it; nor
          did I know how to speak of it…
        - I clearly understood that it was God’s work, but
          I couldn’t understand how he was working at this stage.
        - For the truth of the matter is that the faculties are
          almost totally united with God, but not so absorbed as
          not to function.
        - I am extremely pleased now that I understand it.
          Blessed be the Lord who has so favoured me. (L 16:2)

- We can use this text to make some _important points_ that are
  relevant for all of Teresa’s writings.

- Teresa writes almost effusively, but at the same time with
  great accuracy. Note that she says this prayer is now “`in
  abundance`.”
    - It has come occasionally in the previous Water but is now
      in much greater measure.

- She has the experience but cannot understand it: Why?
    - Well, her mind, thinking and reflecting, was not at work
      during this grace, so how could she?

- Note the very common occurrence in Teresa's writing of short
  exclamations of prayer, generally of thanksgiving and wonder.

- In the 'Second Water' Teresa saw people almost afraid to move
  lest the grace vanish (L 15:1).  Now with the Aqueduct Teresa
  sees God taking over more and more.

- The faculties have only the ability to be occupied completely
  with God. It doesn’t seem that any of them dares to move, nor
  can we make them stir unless we strain to distract ourselves;
  but even then, I don’t think we could do so entirely.
    - One utters many words in praise of God without thinking
      them up, unless it is the Lord who thinks them up; at least
      the intellect is worth nothing here.
    - The soul would desire to cry out praises, and it is beside
      itself—a delightful disquiet. (L 16:3)

- Here it would require effort to use the faculties of intellect
  and will; it is easier to allow God to act.
    - O true Lord and my Glory! How delicate and extremely heavy
      a cross (delgada y pesadísima cruz) you have prepared for
      those who reach this state!
    - ‘Delicate’ because it is pleasing; ‘heavy’ because there
      come times when there is no capacity to bear it; and yet
      the soul would never want to be freed from it unless it
      were for the sake of being with you.
    - When it recalls that it hasn’t served you in anything (que
      no os ha servido en nada) and that by living it can serve
      you, it would want to carry a much heavier cross and never
      die until the end of the world.
    - It finds no rest in anything except in doing you some small
      service. It _doesn’t know what it wants_, but it well
      understands that it wants nothing other than you. (L 16:5)

- Here we have some very important themes that are found
  throughout mystical literature. You should note them in your
  reading of Teresa and in other texts that you study.
    - The mystic can only be understood as one who is _in love_,
      madly in love.
    - However, the awareness of God may be characterised
        - by _light_ and exuberance, or
        - by _darkness_ in which God is somehow perceived.
        - The words apophatic and kataphatic are often used about
          these two quite different ways of knowing or
          experiencing God. [^1]
    - Mystics want in some way to show their love for God;
      nothing is sufficient.
        - Hence, we find in many mystics a desire to suffer,
          which is not neurotic but is coming from the “crazy”
          love experienced.
        - In some way they want “to prove” their love and doing
          something painful for God seems desirable to show their
          unworthiness and God’s totally gracious and undeserved
          love.
    - Mystics think that they have done nothing for God and will
      often recall sins and state their unworthiness.
    - There is a _failure to understand_ what is happening.

- Think of times when you have said “sorry” again and again to
  a person.
    - Whilst this may be a bit of attention seeking, it can
      reflect a genuine sense of remembered failure and sorrow.
    - Think of times when you may have thought of your gift
      (perhaps at Christmas) as very poor and inadequate for the
      person you care deeply about.

- Teresa goes on to indicate the power and totality of God’s
  action here — it is totally beyond human effort and
  achievement:
    - In one of these visits the water is given without measure
      because the gardener is who he is—in truth, the creator of
      the water.
    - And what the poor soul could not achieve in about twenty
      years with its labours to bring repose to the intellect,
      this heavenly gardener accomplishes in a moment. (L 17:2)

- Teresa now tries to distinguish the Second and the Third
  Waters. She notes:
    - “In sum, the virtues are now stronger than in the previous
      prayer of quiet.” (L 17:3
    - ) Another characteristic is that in the Second Water the
      person wants to remain still, in the Third Water there is
      fruitful love:
        - Although this prayer seems entirely the same as the
          prayer of quiet, I mentioned (see L 14:2), it is
          different—partly because in the prayer of quiet the
          soul didn’t desire to move or stir, rejoicing in that
          holy idleness of Mary; and in this prayer it can also
          be Martha in such a way that it is as though engaged in
          both the active and the contemplative life together.
        - It tends to work of charity and to business affairs
          that have to do with its state in life and to reading,
          although it isn’t Master of It completely.
        - And it understands that the best part of the soul is
          somewhere also. (L 17:4)

- The person is united to God at a deeper level than the
  faculties of thinking, desiring or remembering and so outer
  actions like cooking or writing can continue without disturbing
  the grace that is being given. Then Teresa makes a vitally
  `important point`:
    - …it is one grace to _receive_ the Lord’s favour;
    - another to _understand_ which favour and grace it is and
    - a third, to know how to _describe_ and explain it.
        - (L 17:5)

- There are surely hundreds of mystics in any large city or in
  a geographical region.
    - Most will not know that they are receiving mystical graces,
      especially when these are in the dark form (apophatic).
    - They can feel isolated and alone, not daring or able to
      share their experiences.
    - In this case they can easily become discouraged and may
      frequently try to return to an earlier kind of prayer which
      they might consider “better.”
    - Teresa could have added to her third grace the charism or
      gift of writing or speaking about these graces in a way
      that will teach others in their circle (like a good
      spiritual director, leader or teacher), or like Teresa and
      Thérèse of Lisieux be teachers in and for the Church.

- As she concludes her account of the Third Water Teresa gives
  some helpful indications about imagination and memory:
    - The imagination and memory carry on such a war that the
      soul is left powerless.
    - Since the other faculties have ceased to function, these
      two are of no avail, not even for doing harm.
    - They do a great deal though by their disturbance… I don’t
      know what remedy there is for this since until now God
      hasn’t made one known to me.
    - I would be glad to find out one, for, as I say, the
      imagination and memory often torment me. (L 17:6)
    - … The only remedy I have found, after having tired myself
      out for many years, is the one I mentioned in speaking of
      the prayer of quiet:
        - to pay `no more attention to the memory` than one would
          to a madman—leave it go its way, for only God can stop
          it and, in truth, here it remains a slave. (L 17:7)

- Note here Teresa’s limited understanding “since until now God
  hasn’t made known to me [a remedy]."
    - We should never underestimate the amount of teaching that
      Teresa received from God. It is a constant refrain in her
      writings.
    - Here she puts the memory and imagination together. We
      should note, however, a broad distinction:
        - `Memory` is concerned with the past.
        - `Imagination` is creative.

- To sum up your work on this unit it would be good to reflect on
  a few points that may be clearer to you.

- Note here in the words of Archbishop Williams a point made
  earlier in this Water:
    - “The second and third waters are a continuum rather than
      two clearly distinct stages. And their subdivisions need
      some untangling.” [^2]

- What do you think?
    - What is the main point that Teresa is making about God’s
      work and what the human person can do?

- And can you indicate how the Third Water, the `Aqueduct`, is
  broadly different to the Second Water, the Waterwheel?

- Another issue can arise:
    - Teresa in the Third Water gives advice that applies also to
      the Christian life at more ordinary levels.
    - Has something struck you that makes you think of what you
      may have read in other authors, in the lives of holy
      people, in your own experience of in that of friends?
        - [The deep things of God can be found, in a simple or
          initial form, in the experience of people who at the
          time may not otherwise be very advanced on the
          spiritual journey.]

- We shall next proceed to the fourth most easy and effective way
  of watering a garden, `Rain`.


# UNIT 8: TERESA AND PRAYER V:  THE FOUR WATERS

### Unit 8: The Rain (4)

- On the image of rain, see `Unit 15` on the Interior Castle,
  Sixth Mansions. [^3]
    - ./Unit-15-16.txt

- The Fourth Water is the rain;
    - here the gardener does nothing, but the crops are watered
      more effectively and completely than by the other
      labour-intensive ways.
    - When Teresa wrote this section, she was dealing with the
      highest graces that she had received. When she was writing
      she did not know about anything higher.
    - However, by 1577 at the age of sixty-two when she began the
      Interior Castle, she had received still further graces, so
      that what she thought was highest, the Fourth Water, is
      treated later as the `Sixth Mansions`, and gives way to the
      yet higher state of the Seventh Mansions.

- Up to now the person is doing something.
    - The characteristic of the Fourth Water is that the person
      is passive, receiving God’s visitation and grace like the
      Earth being given the rain.
    - In reading the chapters on the Fourth Waters we should keep
      clear in our minds the main matters treated.

- We'll look at three issues:
    - The prayer of the `Fourth` Water.
    - Her treatment of `rapture`.
    - Her constant assault on `techniques` to attain mystical
      graces.

- Granted all that, the Fourth Water is _not as clear_ as the
  others; Teresa’s recent experience of this grace (and probably
  our lack of such experience) leaves a certain feeling of
  incompleteness.
    - Commentators, such as Archbishop Williams and the
      Kavanaugh-Rodriguez edition, give some help; for greater
      clarity, however, we have to await the treatment in The
      Interior Castle.

- St. Teresa sets down the broad principle:
    - In all the prayer and modes of prayer that were explained,
      the gardener does some work, even though in these latter
      modes the work is accompanied by so much glory and
      consolation for the soul that it would never want to
      abandon this prayer.
    - As a result, the prayer is not experienced as work but as
      glory.
    - In this fourth water, the soul isn’t in possession of its
      senses, but it rejoices without understanding what it is
      rejoicing in…
        - All the senses are occupied in this joy in such a way
          that none is free to be taken up with any other
          exterior or interior thing. (L 18:1)

- Here is clearly a new kind of experience.
    - The person is totally passive; it is God who is doing all.
    - There is need for careful discernment here.
    - There might seem to be an explanation apart from mystical
      grace.
    - Could the state described by Teresa be induced by
      techniques similar to those found in some Eastern
      religions?
    - We need to read carefully if we are to grasp her thought.
      In the beginning she is speaking of what she calls
      “`union`” which resembles a swoon, characterised by
      a degree of numbness or anaesthesia.
    - The faculties of memory, intellect and will are captivated
      for a time. She does not understand what is happening, but
      tries to describe it:
        - How this prayer they call `union` comes about and what
          it is, I don’t know how to explain.
        - These matters are expounded in mystical theology (la
          mística teología); I wouldn’t know the proper
          vocabulary.
        - Neither do I understand what the mind (mente) is; nor
          do I know how it differs from the soul or the spirit
          (ni qué diferencia tenga del alma o espíritu tampoco).
        - It _all seems the same to me_, though the soul
          sometimes goes forth from itself (el alma alguna vez
          sale de sí mesma). (L 18:2)

- The “going forth” is found in other mystical writers; see John
  of the Cross,
    - “One dark night…I went out unseen (salí).”
    - The important thing is not, however, going out, but the
      meeting God in what she calls union. Again, this will be
      clearer in The Interior Castle.

- In this high state of prayer and union with God in knowing and
  loving, but without any clear object or attention, other gifts
  are also found.
    - She mentions `tears`, with which Ignatius of Loyola was
      almost embarrassingly gifted. Such tears arise not so much
      from a simple recognition of sinfulness, but are an
      experience of joy, a pure `gift` (L. 19:1-2).
    - At this stage of Teresa’s education in mystical graces, she
      considers `ecstasy` an added and higher gift.
    - She notes:
        - “The elevation of the spirit, or joining with heavenly
          love, which I shall describe, takes place within this
          very union” (L 18:7).
    - She uses a variety of terms to describe this grace.
        - Kavanaugh-Rodriguez notes that these are similar and
          not easy to distinguish:
            - elevation of spirit,
            - joining with heavenly love,
            - flights of the spirit,
            - suspension, rapture,
            - transport, ecstasy. [^4]
    - What they have in common is that Teresa is `overwhelmed`
      and unable to resist the power or grace which is coming
      upon her.
    - We should note that the effects are no longer confined to
      her inner faculties but invade her `body` also.

- Teresa notes that after these graces there is a serious sense
  of desolation that can be variously explained.
    - It is a devastating return from supreme joy to ordinary
      life; God can seem absent; there is physical exhaustion; it
      is, as Williams notes, a grace, a sharing in the cross and
      purification.
    - Teresa knows that this is a gift from God; it is not
      self-generated. [^5]
    - At the time of writing Teresa was stable in this level of
      prayer:
        - When the soul is seeking God in this way, it feels with
          the most marvelous and _gentlest delight_ that
          everything is almost fading away through a kind of
          swoon in which breathing, and all the bodily energies
          gradually fail. (L 18:11)
        - I am saying that in an occurrence of this prayer only
          a short time passes without one of the faculties
          returning to itself.
        - It is the will that holds high the banner; the other
          two faculties quickly go back to being a bother. Since
          the will remains quiet, the others are again suspended
          for a little while—then return again to life. (L 18:12)
        - In this way a person can and in fact does spend several
          hours in prayer. (L 18:13)

- These experiences sometimes lasted a long time and Teresa knew
  that it could not be the `sole way` of praying or seeking God.
    - If the Lord were always to give (this heavenly water) when
      there is need, the gardener would evidently have it easy…
    - But this is impossible while we are still living on this
      earth.
    - A person must always take care so that when one kind of
      water is lacking, he might strive for the other.
    - This water from heaven often comes when the gardener is
      least expecting it. True, in the beginning it almost always
      occurs after a long period of mental prayer. (L 18:9)

- The reason is surely clear. These graces are transient gifts
  which may not be given at some particular time.
    - One never goes beyond reflection on Jesus, on the
      scriptures, on the need to do God’s will.
    - Nor may prayer be abandoned, especially from a false
      humility (L 19:4-5), “to give up the practice of prayer was
      the greatest evil.” (L 19:9).


### Unit 8: Rapture

- One of the longest chapters of Teresa’s Life (ch. 20) is
  devoted to the subject of _rapture_.
    - Teresa again states what we have learned to expect from
      her, namely, that God needed to enlighten her if she is to
      explain difficult matters;
        - I would like to know how to explain, with God’s help,
          the _difference_ there is between _union and rapture_,
          or, as they call it, elevation or flight of the spirit,
          or transport, which are all the same.
        - I mean that these latter terms, though different, refer
          to the same thing; it is also called _ecstasy_…
        - May the Lord explain as He did for the other degrees.
        - Certainly, if His Majesty had not given me
          understanding of the manners and ways in which
          something could be said about them, I would not have
          known how to speak of them. (L 20:1)

- The intense inner spiritual experience flows out to the body
  which becomes cold, and stiff; it can be immobile in the
  position it was when the rapture came, e.g. hands clasped, eyes
  open or closed. (L 20:18-20)
    - Resistance is of little avail. (L 20:3-4)
    - She goes on to speak of `levitation`, the experience of
      being lifted above the ground.
    - On occasion to avoid publicity, she had the nuns try to
      hold her down. (L 20:5)
    - The experience of rapture involves both pain and delight;
      it is an intense experience of God’s power and presence and
      also can be a painful sense of absence. (L 20:6-16)
    - The effects of these graces are very precious: profound
      humility, deep detachment, regrets about past foolishness;
      if a woman shares about such experience and warns people
      not to be ensnared, she will likely be condemned as lacking
      in humility. (L 20:25)

- She now has a new perspective on worldly things (L 20:27).
  Considering `money` she says:
    - Oh, if everyone would consider it unprofitable dirt, how
      harmoniously would the world proceed, how many lawsuits
      would be avoided!
    - What friendship there would be among all if there were no
      self-interest about honour and money?
    - I think the absence of such self-interest would solve all
      problems. (L 20:27)

- She is then acutely aware of her sins and failures:
    - In this prayer it sees not only serious faults and cobwebs
      in its soul, but every speck of dust no matter how small
      because the sun is very bright…
    - The soul is like `water in a glass`:
        - the water looks very clear if the sun does not shine on
          it; but when the sun shines on it, it seems full of
          dust particles. (L 20:26)

- This experience operates too at lower levels and people
  sometimes think they are going backwards.
    - It is merely that their spiritual eyesight is improving as
      God’s light shines more clearly on them; spring-cleaning
      begins when the days become brighter and dust and dirt are
      more obvious.

- Teresa concludes (ch. 21) by treating the new perspective on
  `worldly things` that comes about with such experiences, of
  strength in doing good and of greater courage.
    - The soul is not, however, yet in heaven:
        - “Thus it lives a laborious life and always with the
          cross, but it continues to grow rapidly” (L 21:10).

- It is easy to see how Teresa thought there was no stage higher
  in the spiritual life; she would learn more by the time she
  took to writing her final work 'The Interior Castle' some
  twelve years later in 1577.

- Our own memories of being captivated by love, art, music,
  beauty give some sense of an experience of being taken over,
  though this is very far from the supreme heights of grace that
  Teresa recounts.
    - Spend a little time reflecting on such memories.
    - Can you recall any paintings of saints in ecstasy?
    - Write these down: what experience or feeling did they
      convey to you?
    - Does any icon come to mind? Can you recall reading about
      such experiences in the lives of other holy people?
    - Are there differences?  Make a note of any that you
      remember.

- We will continue our exploration of Teresa’s teaching on prayer
  in her next major work, The Way of Perfection.


### Unit 8: Footnotes

[^1] In theology since the Pseudo-Dionysius (ca. 500 in Syria),
there have been two ways of speaking about God.  Apophatic
theology (from Greek meaning negative) approaches God by denying
that any of our concepts can be applied go him. In apophatic
theology the person rejects all images or ideas of God to enter
into a darkness in which there is nonetheless and paradoxical
knowing through unknowing.  Kataphatic or affirmative theology
approaches God by asserting truths about him especially through
creation, all the whole recognising the limitations of language
and concepts.

[^2] Teresa of Avila 66

[^3] Even at this stage we should remind ourselves that the
mansions or stages of the spiritual journey described by Teresa
in The Interior Castle are plural. There are many places or ways
of living in the First, Second… Mansions.

[^4] See Works 1:474, n. 7 with reference to L 18:7.

[^5] Teresa of Avila 68 referring to L 20:15 and 16.
