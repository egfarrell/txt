# UNIT 5: Teresa & prayer II:  Four Waters


- We now begin a reflection on Teresa’s major tract on prayer
  which is found in her `Life chapters 11-22`.
    - In these units our main focus will be on reading Teresa’s
      own words, learning her language and assimilating her
      teaching on prayer.  These chapters were not actually in
      the first version of her Life: they were added at the
      instigation of Fr. García.

     - There are several choices to be made in the study of
       Teresa: either to follow her writings or to study themes.
    - A choice has been made for the former. It is significant at
      this point because the material covered in the Four Waters
      is found again in a later work, her masterpiece, `The
      Interior Castle` (“The Mansions”).
    - Instead of trying to combine the two, each treatment will
      be studied separately, with only occasional indications of
      parallels.
    - Briefly, we can say that in the Life the treatment is
      geared more towards beginners than in the Interior Castle,
      though some items will be clearer in her earlier work.
    - In the “Introduction” to the Kavanaugh-Rodriguez
      translation we note in relation to chapters 11-22 of
      Teresa’s Life that to explain God's work in her, she finds
      it necessary, to present a detailed exposition of prayer,
      its nature, degrees, and effects.
    - She goes about this task with the help of an allegory, that
      of four different ways of watering a garden:
        - using buckets of water drawn from a `well`, the equal
          of mediation;
        - using a bucket-type `waterwheel` that has to be turned
          by hand, the equivalent of the prayer of recollection
          and quiet;
        - diverting a `stream` along irrigation ditches, equal to
          the prayer of the sleep of the faculties; and
        - allowing the garden to be watered with `rain` from
          heaven, the equivalent of the prayer of union.(1)

- Let's begin our examination of the first water, which is about
  the beginnings of prayer.

- In this unit we'll meet in an initial fashion the crucial
  distinction between what is normal and what is mystical.
    - The matters treated here, together with the commentary on
      the text, will remain central to an understanding of
      Teresa’s work.
    - The two most basic questions in spirituality are perhaps:

    - What is God doing, or what will he do?
    - What is the human person doing, or will have to do?

- In the beginning there is much human activity, but as we
  progress the activity of God increases.

- Once more in this unit, and in those that follow, you need to
  be very careful about two words in Teresa and John of the
  Cross:
        - `Active`:  The human person is doing a lot
        - `Passive`: God is more and more doing the work.

- In other words, the common or dictionary meanings are here
  again neither accurate nor helpful in 16th century Spanish
  spiritual writing.

- Reading:
    - The main reading for this module is Teresa’s Life. The
      First Water, “The Well” chs 11-13.


### Unit 5: THE WELL (1)

- In chs. 11-13 Teresa develops her initial image of drawing
  water painfully from the well.

    - Beginners in prayer are those who draw water from the well.
      This involves a lot of work on their part, as I have said.
      They must tire themselves in trying to recollect their
      senses. Since they are accustomed to being distracted, this
      recollection requires much effort. (L 11:9).

- As this module progresses, we shall learn more about the prayer
  of recollection.  At this stage we should consider how hard it
  can be to concentrate on any topic: we think, we use our
  imagination and our memory; we want or desire something.
  Consider some simple project like tidying a room.  The
  following may pass through our minds:

    - It would be a good idea to tidy the area.
    - Is it worth it? A lot of trouble…
    - How would I like to re-arrange things in the room?
    - I open a box or a cupboard: I see a photograph. This brings
      back memories… I spend some time in memories, and these
      bring other thoughts, some may be pleasant, others negative
      or unpleasant.
    - I get a hold on myself and get back to tidying.
    - But the thought springing from what I found (photograph)
      keeps returning.
    - I may get a bit cross with myself.
    - I have done enough for today.
    - No, I will finish
    - I finish and I judge: it is sufficient / excellent / it
      will do.
    - I’m glad that’s done.
    - In a few weeks, it will be as bad again!



    - Now stop and think about these points:
    - In the above list which numbers primarily concern reasoning
      and judgement?
    - Which are mainly concerned with memory?
    - Which involve the will or desire?
    - Are there parallels to be found in my times of prayer?

- Thinking about one’s past life is an important element in such
  prayer.  There is need for courage and determination,
  especially when the well seems dry (L 11:10-11).
    - We could ask, why is prayer sometimes dry?
    - One could answer that every time we do something that we
      enjoy we may not always experience the same pleasure.
    - Furthermore, the effort of concentration can be tiresome.
      More seriously, it would be bad for us if prayer were
      always enjoyable, because then we would end up praying for
      what we get out of it rather than for God’s glory.
    - But it is vitally important that we persevere since Teresa
      says,
        - [T]the love of God does not consist in tears or in this
          delight and tenderness, which for the greater part we
          desire and find consolation in; but it consists in
          serving with justice and fortitude of soul and in
          humility. Without such service it seems to me we would
          be receiving everything and giving nothing. (L 11:13)


- We need also recall that the centre of the spiritual journey is
  not prayer, but an increase in love (see Matt 22:36-40).
- Teresa coins the phrase “a determined determination”
  (determinada determinación) in a later work.

- Another key Teresian idea then appears:

    - It is very important for beginners to have such freedom and
      determination, [but] I am not saying it so much for
      beginners as for others.  For there are many who begin, yet
      may never reach the end, I believe this is due mainly to
      a failure to embrace the Cross from the beginning. (L 11:15)

- We shall see as we progress the profound and subtle views of
  Teresa concerning the Cross.
    - At one level authentic human living will always involve the
      Cross, at least in the form of our needing to choose what
      is not pleasant, or to forego what would be attractive.
    - This level of the Cross is found in seeking to avoid sin
      and practice virtue.
    - But generosity and authenticity will involve other
      occasions of saying “yes” to what is unpleasant and saying
      “no” to what is attractive. All this comes under the
      heading of the Cross.
    - In everybody’s life there will also be some degree of
      suffering that can also be called the Cross. The Cross will
      be crucial in the movement from Mansions three to Mansions
      four in the Interior Castle.
    - Teresa has had a diversion, now she returns to
      practicalities. This prayer requires a lot of effort on our
      part, but there is also consolation:

        - The soul can place itself in the presence of Christ and
          grow accustomed to being inflamed with love for his
          sacred humanity.
        - It can keep him present and speak to him… not through
          written prayers but with words that conform to its
          desires and needs. (L 12:2)

- Here we have already a hint of Teresa’s prayer as a loving
  conversation that we examined in the last unit.  It also
  indicates the approach of such prayer, which Teresa now
  develops:

    - Keeping Christ present is what we of ourselves can do…. But
      this edifice is built entirely on humility, the closer one
      comes to God the more progress there must be in this
      virtue; and if there is not progress in humility,
      everything is going to be ruined. It seems a kind of pride
      to desire of ourselves to ascend higher since, in view of
      what we are, God does too much just in drawing us near to
      himself. (L 12:4)

- We have already seen Teresa’s insistence on humility.
  Observations on that virtue will be found in all her works
  right up to the very highest mystical graces (Mansions 7).
    - The issue at this point is that of people seeking to
      achieve some “mystical graces,” for example using
      techniques to achieve empty prayer.  Teresa is extremely
      negative about the presumption of such self-advancement.

    - Now stop and think. Since it is a core value for Teresa in
      all her works, we need to reflect briefly on the meaning of
      `humility`?
    - What does it mean?  Consider the following:
    1. Humility means concentrating on our sins.
    2. Humility involves having a negative view of ourselves.
    3. Humility means not thinking about any good in ourselves.
    4. Humility means being prepared to be like a doormat,
       turning the other cheek (see Matt 5:39).

    - These four points present a negative and inadequate
      interpretation of humility, more or less in line with the
      etymology Latin humilis = low. Which, if any, of these do
      you characteristically associate with humility.
    - Which would you decidedly reject? Think about your reasons
      for this.

 - But the meaning in Teresa can be better caught by noting that
   even in the highest state of mystical grace (Mansions 7) the
   Lord with whom she is so intimate is “His Majesty.”
    - The most profound humility is discovered not by looking at
      ourselves, but at God. Humility is truth and a truly humble
      person is marked by praise and thanksgiving.

    - We now come to the first extensive treatment of the
      difference between active and passive, and the issue of
      acting prematurely— “jumping the gun”—in prayer.
    - The text here needs careful reading:

- In mystical theology… the intellect ceases to work because God
  suspends it, as I shall explain afterwards if I know how, and
  He gives me his help to do so.
    - Taking it upon oneself to stop and suspend thought is what
      I mean should not be done; nor should we cease to work with
      the intellect, because otherwise we would be left like cold
      simpletons, and be doing neither one thing nor the other.
    - When the Lord suspends the intellect and causes it to stop,
      He himself gives it that which holds its attention and
      makes it marvel; and without reflection it understands more
      in the space of a Creed than we can understand with all our
      earthly diligence in many years…
    - And I say it again, even though it may not be understood,
      this effort to suspend the intellect is not very humble… (L
      12:5)

    - Once again, I counsel that it is very important for the
      spirit not to ascend unless the Lord raise it up. (L 12:7).

- There are several very important matters here:

    - “Mystical theology” is to be understood in a medieval sense
      found also in the Eastern Churches. Theology here is not an
      intellectual exercise involving study and intellectual
      effort.  Theology is religious experience, a knowing God in
      the biblical sense of personal encounter.  Mystical means
      that it is passive, the work is done by God; it is given,
      not acquired.

    - Try emptying your mind. Put away all thoughts; stop
      thinking; be empty. What is it like?
        - Can you keep this emptiness for a period of a few
          minutes?
        - Such emptying is clearly possible; it might be easy or
          difficult; it might be relaxing.
        - But it is not prayer.  There is no prayer unless we are
          reaching towards a divine person. “Reaching towards”
          are two very carefully chosen words.  We may indeed not
          experience anything; we may really be empty. But there
          is a desire to meet God.

    - Teresa says that in the higher stages of prayer God takes
      away the ability to think during prayer.  But it is not
      empty for she says, “he himself gives it that which holds
      its attention and makes it marvel.”
    - It is not mere emptiness, but genuine communication from
      God, but not in a way that we can achieve ourselves.
    - Notice too that this whole matter is difficult. Teresa says
      that she will try to explain it, but she needs God’s help
      to do so.  We meet this notion elsewhere in Teresa and she
      will tell us how at times she was puzzled by something
      until God enlightened her.
    - Note again the warning not to try to obtain this higher
      prayer.  If we can achieve it by techniques and effort,
      then it is not “mystical theology” which is always a gift.


### Unit 5: Some temptations

- Teresa now deals with some of the things that can go wrong,
  even in this early stage of the spiritual journey.
    - Firstly, she insists that people have confidence and not be
      overly concerned about distractions:

    - Now strive in the beginning to walk in joy and freedom, for
      there are some who think their devotion will go away if
      they become a little distracted. (L 13:1)
    - Have great confidence, for it is necessary not to hold back
      one’s desires, but to believe in God that if we try, we
      shall little by little, even though it may not be soon,
      reach the state the saints did with his help. (L 13:2)

- There is a crucial `Carmelite teaching` underlying this
  passage.
    - At some stage in the different modules of this course
      you'll come across the teaching of The Institute of the
      First Monks.
    - [There was a Spanish translation of this work available to
      Teresa at least in Avila.]
    - There is the famous passage in 'Book One' about the aim of
      the hermit life. We recall it here:
        - In regard to this life, we may distinguish two aims,
          the one of which we may attain to with the help of
          God's grace, by our own efforts and by virtuous living.
          This is to offer God a `heart holy and pure` from all
          actual stain of sun...
            - (ascetical)
        - The other aim of this life is something that can be
          bestowed upon us only by God's bounty:
            - namely to taste in our hearts and experience in our
              minds, not only after death but even during this
              mortal life, something of the power of the `divine
              presence`, and the bliss of heavenly glory.
            - (mystical)

- The first aim is what is sometimes called ascetical; it is
  about what we can do with God's grace and our efforts.

- The second aim is mystical, and it a special grace.

- According to this, it is quite legitimate to aspire to mystical
  knowing and loving, if God wills to give it to us.
    - Teresa’s immediate point is that the devil `tempts` people
      to think such desires lack humility.
    - But underlying her thought is another issue that we find in
      all the major Carmelite writers:
        - why other people can become very holy as a result of
          taking seriously God’s call to love and union with him?

- Watch out for this question in Teresa and in other Carmelite
  writers like John of the Cross, Thérèse of Lisieux, Elizabeth
  of the Trinity.
    - Is our attitude one of _false humility_ and disbelief?
    - At the risk of trivialising, we could think of Danny Kay’s
      recounting of “The Ugly Duckling” tale:
        - “Me a swan? Go wan.” Similarly,
        - “Me a mystic?... You must be kidding.”

- _Secondly_, though Teresa is solicitous that we do not damage our
  health, excessive care of health can be a temptation. (L 13:7).

- Teresa then points to a _third problem_:
    - Another temptation is then very common. Since they begin to
      enjoy the serenity and gain that comes, they desire
      everyone else to be very spiritual.
    - To desire this is not wrong. Striving to bring it about
      could have unhappy results if there is not a lot of
      discretion…” (L 13:8)

- But there is a _fourth possibility_ that arises from
  self-deception that leads to self-righteousness and judgemental
  attitudes, both cloaked in virtue:
    - The devil tempts them in another way through distress over
      the sins and failings of others…
    - He puts it into their heads that this distress stems only
      from the desire that God be not offended and from the
      concern for his honour; and next they seek a remedy.
    - This desire disquiets them so much that it hinders their
      prayer; and the greatest harm lies in their thinking that
      this distress amounts to virtue, perfection and great zeal
      for God… (L 13:10)

- Such people, she remarks elsewhere, are a right pain!
    - We all know of people who give prayer, piety and
      spirituality a bad name.
    - It can take the form of, “I do not mind about it myself,
      but the injustice of it as it affects others…”
    - Teresa then gives simple, direct advice:
        - But the safe path for the soul that practices prayer
          will be _not to bother about anything or anyone_ and to
          pay attention to itself and to pleasing God…
        - But let us strive always to look at virtues and good
          deeds we see in others and cover their defects with the
          thought of our own great sins. (L 13:10)

- Here we find one of the central intuitions of Teresa, the need
  of `charity`, here with an emphasis `on thought`.


### Unit 5: Reflective prayer

- We have seen Teresa’s extremely negative views of people
  wanting to ascend higher before they are called to it by God.
    - But the issue is not simple, and we now have a very
      important qualification that must be taken into our
      consideration of reflective prayer.
        - But returning to those who practice discursive
          reflection, I say they should not pass the whole time
          thinking.
        - For although discursive reflection is very meritorious,
          they don’t seem to realise that since their prayer is
          delightful there should _ever be a Sunday_ or a time
          when one is not working; but they think such time is
          lost.
        - I consider this loss a great gain.
        - But as I said (see L 12:2) they should put themselves
          in the presence of Christ and, without tiring the
          intellect, speak with and delight in him and not wear
          themselves out in composing syllogisms;
            - ... rather they should show Him their needs and the
              reason why he doesn’t have to allow us to be in his
              presence.
            - (L 13:11)

- We have seen that the prayer of those `at the well` is
  discursive and active, that is, engaged on a topic using their
  minds to think about it, to make comparisons, to draw
  conclusions.
    - But Teresa warns that it must not be a frantic activity.
    - The reflection can be quiet and at times with their minds
      at rest. They are not to tire their minds with elaborate
      thinking.
    - A key idea here is putting themselves in the presence of
      Christ and speaking with him. Here we have an echo of “the
      close _sharing between friends_” found in Teresa’s
      description of prayer (Life 8:5).
    - Teresa notes the need for guidance.
        - The beginner needs counsel so as to see what helps him
          most. For this reason a master is very necessary
          providing he has experience. (L 13:14)

- The spiritual journey has many subtleties and pitfalls;
  a person of practical spiritual wisdom can be very important.

- Have you any thoughts about guidance in prayer and generally in
  the spiritual journey?

- Do most people need a spiritual director if they go beyond
  initial stages? Do they always need a flesh and blood director
  or is it _just direction that they need_?

- What are your thoughts here – and your experience?

- Keep alert to what Teresa says about `spiritual directors`.
    - Both she and John of the Cross know the results of bad
      direction or none.
    - A very pious director may give very poor guidance.
    - With much nuance the Carmelite school will generally favour
      learning over piety: a learned but not very pious director
      may do less damage, or be preferable to, a pious fool (see
      Life 13:15-22 and John of the Cross, Ascent of Mount
      Carmel, Prologue).

- Teresa ends with a very important clarification.
    - The way of prayer described in the 'well' analogy is not
      a stage that we progress through and then leave.
    - She insists that the prayer of the Well can never be
      abandoned:
        - The path of self-knowledge must never be abandoned, nor
          is there on this journey a soul so much of a giant that
          it has no need to return often to the stage of an
          infant and a suckling…
        - There is no stage of prayer so sublime that it isn’t
          necessary to return to the beginning.
        - Along this path of prayer, self-knowledge and the
          thought of one’s sins is the bread with which all
          palates must be fed no matter how delicate they may be;
          they cannot be sustained without this bread. (L 13:15)

- There are two points here:
    - We will never get past or go beyond self-knowledge and the
      awareness of sin.  There has to be a constant return to
      this fundamental fact.
    - Reflection must continue. We cannot get to a stage when we
      rely only on `God’s special direct communications`; there
      will be ordinary times for commonplace and run-of-the-mill
      thoughts and desires.

- Summary. We have considered the first stage of prayer in some
  detail.  In it we find many of the great themes that Teresa
  will develop later.
    - What would you consider to be the important or interesting
      `issues` in this unit?  Would you write down five things
      that struck you?

    - To what extent would you agree that Teresa is encouraging,
      given that she is also speaking from her `own experience`
      of what can go wrong?

    - Would you like her as a `spiritual director`?  What would be
      your reasons for your response?

- Having seen in some detail the first stage of prayer (in which
  the human person is most active), we'll now proceed to the
  higher stages of prayer in which God is increasingly at work.


# UNIT 6: Teresa & Prayer III: Four Waters

### Unit 6: WATERWHEEL (2)

- The material on prayer in these units may not be easy to grasp
  immediately.
    - Teresa will be dealing with the heights of the spiritual
      journey. If you find certain passages or ideas difficult,
      mark them and return at a later time, when they'll probably
      have become clearer.

- What is important in these units is to grasp the main idea
  presented by the `Water analogy`, which is the growing ease of
  watering the garden through a waterwheel, aqueduct and finally
  rain.
    - The theme is the ever growing activity of God in prayer and
      the diminishing activity of the person praying. The
      key-image in this unit is:
                - {image of a water wheel}

- The immediate point of the second analogy is that, although
  some considerable effort is needed with a waterwheel, it is
  comparatively less work than drawing from a well.

- It is not entirely clear for us today the kind of `waterwheel`
  Teresa had in mind.
    - Illustrations normally feature the kind of waterwheel that
      turns and raises water through cups on the wheel being
      filled from a source like a stream.
    - But there were earlier ones that needed human effort to
      turn the wheel, or as in the illustration a few paragraphs
      down, a working animal such as a donkey or a camel.
    - This prayer still requires effort, but the work of God
      becomes more evident. The waterwheel is the symbol for the
      prayer of quiet.

- At this stage, the soul begins to be recollected and comes upon
  something supernatural because in no way can it acquire this
  prayer through any effort it may make…
    - In this prayer the faculties are gathered within so as to
      enjoy that satisfaction with greater delight.
    - But they are not lost, nor do they sleep.
    - Only the will is occupied in such a way that, without
      knowing how, it _becomes captive_; it merely consents to
      God allowing Him to imprison it as one who well knows how
      to be the captive of its lover. (L 14:2)

- It is important here to note the difference between what might
  be called “quiet prayer” and the Teresian “prayer of quiet.”
    - The former we saw at the conclusion of the first water, the
      Well.
    - The latter is a mystical gift that is beyond merely human
      effort. It is God who quiets. The faculties, intellect and
      will principally, are not very active.

    - All that takes place here brings with it the greatest
      consolation and with so little labour that prayer does not
      tire one even though it lasts a long while.
    - The intellect's work here is very slow paced, and it
      obtains a lot more water than it pulled out of the well.
        - (L 14:4)


### Unit 6: Treadmill well

- This prayer is very purifying. Because of the delight
  experienced, various attachments and other pleasures seem less
  significant or important:

    - In arriving here it soon begins to lose its craving for
      earthly things—and little wonder!
    - It sees clearly that one moment of the enjoyment of glory
      cannot be experienced here below, neither are there riches,
      or sovereignties, or honours, or delights that are able to
      provide a brief moment of that happiness, for it is a true
      happiness that, it is seen, satisfies us. (L 14:5)

- Again Teresa takes up a central concern of hers:
    - why are not more people more diligent answering God’s
      invitation to enjoy his gifts?
        - May it please His Majesty to give me grace to explain
          this state well because there are many, many souls who
          reach it but few that pass beyond it; and I don’t know
          whose fault it is.
        - I know many souls that reach this stage, but to me it
          is a terrible pity that those that pass beyond, as they
          should, are so few I am ashamed to mention it. (L 15:5)

- Teresa then gives guidance about how to behave in this state.
    - What the soul must do during these times of quiet amounts
      to no more than proceeding gently and noiselessly (con
      suavidad y sin ruido).
    - What I call “noise” is running about with the intellect
      looking for many words and reflections so as to give thanks
      for this gift and piling up one’s sins and faults to see
      that it is unmerited. (L 15:6)
    - Finally at this stage one doesn’t have to renounce
      completely discursive mental prayer or the use of some
      words, or even vocal prayers if there should be the desire
      or ability; if the quiet is great, it is difficult to speak
      without a good deal of effort. (L 15:9)

- Just as at the end of the Well, Teresa here is again warning
  people against thinking that they have _gone beyond_ various
  earlier forms of prayer.

### Unit 6: Discernment

- Up to now we have not seriously encountered a major theme is
  Carmelite and Ignatian spirituality, namely `discernment`.

- In the first waters we can have a shrewd idea if we are praying
  or not.
    - But once a person moves beyond the activity of drawing
      water from the well and God’s gifts become more
      significant, the question must arise whether what a person
      is experiencing is from God or not.
    - For Carmelites and Ignatian spiritual writers there are
      three possible sources for any experience:
        - From God.
        - From oneself.
        - From the devil.

- It may not always be possible to discern if any particular
  experience is genuine or wholesome, especially if it occurs
  rather early in the spiritual journey.
    - But a _pattern_, that is, several examples over a period of
      time, can give greater assurance or certainty about the
      source.

- From Teresa’s `Life 15:9-14` we can deduce a number of
  criteria, all the time remembering the crucial discernment
  principle enunciated by Jesus:
    - “By their fruits, you will know them” (Matt 7:17-20).

- We list the signs under seven headings:
    - “We can discern, in my opinion, whether this quiet comes
      from the spirit of God or whether we procure it ourselves
      once God begins to give devotion and we, as I said (see
      L 11:12-16; 12:3) want to pass on to the quiet through our
      own efforts. When we procure the quiet ourselves, it
      produces no effect, quickly goes away, and leaves behind
      aridity.” (L 15:9)
        - $MY_NOTES/practical/Spirit/Carmelite/CIBI/Misc/gpt-prayer-of-quiet.txt

    - “If the quiet is from the devil, I think an _experienced
      soul_ will recognise this because it results in
      `disturbance` and a `lack of humility`”  (L 15:10).

    - It is an important matter for beginners in prayer to start
      off by becoming detached from every kind of satisfaction
      and to enter the path solely with the determination to
      _help Christ carry the cross_ like good cavaliers, who
      desire to serve their king at no salary since their salary
      is certain.” (L 15:11)

    - There remains a sign that the Lord was present in their
      prayer:
        - and it is that if they `fail`, they `rise` again
          quickly.

    - When the prayer comes from God’s spirit, there is no need
      to go dredging up things in order to derive some _humility_
      or _shame_.
        - God is in charge here and he will supply what is
          needed.

    - He bestows a strong desire to advance in prayer and not
      abandon it no matter what trial may come upon the person.

    - The soul offers itself up in all things…

    - "He casts out from it _all servile fear_ and grants
      a mature trusting fear. It is aware of the beginning of
      a love of God that has less self-interest” (L 15:14).

- Even if the exact nature of the waterwheel isn't fully clear,
  we note two reasons why it's a suitable image:
    - there is a _reduction of effort_ but no cessation from
      _activity_.

- This unit has begun to introduce the reality of `passive
  prayer`.
    - Can you remember any experience of something happening,
      even if only momentarily, that is not the result of
      reasoning or effort?
    - It might be at prayer, or it may be some other time, such
      as seeing a branch, a tiny insect, a sky pattern or
      a person in a new way that is somehow “given” or
      experienced as a special blessing?
    - Such incidents, even if very simple or brief, can help us
      to enter a little into the reality of the waterwheel
      analogy.
