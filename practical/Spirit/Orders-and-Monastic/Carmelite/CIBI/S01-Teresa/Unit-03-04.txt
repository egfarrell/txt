# UNIT 3: THE REFORMER

- St Teresa of Avila is known principally as a mystic and
  reformer of Carmel.
    - Describing her reforms and the _17 foundations_ she made
      could be placed anywhere in this module.
    - The decision to examine it now is practical, even though
      it'll be understood more as we progress,
    - In any material about Teresa the reforms will come up. This
      unit can serve as a reference point to return to.

### Unit 3: Intro

- There is a certain repetition of matters covered in other
  modules of level six.  But these are presenting the material
  more from an historical perspective. The facts of Teresa’s
  reforms are important and they'll be lightly covered in this
  unit.  But we are most concerned with asking, what is going on?
  That is:
    - What is God doing?
    - What is Teresa doing?
    - Are there _patterns_, recurrent _themes_ that will allow us
      to appreciate the spirituality of this genius?

- Thus, we'll begin to know be Teresa better: she was a most
  practical woman, a reformer and doctor of the Church, but also
  a person like Elijah who knew weakness, temptation and
  self-doubt that were conquered by God’s grace. We find two
  major places in Teresa’s writings which deal with the reform:
    - `Life` chs 32-36
    - The Book of her `Foundations` (abbrev. Foundations)

- In her lifetime Teresa founded seventeen monasteries of the
  Reform. we'll study her account of the _first foundation in
  Avila_ by following her `Life` and the `Foundations` chs 13-14.
    - Then we'll look at the foundation of Duruelo for the friars
      in 1568. But first we'll examine the book of Foundations.


### Unit 3: Book of Her Foundations

- In the book of her `Life` (chs 32-36) Teresa had already
  written how she had embarked on reform.
    - One of her critics the Jesuit, Jerome Ripalda, was so
      impressed by reading her `Life` that he told her to write
      about her foundations.
    - She began it in Salamanca on 25 August 1573; its writing
      would occupy the rest of her life. The main places and
      stages of writing of the chapters were:
        - Salamanca (1573) 1-9
        - Avila     (1574) 10-20
        - Toledo    (1576) 21-27

- She wrote an account of the other foundations immediately after
  she had made them. The final four foundations are in chapters
  28-31.  She wrote the last in Burgos, three or four months
  before her death (Spring 1582).

- In the Prologue of the `Foundations`, she gave two aims:
    - Firstly, to recount the favours God had given to the Order
      through these foundations and to express this in truth
      without exaggeration.
    - Secondly, to give praise to the Lord. [^1]

- It is clearly about divine charisms. The work begins with
  a mystical intervention of the Lord, and it ends with an
  account of ...(?) [^2]

- We know that Teresa wrote with great speed, “as fast as a court
  clerk” according to contemporaries.
    - She made no corrections as she wrote, and in all her works
      she'd frequently interrupt her narrative to make asides
      about prayers or some didactic material to help her
      sisters.
    - In the case of the `Foundations`, she wished not only to
      give an historical account of each new monastery, but also
      to give instructive and inspirational material.
    - Let's note these asides now so we'll not lose our way. Her
      major _digressions_ (chapter numbers in brackets) are:

    - Mystical graces in Carmelite communities (4)
    - The union of obedience, prayer and fraternal service (5)
    - Spiritual and psychological aberrations in enclosed life
      (6, 7)
    - Advice about revelations and visions (8)
    - Advice to prioresses (18)
    - Biographical sketches or activities about individuals:
        - Casella of Padilla (10-11),
        - Beatrice of the Incarnation (12),
        - Beatriz Gomez (26)
    - as well as profiles of:
        - John of the Cross (13-14),
        - Gracián (23),
        - the founders of Pastrami (17).

- There is a rhythm between history and instruction; she was an
  exceptional foundress and reformer, but she preferred the role
  of spiritual guide. The `Foundations` has:
    - 300 numbered paragraphs devoted to history with
    - 211 given to spiritual guidance.

- When we read the text, we notice two polarities:
    - Firstly, the _difficulties_ of the journeys with affliction
      from icy winds, snow, scorching sun, bad meals, inns that
      were often filthy and the place for quarrels, fights, music
      and dancing. Teresa and those travelling with her suffered
      from attacks of fever and other illnesses.
    - Secondly, she knew the clear and constant _presence of God_
      throughout.
    - The last chapter gives a striking picture of Teresa
      herself, especially if read in conjunction with her
      `Relatión`
        - (Spiritual Testimony n. 6, written at Malagón on
          9 February 1570, which gives the clear commission of
          the Lord to make many foundations and write their
          history)

- The Teresian scholar Egido, remarks that though there are flaws
  in the composition of the `Foundations`—unfinished words,
  strange or absent punctuation—therein lies the mystery of its
  beauty.
    - It is the most natural, the most spontaneous, and the most
      accessible of the major works of Teresa.
    - Her literary ingeniousness or artlessness, which does not
      mean lack of grace or charm— her obvious shrewdness, the
      pain and humour never far from the surface, the occasional
      social satire of the keenest kind—all these give us the
      impression of being before a cultivated woman of the 16th
      century who narrates things as if she were speaking
      directly to her sisters in typically colloquial
      language.[^3]

### Unit 3: Reform

- In the fourteenth century there was serious decline in
  religious life. By the late Middle Ages monasteries had become
  very wealthy and religious sought privileges and exemptions of
  all kinds, especially for superiors and for _those with degrees_.

- There was a general collapse of religious observance throughout
  Europe as a result of the Black Plague, [^4]  whose
  significance for religious history and spirituality as well as
  for political, economic and social history can scarcely be
  exaggerated.
    - From about 1500 the climate grew colder. This change is
      called “the little ice age”; it would last to about 1700
      and involved serious hardship for people in Europe.

- There were reform movements of various kinds in different
  orders as religious sought to return to the primitive spirit of
  their founders; the `reformed` were called “`observants`” and
  the remainder were called “conventuals.”
    - The Catholic Monarchs as they were called, Ferdinand and
      Isabella, promoted the observant movement.  Some of the
      reformed such as Franciscans and Augustinians were called
      “discalced” since they abandoned costly shoes for sandals
      (`dis` as negative prefix, and `calceus`, shoe).
    - In the 16th century there were also serious attempts to
      implement the reforms of the Council of Trent, which had
      ended in 1553.
    - The Society of Jesus [Jesuits] had been founded in 1534 and
      received papal approval in 1540; its main aim was
      propagation of the faith and promotion of Christian
      spirituality.
    - Its members became involved in opposing the Reformation in
      Germany from 1550.  In Spain they gave important support to
      Teresa.
    - There were two main sources of reform in Spain:
        - the king and the papacy.
        - Teresa did not have a great “Roman” sense of the Church
          though the word occurs frequently; for her the Church
          is primarily the Church in Castile.
        - In the case of Carmelites there was also the reforming
          activity of the Prior General, John Baptist Rubeo
          (known in Italian as Rossi), who visited Spain in 1566.

- There is need for caution in speaking of the Convent of the
  Incarnation in Avila where Teresa entered.
    - The life there was marked by serious austerity and by
      splendid liturgy, but the singing of the Divine Office,
      occupying nearly five hours per day, left no time for
      mental prayer.
    - In fact, one of the first actions of Teresa in her reformed
      monasteries was to cut out singing on ordinary days to free
      up two hours for prayer. [^5]
    - The monastery was very large, with over 200 living
      there—nuns, their servants and relatives. Many of the nuns
      had personal servants, which was common at the times.

- You may note the word “monastery” in Teresa’s writings.
  A monastery had a certain stability.  Even today their
  inhabitants' “nuns” and “monks” do not generally change houses;
  both the novitiate and the graveyard are on the same property!
    - Furthermore, the religious in a monastery take solemn vows
      which have some stricter obligations. In correct usage
      religious who are not in solemn vows are properly called
      “sisters” and their religious house were generally called
      “convents.”
    - The usage among male congregations is more flexible.
      Carmelites are properly called “friars” from the Latin for
      “brother” [the original name was Order of the Brothers of
      Our Lady of Mount Carmel].
        - Their houses are variously called friary, priory and on
          the European continent the word “convent” is frequently
          used.

- Stop here for a moment and summarise the situation in which
  Teresa was living:
    - what were the difficulties?
    - ... the opportunities?


### Unit 3: First Foundation: St. josephs Avila

- In her `Life` chapters `32-36` we find St. Teresa’s account of
  the reform, beginning at Avila. In these chapters some constant
  themes recur in her accounts of her foundations.  Reform was in
  the air.  Teresa herself at the time was wondering about how
  she could best serve God. She concluded:

- I thought that the first thing was to follow the call to the
  religious life ... by keeping my rule as perfectly as I could.
  [^6]

- She acknowledged the many fine qualities of the community
  members, but she was not happy about the Rule being mitigated.
    - This was the mitigation of Eugene IV in 1432.
    - She was uneasy about the level of comfort in the monastery
      and especially about nuns going in and out of the convent;
      some nuns stayed outside with relatives and friends.
    - She herself was often sent by the prioress to people who
      wanted Teresa to stopover with them. [^7]
    - There were discussions about reform in the monastery of the
      Incarnation. There were about ten people who would meet at
      one time or another in Teresa’s cell. It was a cousin of
      Teresa, Marie Cepeda y Ocampo, who was living at the
      Incarnation that first seems to have suggested that if they
      could not be nuns like the discalced [Franciscan], it would
      still be possible to find a monastery.
    - Teresa discussed the idea with a wealthy widow with whom
      she was staying, Doña Guiomar de Ulloa; she was enthused
      [^8]  and later would provide funds for the establishment
      of the first foundation.

- Teresa was not much in a hurry to move on the matter; she was
  content where she was. As we'll find time and again, Our Lord
  gave her guidance:
    > One day after Communion, His Majesty earnestly commanded me
    > to strive for this new monastery with all my power and He
    > made great promises that it would be founded and that He
    > would be highly served in it.  He said that it should be
    > called St. Joseph and that this saint would keep watch over
    > us at one door, and our Lady at the other, that Christ
    > would remain with us, that it would be a star shining with
    > great splendour. He said that though religious orders were
    > mitigated one shouldn’t think He was little served in them;
    > He asked what would become of the world if it were not for
    > religious and said that I should tell my confessor what He
    > commanded, that He was asking him not to go against this or
    > hinder me from doing it. (9)

- This has many _common points_ found in Teresa’s narratives
  about her foundations. We should note :
    - The mystical experience occurs after `Communion`.
    - God stirs a somewhat `reluctant` Teresa into action.
    - He gives `reassurance` and promises support and presence.
    - The `Blessed Virgin` and St. `Joseph` are special
      protectors and facilitators.
    - The project will wonderfully `succeed`.
    - Teresa was to `proceed without looking` at other ways and
      works of the Lord; those who do not follow her reform (the
      mitigated) would still serve the Lord well.
    - She was to be `totally guided` by her confessor.
    - The Lord will see to it that her `advisors` too will get
      divine help.

- We should note that the normal way of spiritual direction at
  the time for nuns was within confession.
    - Teresa did at times have one-to-one consultations in
      convent parlours and elsewhere.
    - Since great discretion was needed about various moves
      concerning foundations, discussions with a confessor should
      be secret. [^10]

- Eventually Teresa told her confessor; the provincial agreed
  with the idea; her friend the Franciscan mystic St. Peter of
  Alcántara was positive and encouraging.[^11]
    - But as soon as word got out she faced gossip and derision
      with great opposition and persecution from all quarters: it
      seemed that everyone was opposed; the provincial changed
      his mind and withdrew his permission; those in the convent
      were very critical of Teresa; her companion, the widow,
      Doña Guiomar, was told that she was contributing to a great
      scandal and was told that she could not get absolution
      unless she gave up on the idea.
    - They both consulted a learned Dominican, Pedro Ibáñez,
      Teresa did not mention the encouragement from the Lord to
      him. Don Pedro asked for eight days to consider the matter.
        - He considered the plan foolish and as he considered how
          he might tell this to Teresa, he became convinced that
          the project should be undertaken and with urgency.
        - His approval meant that some holy people who had been
          against the project now gave it support. [^12]
    - She suffered much rejection from her own community and from
      her confessor. Then a main supporter the Dominican Ibáñez
      was moved to another house.  But she found a new confessor
      whose superior supported her. [^13]

- We should note that in discussing the project with selected
  people, Teresa did not speak of the assurance she had received
  from the Lord in mystical experiences.
    - People were left to judge the project objectively on its
      merits.
    - Eventually her Jesuit confessor and his superior were
      convinced that they should not oppose the foundation and
      permitted Teresa to return to it in great secrecy.
    - There was an enormous amount of practical business:
        - buying the property, having it adapted as a monastery.
        - She felt very much alone but had the Lord’s support.
        - On one occasion when she had no money to hire workmen
          St. Joseph appeared to her and told her to go ahead.
          [^14]
        - In the midst of all this turmoil she also had mystical
          vision of St. Clare, to whom she would be very devoted.
          Immediately after she describes the well-known vision
          of Our Lady and St. Joseph. [^15]

- A major issue in Teresa’s reform was poverty.
    - She desired to have strict poverty, even to the extent of
      `begging` for subsistence, but she was aware that others
      who might join the reform may not have her conviction on
      the matter.
    - Those whom she consulted were against this level of
      poverty, except for the Franciscan, Peter of Alcántara.
      [^16]

- When she returned to Avila, from staying with a titled and
  well-connected lady, [^17]  she met the bishop with Peter of
  Alcántara and another holy layman.
    - These latter two persuaded the bishop to accept the
      monastery under his jurisdiction (recall that the other
      possible religious superior, the Carmelite provincial, was
      still opposed).
    - Eventually four women took the habit at St. Joseph’s on the
      feast of St. Bartholomew, 24 August 1562.
    - Teresa rejoiced to have reservation of the Blessed
      Sacrament. In her reforms this is her prime aim; it
      generally constitutes a foundation or formal establishment
      of a religious house. [^18]

- Immediately afterwards she faced serious assaults of temptation
  from the devil.
    - She was very vulnerable to doubt, anxious as she was to do
      God’s will.
    - In addition to visions and locutions and the question of
      their genuineness, there were many other forces at play
      both positive and negative, any of which might be
      reflecting God’s will:
        - the negative attitude of the provincial of the friars
          to whom the Incarnation was subject;
        - the role of the local bishop;
        - the place of the Father General, John Baptist Rubeo
          (Rossi) and of the local prioress at the Incarnation;
        - the advice or commands of her confessors and wise
          theologians, generally Dominican and Jesuit along with
          Peter of Alcántara.
    - To make things more complicated, God often gave instruction
      about what was to be done but left it to Teresa and others
      to find the way, which, however, he would so often
      providentially arrange.

- She was also subject to much misunderstanding not only in the
  Incarnation, but amongst the inhabitants of Avila.
    - There was an appeal to the Royal Council of Spain.
    - It would be two years before there was generally peaceful
      acceptance of the monastery; significant opponents and
      ordinary people became favourable and several became
      benefactors.(19)

- In Life 36:26 there is reference to the “unmitigated” Carmelite
  Rule, which then and since has caused misunderstandings.
    - Teresa thought that the Rule approved by Pope Innocent IV
      was the “primitive” text, but it wasn’t.
    - This 1247 text was the `Formula of Life` of Albert of
      Jerusalem modified by two Dominicans and approved by the
      Pope; it was in this way constituted a rule in a canonical
      sense.
    - The later mitigations of Eugene IV in 1432 were the only
      mitigation or changes that Teresa knew; she did not want
      them.
    - She never knew the “primitive” or original Formula of Life
      written by Albert, of which one can note, there is no
      surviving manuscript.

- Rereading this summary of the foundation at Ávila, what six or
  seven points seem most significant to you. Have you found any
  fresh aspects of Teresa’s personality?


### Unit 3: The Foundation at Duruelo

- Four years after the foundation of Joseph’s, the Prior General,
  Rossi, visited Avila, where he met Teresa the following Spring
  1567.
    - He approved her Reform and gave her licences to found
      further convents in Castile and two priories for the
      friars, when she wrote to him and pointed out the necessity
      of having discalced friars to minister to the reformed
      nuns. [^20]
    - She had however to obtain the permission from the current
      and previous provincials.
    - Two Carmelite friars were already prepared to join Teresa’s
      reform; both of them had been contemplating transferring to
      the Carthusians, Antonio de Jesús and John of the
      Cross.[^21]


- She was offered a property in a very tiny and remote village,
  north-west of Avila.
    - Having got lost on the way, they arrived at night and found
      the house which was filthy and vermin infested.
    - The friars, Antonio and John, both said they were prepared
      to live in a pigsty. [^22]

- Then there were the problems of getting the provincials’
  permission.  The current provincial, Alonso González, gave what
  she wanted;
    - in the account below, we can see why he might have found it
      hard to refuse; she seems to have played on his age and the
      judgement at death.
    - The other provincial had some need at the time and required
      help (probably money) from one of Teresa’s supporters, Doña
      María de Mendoza, brother of the Bishop of Avila. Teresa’s
      delightful account reads:
        - It pleased God that the provincial, Fray Alonso
          Gonzalez, from whom I had to obtain approbation, was
          there [Medina]. He was elderly, good natured and
          without malice. I told him many things, and reminded
          him of the account he would have to give if he hindered
          a work as good as this when asked by God to carry it
          out. His Majesty, wanting the foundation, put him in
          the right disposition, for he mellowed very much.
        - When Doña María de Mendoza and the bishop of Avila her
          brother (who is the one who always favoured and
          protected us) came, they convinced both him and Father
          Fray Angel de Salazar, the previous provincial, the one
          from whom I feared all the difficulty.
        - Moreover a certain need arose at the time for which the
          latter provincial had need of assistance from Doña
          María de Mendoza.
        - This fact, I believe, helped a great deal, although
          even if this opportunity had not been present, our Lord
          would have moved the provincial’s heart just as he did
          the heart of Father General who was anything but
          inclined to the idea.
        - Oh, God help me how many obstacles I have seen in these
          business matters that seemed impossible to overcome,
          and how easy it was for His Majesty to remove them.(23)

- One would nearly one feel sorry for the provincials; what
  chance had they with God and Teresa intent on the foundation?

- The foundation could now begin. The friars set out to collect,
  mostly by begging, items needed. Teresa notes that Fray Antonio
  had five clocks even before he seems to have had a bed.  Teresa
  sees the two friars living in the spirit of “those holy
  founders from whom we descend.”[^24]
    - The allusion here is to those who were thought to have been
      the early Carmelites from the period back to Elijah, as
      recounted in the Institutes of the First Monks.
    - The first Mass was celebrated in what Teresa called “that
      little stable of Bethlehem” in Autumn 1568.

- In the Foundations 14, she breaks off to speak about the
  founding of Mancera (nn. 9-10), before resuming her account of
  Duruelo and the outstanding reputations the friars soon
  acquired among people especially through their preaching and
  confessional ministry in neighbouring towns.
    - She was, however, concerned about the severity of their
      ascetical life and wished them to curtail it; the friars
      ignored this advice.

- In conclusion Teresa observes:
    - I understood that this foundation was a much greater grace
      than the favour He granted me to found houses of nuns.(25)

- At the time of writing this account (about 1574) Teresa could
  number ten houses of reformed friars (actually nine as Duruelo
  foundation had been moved to Mancera).[^26]

- There are some important common features in the account of the
  foundation of St. Joseph’s in Avila and of Duruelo.  Could you
  write down four or five that strike you?

- Having examined these two foundations, you should read the
  account of one or more other foundations. They are generally
  one or two chapters long:
    - Medina del Campo      (3-4);
    - Malagon               (9)
    - Valladolid            (10)
    - Toledo                (15-16)
    - Pastrana              (one for nuns and one for friars 17)
    - Salamanca             (18-19)
    - Alma de Torres        (20)
    - Segovia               (21)
    - Beas                  (22)
    - Seville               (23-26)
    - Caracava              (27)
    - Villanueva de la Jara (28)
    - Palencia              (29)
    - Soria                 (50)
    - Burgos                (31)

- The `Foundations` is a book that is an important reference to
  which we can return during this module and afterwards.
    - It can contribute greatly to our picture of Teresa as we

      she experienced.

# UNIT 4: TERESA AND PRAYER I

- One of the main concerns of Teresa of Avila in her reform was
  to make time for _personal prayer_ in the new communities.
    - The Incarnation monastery at Avila, we have noted, had
      splendid liturgy, but really no time for personal prayer.
      We now begin an exploration of Teresian prayer in six
      units.

- In this unit, we shall look at the following:
    - The general context of the times.
    - The occasion of Teresa’s writing on prayer.
    - Key issues.
    - Her famous definition of prayer in Life ch. 8.

- By the end, you'll have encountered the heart of Teresian
  prayer. You'll have met some of the important issues that will
  remerge in the following units (5-9)

### Unit 4: Context of the Times

- Printing in the Western world had begun about `1440` with
  typography, but it was Johan Gutenberg and his moveable type
  that made mass production of books possible from `1480` (just
  thirty-five years before Teresa was born).
    - The Church was worried by this new development.
    - Instead of manuscripts which were costly, slow to produce
      and not easily transported, there were now multiple copies
      disseminated—in the 15th century the average print run was
      between 100 and 300 copies.
    - Church authorities wanted to control this new phenomenon;
      they sought censorship, which in Spain became the role of
      the Inquisition.
    - In the first part of the 16th century Church authorities
      were very concerned about deviant mysticism, especially
      among the Alumbrados, mentioned in the first unit. Mental
      prayer became particularly suspect.

- Firstly, it seemed to be new and might lead to `false
  mysticism`.

- Secondly, it was feared that if `priests` became attached to
  it, they'd neglect their pastoral duties.

- Thirdly, mental prayer was deemed unsuitable for women: they
  were uneducated, probably light-headed and could easily go
  astray. They were to be restricted to attending Mass and to
  vocal prayer only.

- Teresa’s most radical position in the 16th century was her
  insistence on prayer, even against the known views of the
  leading Spanish theologians of the time, Melchior Cano and
  Fernando Valdés, the Supreme Inquisitor, who brought out an
  Index of books advocating prayer in 1559.
    - Valdés was known for his keenness in seeking out and
      preventing error; a fine example of his zeal was his charge
      levelled at the Spanish Dominican mystic, Louis of Grenada
      (1504-1588), of teaching mental prayer to totally
      unsuitable people like carpenters’ wives.

- A consequence was that there were very few books on prayer in
  circulation. When early in her religious life Teresa had to
  leave the convent through illness her uncle gave her a work by
  Franciscan Francisco de Osuna, `Third Spiritual Alphabet`.
    - This book introduced her to prayer, but it soon fell to the
      Index of prohibited books.
    - Several times Teresa found books helpful for herself or her
      nuns, only to find the same problem. It was one of the main
      reasons why she wrote on prayer.

- We find echoes of this suspicion of mental prayer in many
  places in Teresa’s writings. We can see a deeper meaning in
  a simple sentence like,
    - “Whoever has not begun the practice of prayer, I beg for
      the love of God not to go without so great a good. There is
      nothing here to fear, but only something to desire.” [^27]
    - And a few sentences later: “I don’t understand what they
      fear who begin to fear the practice of mental prayer.
      I don’t know what they are afraid of.”[^28]
    - She was here carefully skirting the theologians who were
      opposed to mental prayer.
    - She was advised later to tone down some of her implicit
      criticism of the Inquisition and of theologians.
    - Teresa also used what the American scholar Alison Weber,
      called “the rhetoric of femininity:”[^29]
        - Teresa sought to deflect criticism by speaking
          ironically about women’s weakness, implying that one
          could not expect too much from a poor women like
          herself—and all the time she was pursuing her goals
          with the utmost determination, shrewdness and single
          mindedness.

- Teresa’s importance in the history of spirituality cannot be
  exaggerated. It has been said that she and Ignatius of Loyola
  saved mental prayer for the Church.
    - Their methods and approaches are different; but at the most
      profound levels they show remarkable convergence.


### Unit 4: Context in Teresa’s Life

- We recall that Teresa’s early years—eighteen of them—in the
  Incarnation were not at all fervent.
    - Before her conversion at the end of that period in
      1556-1557 she did make some attempts at prayer, but she
      made two mistakes that she would very much regret later.

- Firstly, she gave into the temptation from the devil which
  suggested that she was not worthy to pray.[^30]
    - This false humility led to her abandoning prayer for nearly
      a year.

- In ch 22 of her `Life` she describes her second major mistake,
  namely how she was led astray by Alumbrados influences:
    - she neglected the Incarnation.
    - She would afterwards always regret this mistake:
        - I thought the `humanity [of Christ]` was an impediment.
          O Lord of my soul and my Good, Jesus Christ crucified!
          At no time do I recall this opinion I had without
          feeling pain; it seems to me I became a dreadful
          traitor—although in ignorance.[^31]

- In various places in her works we'll find allusions to these
  errors. You will spot some of them, now that you've been
  alerted to their existence and their place in Teresa’s life.

- There is another reason for her writings on prayer. She had
  learned from Osuna three stages of prayer:
    - `vocal prayer` of which the Our Father is the model.
    - The second was `meditation` and reflective which were good
      thoughts about the _life of Our Lord_ or other truths of
      the faith.
    - Thirdly there was `mental prayer` or spiritual prayer.[^32]

- Recall again the need to grasp language and terms not through
  an ordinary dictionary, but as they are used by Teresa.
    - In this matter we need help. You'll find the notes and
      introductions to her works helpful, as well as writers like
      Archbishop Rowan Williams.

- The first difficulty arises in distinguishing the last two
  stages of prayer. There was also an issue stemming again mainly
  from Alumbrados circles of '_no pensar nada_' (to think of
  nothing).
    - Being empty, not having thoughts was seen as a higher form
      of prayer. People tried to induce such emptiness; for
      Teresa this seemed to lack humility and to be a waste of
      time. We are not to barge into higher forms of prayer until
      prepared and invited by God.

- A bigger problem was that Teresa’s confessors did not
  understand the mystical graces that she was receiving; they
  thought that if she wrote what she was experiencing, the
  matters might be clearer and could moreover be studied at
  leisure.
    - As Teresa sought advice from learned men, she in turn
      brought some of the most illustrious theologians of the
      time along the path of prayer.
    - Her Jesuit confessor, Balthasar Alvarez, once pointed to
      a large pile of books and said to another Jesuit,
        - “All these books I read in order to understand Teresa
          of Jesus.”(33)
    - The addressee of Teresa’s `Vida`, García de Toledo, was to
      her “a father and a son;” he guided her especially by
      giving reassurance; she in turn led him deeply into prayer.

- We were taught how to pray as children; do we need ongoing
  education in this area?
    - Spiritual direction?
    - What encouragement or inspiration has Teresa, as you have
      met her in these units, offered you?
    - How important is the Incarnation in your own approach to
      prayer?


### Unit 4: The Life/Vida

- The main theme of `The Book of Her Life` (henceforth `L`) is
  prayer. It is not an autobiography in the usual sense; instead
  of recalling events of her life, it's more a celebration of
  what God has done for her; she once called it `The Book of
  God's Mercies`.
    - In format it resembles a long letter, with only a later
      version broken up into chapters, adding headings.
    - The main features of the book can be summarized.
    - As we have already noted (end of unit 2), it is in four
      parts:
        - Early life (sins, graces, vocation) chs 1-10
        - Treatise on the degrees of prayer   chs 11-22
        - Mystical life                       chs 23-31
        - Effects                             chs 32-40


### Unit 4: Life - chapter Eight

- The Catechism of the Catholic Church gives St. Teresa’s
  definition at the beginning of its treatment of contemplative
  prayer.
    - Note carefully the terms used. This text is yet another
      confirmation of the need for care in dealing with the
      language of spirituality!

- What is contemplative prayer?
    - St. Teresa answers “Contemplative prayer [oración mental]
      in my opinion is nothing else than a _close sharing_ between
      _friends_; it means taking time frequently to be alone with
      him who we know loves us." Catechism # 2709

- Notice that Teresa speaks of “mental prayer,” which the
  Catechism translates as “contemplative prayer.”
    - Moreover, though its text footnotes the Kavanaugh-Rodriguez
      translation of Teresa, it changes their “intimate sharing”
      into “close sharing” Teresa actually wrote amistad
      [friendship].[^34]

- The use of Teresa’s text as an introduction by the Catechism is
  an invitation to consider her teaching carefully.  We should
  also review its context.
    - The wider context is the substantial treatment of the
      degrees of prayer [the “Four Waters” allegory] in
      chs.11-22.
    - Following the late Fr. Jesús Castellano Cervera, OCD we can
      see the whole of ch. 8 and ch. 11:1-5 are like a great
      prelude to the tract on prayer.[^35]

- In chapter seven of the `Life` Teresa deals with her father’s
  death, with his great growth in prayer and with the benefits
  she received from his confessor, a Dominican, Vincente Barrón.
    - Teresa at that time had more or less given up prayer, but
      he led her back. She experienced  great tension:
        > I was living a very burdensome life, because in prayer
        > I understood more clearly my faults. On the one hand
        > God was calling me; on the other hand I was following
        > the world. All the things of God made me happy; those
        > of the world held me bound. [^36]

- In the following chapter she is even more explicit in looking
  back over nearly twenty years:
    > I should say that it is one of the most painful lives,
    > I think, that one can imagine; for neither did I enjoy God,
    > nor did I find happiness in the world. When I was
    > experiencing the joys of the world, I felt sorrow when
    > I recalled what I owed to God, When I was with God, my
    > attachments to the world disturbed me. This was a war so
    > troublesome that I don’t know how I was able to suffer it
    > even a month, much less for so many years. [^37]

- In this chapter there are several important themes:
    - God’s mercy despite her infidelity (8:2-4);
    - The great good of prayer, that is mental prayer (8:5);
    - Prayer to be kept up:
        - “I can speak of what I have experience of. It is that
          in spite of any wrong they who practice prayer do, they
          must not abandon prayer since it is the means by which
          they shall remedy the situation; and to remedy it
          without prayer would be much more difficult. May the
          devil not tempt them, the way he did me, to give up
          prayer out of humility.” (8:5).
    - Prayer is not to be feared:
        - “I don’t understand what they fear who begin to fear
          the practice of mental prayer. (8:7).
    - The difficulty of entering prayer especially at the
      beginning; anything else seemed preferable; the time passed
      so slowly. The temptations to avoid prayer or to give it
      up. (8:7 and 10).
    - The essential need of humility (more developed elsewhere);
    - Prayer is the door to God’s favours. It was through prayer
      that Teresa shook off her dangerous attachments and
      friendships (8:9 and 11).

- We are now in a position to appreciate more Teresa’s
  definition. Prayer is to be seen in the perspective of God
  loving us first (see John 15:15).
    - God offers _friendship_.
    - The way to respond to that friendship is through _spending
      time_ with God; it is to be _frequent_, thus emphasising
      the need for perseverance.
    - It is to be _alone_, marking the exclusivity and depth that
      the friendship is to reach.


### Unit 4: What goes on in Prayer?

- There is no “Teresian Method” of prayer. It is what arises out
  of cultivating friendship.
    - Teresa often says that she could not pray discursively with
      her intellect (going over a theme actively reflecting,
      asking: what? who? why? when? how? about the topic).
    - Instead she thought of Christ within her.
    - She had many distractions. She also found a book helpful,
      or a picture, or nature (fields, water or flowers reminded
      her of her Creator).
    - Teresa did not have a vivid concrete imagination.
    - She described her situation as speaking to someone in
      darkness; she is certain of the person’s presence, although
      she cannot see.[^38]


### Unit 4: Conclusion

- Later in the module we'll find more indications about what
  Teresa was doing at prayer, or more accurately what God was
  doing.
    - At this point we note her central intuition that prayer is
      a gift, that is about love and that the best category in
      which to consider it is _friendship_.
    - Already at this point we have considered the background to
      her teaching on prayer:
        - the spiritual climate at the time;
        - her own progression; and
        - the central meaning of prayer.

- To what extent do you think the idea of friendship and
  relationship is helpful in thinking about prayer?
    - Recall different ideas you may have had, or heard of, about
      the kernel of prayer. What do you consider it to be?

- In the next module we begin her treatment of the “Four Waters.”
