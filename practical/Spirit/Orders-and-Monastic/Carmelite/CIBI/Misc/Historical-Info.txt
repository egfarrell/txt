### History
    - 1200-1214: 
        - The Carmelite Order originates as a group of hermits
          living on Mount Carmel in Palestine, seeking to follow
          the prophet Elijah's contemplative example.

    - 1214-1217: 
        - The first Rule of St. Albert is given by Albert
          Avogadro, Patriarch of Jerusalem, providing a formal
          structure for the community.

    - `1247`: 
        - Pope Innocent IV modifies the Rule of St. Albert to
          adapt the Carmelite way of life to `mendicant` ideals,
          allowing for greater engagement in preaching and
          ministry.

    - 1251: 
        - According to tradition, the Blessed Virgin Mary appears
          to St. Simon Stock in England and gives him the Brown
          Scapular as a sign of her protection.

    - 1291: 
        - The fall of Acre marks the end of the Crusader States,
          forcing the Carmelite Order to leave Mount Carmel and
          spread to Europe.

    - 1318: 
        - Pope John XXII approves a stricter version of the
          Carmelite Rule, affirming their mendicant identity.

    - 1370s: 
        - Gerard of Bologna introduces early reforms emphasizing
          stricter adherence to poverty and contemplation,
          influencing later reform efforts.

    - 1413: 
        - The General Chapter of the Order in Piacenza implements
          reforms addressing discipline and the spiritual decline
          of some Carmelite communities.

    - `1432`: 
        - Pope Eugene IV grants the Carmelites the bull Romani
          Pontificis Providentia, which `mitigates the Rule` and
          raises concerns about laxity within the Order.

    - 1452: 
        - The Mantuan Reform begins, emphasizing strict
          observance of poverty and solitude within the order.

    - 1476: 
        - Blessed John Soreth, a significant reformer, revises
          the Carmelite Rule and establishes the Carmelite Third
          Order for laypeople, encouraging broader participation
          in Carmelite spirituality.

    - 1499: 
        - Reformist Carmelite houses associated with the Mantuan
          Reform are granted additional autonomy, fostering
          greater renewal within the Order. (Albi)

    - 1562: 
        - St. Teresa of Ávila founds the first convent of
          Discalced Carmelites (the reformed branch), San José,
          in Ávila, Spain.

    - 1568: 
        - St. Teresa and St. John of the Cross establish the
          first monastery of Discalced Carmelite friars at
          Duruelo, Spain.

    - 1581: 
        - The Discalced Carmelites are formally recognized as
          a separate branch of the order at the General Chapter
          in Alcalá de Henares, Spain.

    - 1591: 
        - St. John of the Cross dies, leaving a legacy as
          a mystic, poet, and reformer.

    - 1617: 
        - The Discalced Carmelites are divided into separate
          provinces for men and women, further organizing the
          reformed branch.

    - 1726: 
        - St. Teresa of Ávila is canonized by Pope Benedict XIII,
          solidifying her status as one of the greatest figures
          in Carmelite history.

    - 1826: 
        - Pope Leo XII officially approves the Feast of Our Lady
          of Mount Carmel for the universal Church, highlighting
          the order's Marian devotion.

    - 1876: 
        - The Carmelite nuns of Lisieux accept Thérèse Martin,
          who becomes St. Thérèse of Lisieux, one of the most
          beloved saints of modern times.

    - 1925: 
        - St. Thérèse of Lisieux is canonized by Pope Pius XI,
          greatly influencing Carmelite spirituality worldwide.

    - 1950: 
        - Pope Pius XII declares St. Thérèse of Lisieux
          a patroness of the missions, linking the Carmelite
          contemplative life to global evangelization.

    - 1965: 
        - The Second Vatican Council brings renewed attention to
          the Carmelite charism of contemplation and reform,
          encouraging adaptation to modern needs.

    - 1970: 
        - St. Teresa of Ávila and St. Thérèse of Lisieux are
          declared Doctors of the Church, recognizing their
          profound theological contributions.

    - 2001: 
        - The 750th anniversary of the Brown Scapular devotion is
          celebrated, emphasizing the ongoing importance of
          Carmelite spirituality.


### Holy Roman Empire

- The Holy Roman Empire was an attempt to continue the legacy of
  the Roman Empire in Europe, but it was quite different in
  practice. Here's how it came about and what it represented:

1. Origins and Legacy: 
   - The original Roman Empire fell in the West in 476 CE, but
     its cultural and political legacy endured. In 800 CE, Pope
     Leo III crowned Charlemagne (King of the Franks) as "Emperor
     of the Romans," marking the foundation of what later became
     the Holy Roman Empire.
   - The idea was to revive the unity and authority of the
     ancient Roman Empire under Christian leadership, blending
     Roman traditions with medieval European governance.

2. The Holy Roman Empire's Reality:
   - The Holy Roman Empire was less centralized than the ancient
     Roman Empire. Instead of a unified state, it was a loose
     collection of territories, principalities, duchies, and
     kingdoms, each with its own ruler, laws, and traditions.
   - The emperor was often more of a symbolic leader, chosen
     through an election by powerful regional rulers (the
     *prince-electors*), rather than an all-powerful autocrat
     like Augustus or Constantine.

3. Charles V's Role:
   - By Charles V's time (1519–1556), the Holy Roman Empire still
     carried the name and some symbolic authority of Rome, but it
     was increasingly fragmented, particularly due to religious
     tensions like the Protestant Reformation.
   - As emperor, Charles faced the monumental task of maintaining
     unity across his sprawling domains and addressing the
     challenges posed by Protestantism, Ottoman expansion, and
     internal political conflicts.

- In essence, the `Holy Roman Empire` wasn't a direct
  continuation of ancient Rome but an attempt to preserve and
  reinterpret its legacy within the medieval Christian world.
    - Voltaire famously quipped that the Holy Roman Empire was
      "neither holy, nor Roman, nor an empire," but it remained
      a significant institution in European history for over
      a millennium.


### Thomas Connecte
    - He was a 15th-century French Carmelite friar known for his
      fiery sermons and controversial stance against
      ecclesiastical corruption. 
    - Born in Brittany around the late 14th century, Connecte
      gained prominence as a reformer who strongly criticized the
      clergy's moral laxity, particularly their opulent
      lifestyles and indulgences. His sermons often targeted what
      he saw as hypocrisy within the Church, including the vanity
      of women and the behavior of some clerics.
    - Connecte's outspoken nature and bold denunciations earned
      him a following among common people but also brought him
      into conflict with church authorities. 
        - He moved across different regions of Europe preaching
          reform, but his inflammatory rhetoric eventually led to
          accusations of heresy. 
        - In 1432, during a stay in Rome, he was arrested and
          brought to trial. The Church convicted him of heretical
          practices and dissent, culminating in his execution by
          burning at the stake.
    - Though his efforts to reform the Church were suppressed,
      Thomas Connecte remains a notable figure in Carmelite
      history and the broader Church, illustrating the tensions
      between reformist ideals and institutional authority in the
      late medieval period.
