Saint Teresa of Avila, born in 1515, lived through significant
religious and cultural shifts like the Renaissance and
Reformation. Despite early illness, she became a renowned mystic
and religious reformer in Avila. The city, a hub of noble and
religious activity, significantly influenced her ideas. Avila's
rapid growth due to the wool trade created social and economic
tensions, impacting religious dynamics. Reformers emerged,
advocating education, poor relief, and clerical training, in
contrast to traditional religious expressions. Teresa's efforts
to reform the Carmelite order gained support amid these changes.
The city faced decline post-Teresa's death, exacerbated by
economic and demographic challenges. Scholarly interest in Teresa
and her era has grown, unveiling Avila’s ethnic diversity and
religious reforms. Recent studies have deepened understanding of
Spain's socio-religious history, exploring Teresa's role in
religious reform and her complex relationship with obedience and
authorship.The book celebrates the 2014 edition of "The Avila of
Saint Teresa" with acknowledgments to various scholars. It
explores Avila's history, marked by battles between Christians
and Moors on the Castilian plateau. King Alfonso VI claimed Avila
in 1083, prompting its Repopulation with settlers from Spain and
France. Built with Moorish prisoners' help, Avila's imposing
walls signified its military society, leading to its moniker
Avila de los Caballeros. A local legend of Jimena Blazquez
illustrates the community's values and women's role in conflict.
By the late Middle Ages, Avila had close ties with the Castilian
monarchy, being named Avila del Rey. During Teresa de Ahumada's
birth in 1515, Avila had 4,000-6,000 people and experienced
economic growth fueled by the wool trade. Artisans, especially
wool workers, dominated neighborhoods like san Esteban. This
area, despite being industrious, had a sketchy reputation.
Histories suggest unsavoriness around San Esteban, particularly
concerning the placement of San Segundo's relics.Antonio de
Cianca criticized the chapel's location near tanneries and its
unsavory visitors. Despite resistance, Avila's elite moved the
relics to a more secure location. This became a pattern of elite
influence in Avila's ecclesiastical decisions. The city's markets
were bustling trade hubs, central to Avila's economy. These
marketplaces, like Mercado Grande and Chico, hosted fairs and
were pivotal in regional commerce. Avila, though active in trade
and industry, retained a rural character with agriculture right
at its gates. The moriscos, Muslim converts, and Jews contributed
significantly to Avila's economy. The Jewish community faced
increasing hostility in the late fifteenth century, leading to
forced conversions. Despite conversions, suspicion persisted,
birthing a "converso problem." Aristocratic families dominated
Avila politically and religiously, shaping the city's values.
Avila's nobility, including powerful families like the Davila and
Bracamonte, exerted significant influence, driven by concerns of
family honor and legacy.In 16th-century Avila, elite families
sought status through claims of ancient ancestry, often tracing
back to the city's medieval Reconquest. This interest in
genealogy linked to values of military valor and heraldry. Many
families falsely asserted noble roots from the Reconquest, while
others had more recent origins, often linked to dynastic wars.
The notion of "pure" Christian blood became essential for honor
due to the limpieza de sangre doctrine, and converso origins were
often concealed. Aristocratic families orchestrated elaborate
rituals and legal battles over parish antiquity to claim
precedence in religious ceremonies.

Avila's city council, predominantly aristocratic, maintained
a strong oligarchic influence despite royal oversight attempts.
Council positions were often hereditary, with regidores passing
roles to their sons. City governance involved various
responsibilities, including trade regulation and public works,
often performed alongside the crown's corregidor, whose
effectiveness varied. The continuity of family names in politics
characterized the city council, evident in families like the
Peso, who held power for generations. Some elite families also
controlled the bishopric, underscoring their significant societal
influence.In 16th century Avila, bishops were often absent from
their dioceses due to court duties or travel. When present, they
faced conflicts with the cathedral chapter, like Bishop Alonso de
Vilches, who needed papal help to resolve issues. Bishops often
appointed family members to positions, causing friction with the
chapter. Due to bishops' frequent absences, the cathedral chapter
became the primary diocesan administrator, overseeing religious
activities and managing landholdings. The dean, as chapter
leader, handled important ecclesiastical matters and became one
of Avila's powerful figures, often from elite families. The
chapter consisted of various clergy and lay employees, with
dignitaries holding the most prestigious positions. Family names
persisted in the chapter across generations through nepotism,
such as the Cabero family, who dominated cathedral prebends for
generations. Families like the Mujicas formed complex networks of
patronage and alliances, securing influence in both church and
city council. Aristocratic families commonly founded and
supported monastic institutions, reflecting their power and
impacting figures like Saint Teresa. In Avila and elsewhere,
religious reform movements surged, inspired by concerns over
church corruption, and were particularly championed by the
"Catholic Monarchs," Ferdinand and Isabella. This governmental
involvement in ecclesiastical matters prompted a wave of monastic
foundations and reforms across Avila and Spain.In 16th-century
Avila, Isabella sought papal approval to reform religious houses,
emphasizing correcting dissolute clerical behavior and financial
mismanagement. She aimed to create respectable religious
communities serving God and society. Inspector Alfonso Carrillo
de Albomoz noted the decline of some convents and worked to
combine resources, aiming to establish well-endowed houses
focused on prayer. This spiritual renewal coincided with efforts
by Avila's elites to bolster their political and familial status.
Figures like Juan Nunez Davila and women, such as Catalina Guiera
and Maria Davila, led the founding and rebuilding of religious
institutions, using religious patronage to assert economic and
social independence. These female founders, often widows without
children, used their resources to establish convents where they
served as abbesses, maintaining control and status. They also
ensured lineage ties through patronage roles, using religious
foundations to reinforce family identity and prestige.In 16th
century Avila, influential families controlled religious
endowments to extend their influence. By establishing hereditary
roles like lay patron and abbess, they secured prayers for their
kin, expressing concern for salvation and commemorating family
unity. Notable examples include Dolia Maria Davila and Dona Maria
de Herrera, who used iconography and ancestral remains to
reinforce family links. Elite women like Dona Maria Davila merged
spiritual and dynastic goals, preserving wealth while securing
their family legacy. The city's population rapidly grew, drawing
nobles and peasants alike to urban life. This demographic surge
led to a "construction boom," improving roads and buildings,
while religious institutions expanded their premises to
accommodate increased urban activity.In 16th century Avila, urban
expansion led to conflicts over space and property. Residents and
religious orders often disputed boundaries and public land use.
Overcrowding and housing shortages were severe, with some living
in marketplaces. Water scarcity was a chronic issue due to low
rainfall and poor storage solutions. Despite technical efforts,
the city turned to spiritual remedies. Poverty was widespread,
prompting the establishment of hospitals like Santa Escolastica
and San Lazaro. Food shortages led to high grain prices, forcing
the city to regulate and eventually create a public granary.
A new social class of wealthy, non-noble men emerged, playing key
roles in trade and finance but facing social barriers. They used
lawsuits to gain status and privileges, exemplified by the
Sanchez de Cepeda family's legal battles for noble status. These
challenges reflected broader social tensions in Avila's evolving
urban landscape.In 16th-century Avila, many sought noble status
through manipulation and corruption, as demonstrated by the
Cepeda family's victory in a social status lawsuit. They formed
alliances with elite city officials through strategic marriages.
Another notable conflict arose over the relics of San Segundo,
with affluent merchants opposing the cathedral's claim to them.
Meanwhile, social unrest brewed as Avila's "new men" sought
political influence, eventually joining the Revolt of the
Comuneros against King Charles V's fiscal policies. This revolt
began as an elite-driven movement but turned more popular and
violent. The rebellion ultimately failed, with elites distancing
themselves to protect their privileges.In the aftermath of the
Revolt of the Comuneros, Avila's merchants and artisans suffered
dashed hopes for political representation. Many rebels faced
heavy fines, and some were excluded from royal pardons. Avila's
oligarchy, unsettled by the uprising, took measures like founding
a public granary to prevent future violence. The city's
population grew, attracting rural migrants and creating
opportunities for "new men" seeking influence through courts and
strategic alliances.

Religious reform emerged as a response to urban growth,
emphasizing personal conversion and aiding the poor. These
movements paved the way for Teresa of Jesus's monastic reforms.
Influential preacher Juan de Avila championed active, committed
clergy focused on education and charity. His works criticized
a system favoring the elite, advocating for a clergy serving all
people, regardless of wealth. Despite challenges, his educational
endeavors, like the "Doctrina Cristiana," spread, popularizing
humanist educational theories and inspiring lasting educational
reforms in Spain.Gaspar Daza played a pivotal role in religious
reform in 16th century Avila. Influenced by Juan de Avila, he
gathered a group of clerics and devout laymen dedicated to
spiritual renewal. Daza was notably involved in various religious
reforms and was recognized as an effective preacher and
compassionate caretaker of the poor. He inspired many, including
prominent figures like Teresa de Ahumada, to pursue Carmelite
reform. The foundation "Los Ninos de la Doctrina Cristiana,"
supported by Daza and his circle, focused on forming character
and preparing young men for priesthood.

The Jesuits, led by Ignatius Loyola, expanded on Juan de Avila's
methods, emphasizing public preaching, education, and relieving
poverty. The Jesuits’ approach addressed the spiritual needs of
individuals, offering a new hierarchy based on mission and
spiritual commitment rather than social status. They established
training colleges like San Gil to focus on academic and moral
education. Jesuits such as Baltasar Alvarez were noted for
counselling diverse social groups, emphasizing internal spiritual
transformation and service to others. Their work reflected a new
reform vision that prioritized personal and societal improvement
through spiritual and educational advancement.The Jesuits gained
substantial influence in 16th-century Avila, supported by
reformers like Gaspar Daza and Hernando Alvarez del Aguila.
Alvarez helped establish a Jesuit college in San Gil with papal
approval. He had connections with important local families,
including Teresa of Jesus's relatives. This group prioritized
kinship and community bonds over ancestry, supporting education
and reform efforts, including the Jesuit initiative. Meanwhile,
spiritual reform also sparked a grassroots movement of ascetic
individuals open to both genders.

Centers of female spiritual activity flourished, reminiscent of
previous mystical movements. Mari Diaz, a revered figure in
Avila, epitomized this. Born around 1490, she moved to Avila
seeking spiritual fulfillment. Despite familial opposition, she
committed to a life of poverty and spiritual devotion. Diaz
resided modestly, gaining fame for her extreme piety and
self-sacrifice. Diaz sought Jesuit guidance, eventually being
directed by them. 

Her stay at Dona Guiomar de Ulloa's palace amplified her
reputation. Despite hardships, Diaz earned respect and became
widely influential, even counseling the young and mediating
disputes. Known for her wisdom and piety, she aided people across
social classes. Mari Diaz showcased virtues like humility,
influencing clerical behavior and becoming a symbol of spiritual
reform.Mari Diaz, a prominent beata in 16th-century Avila, was
deeply involved in religious education and reform. She taught
children prayers and provided moral education to young women,
creating a network of spiritual support. Her austere lifestyle
drew the city's poor and elite women alike. Despite her poverty,
Diaz was revered and had a grand funeral. Her death united
Avila's divided community. Together with Gaspar Daza, Diaz helped
propose establishing a seminary at San Millan, supported by
Bishop Mendoza, which marked progress in clerical education.
These reforms reflected broader spiritual innovations in Avila
during this period. Teresa de Ahumada, influenced by these
reforms, founded the Carmelite reform, blending institutional
discipline with emotional spirituality. Her early life was shaped
by familial issues of honor and spirituality. Teresa eventually
embraced a religious life, joining and transforming the convent
of La Encarnacion, which depicted social hierarchies of its
time.Nuns brought aristocratic values to convents, filled with
gossip and ambitions. Teresa, raised similarly, valued reputation
but eventually rejected shallow "black honor" after spiritual
awakenings. Relaxed Carmelite rules allowed nuns to keep wealth,
leading to disparities within convent life. Teresa initially
participated in this laxity, enjoying comforts and maintaining
social ties, even living outside the convent due to health or
duty. Her gradual conversion spanned twenty years, spurred by
spiritual readings and divine revelations. Despite inner
conflict, she began embracing asceticism and mystical
experiences, with Jesuit guidance affirming her spiritual path.
Support from religious reformers fueled her passion for reform.
Mystical visions, like her pierced heart, catalyzed her
commitment to God. In 1560, a vision of hell inspired an
organized response to serve God, marking her deepening spiritual
journey.Teresa de Ahumada experienced a spiritual awakening,
leading her to reform the Carmelite order. She envisioned
a convent embodying poverty and humility, inspired by Peter of
Alcantara's Franciscan ideals. Despite resistance, Teresa
cherished voluntary poverty, rejecting fixed incomes for her
convent, San Jose. Her resolve was strengthened by visions and
spiritual allies, emphasizing detachment from worldly ties. This
approach fostered equality irrespective of lineage, challenging
societal norms. By prioritizing humility and devotion, Teresa of
Jesus crafted an egalitarian religious community, reflecting her
deep transformation from noblewoman to humble nun.Teresa of Avila
founded a convent with thirteen nuns, stressing humility and
asceticism. She valued small communities, fearing large ones led
to factions. Teresa mandated strict poverty, with simple attire,
and rejected her previous comforts. Enclosure was important to
her, allowing spiritual freedom from worldly distractions. She
emphasized obedience, believing it brought spiritual security.
Teresa’s reform aimed to counter Protestant influences through
prayer, seeing it as a powerful weapon against heresy. Her
convents were established to support church reformers and provide
spiritual support. Initially, her efforts faced resistance from
city officials concerned about economic impacts and societal
norms. Teresa’s focus on mental prayer also drew opposition.The
16th-century Spanish religious climate was repressive, especially
towards expressions of heterodoxy associated with women. Despite
this, Teresa of Jesus led the Carmelite reform, driven by her
powerful personality. Initially, Spain embraced diverse spiritual
movements, supported by figures like Cardinal Cisneros. However,
a shift occurred around 1555, as Protestantism spread in Europe,
prompting Spain to guard Catholic orthodoxy fiercely. The
Inquisition cracked down on dissent, focusing on women and
conversos, while the Church at Trent defined Catholic doctrine
strictly.

Printing was tightly controlled; many vernacular religious texts
were banned. This environment fostered tension between "learned
men" and spiritual advocates supporting personal piety and
prayer. Teresa, championing personal mystical experience, faced
suspicion from academics fearing heterodox influences. Her
spiritual experiences fueled mistrust among church authorities
who were wary of independent female spirituality.

Teresa's reform, emphasizing mental prayer and rejecting fixed
incomes, challenged traditional religious practices, leading to
opposition from Avila’s leaders. With support from local
reformers like Gaspar Daza and Peter of Alcantara, Teresa slowly
gained acceptance. Bishop Alvaro de Mendoza became a crucial
ally, helping legitimize her movement. Her reforms aligned with
the Council of Trent's values, reinforcing the Church's religious
and hierarchical structure. Over time, Teresa attracted
followers, including many conversos, to her egalitarian ideals.
Despite challenges, her reform spread through Spain, gradually
gaining acceptance and impacting the Catholic Church's mission
against Protestantism.By the mid-sixteenth century, Avila faced
intense scrutiny of mystical religious practices due to fears of
heterodoxy. Teresa of Jesus's efforts aligned with the Church's
focus on clerical control, leading to successful Carmelite
reforms. However, after her death in 1582, Avila underwent
profound economic and religious transformations amidst
demographic crises caused by droughts, locusts, and plague, which
hit the poor hardest. The wool trade's decline exacerbated
poverty, while the expulsion of the Moriscos in 1610 deeply
affected the city's economy. The population dwindled, and Avila’s
status fell, luring nobles and professionals to Madrid. Religion
shifted back to dynastic foundations, influenced by nobles who
funded religious institutions. Reformist religious ideals faded,
replaced by traditional practices, anchored in economic and
social realities.In 1577, Don Rodrigo del Aguila founded
a Discalced Franciscans monastery in Avila, marking aristocratic
engagement in religious reform. The Jesuits of San Gil faced
financial strain in the 1580s. By 1616, with pressure from
Rodrigo del Aguila and the Jeronimites, they moved, accepting
money despite mission compromises. In 1623, Don Diego de Guzman
transformed San Ignacio with a substantial endowment but imposed
strict obligations, prioritizing family prestige over religious
reform. During this period, Saint Teresa's Discalced Carmelites
experienced shifts, forced to accept financial stability over
strict ascetic principles. Similarly, entry became restricted,
favoring nobility. Bishops gained control over Avila's religious
scene post-Council of Trent, challenging local elite dominance.
Bishop Diego de Alava initiated reforms in the 1550s. However,
effective consolidation of episcopal power came later with
strong-willed bishops like Pedro Fernandez Temiño in the 1580s,
inciting tensions with cathedral clergy and the city. Ultimately,
aristocratic interests overshadowed ecclesiastical autonomy, as
seen in the integration of noble families' needs into the
religious fabric of Avila.In 1568, Bishop Alvaro de Mendoza
approved a colegio in Avila, which faced opposition when Pedro
Fernandez Temiño proposed a new seminary. The city council
resisted Temiño's plan, fearing it contradicted the founder's
intentions. Despite legal delays, the Royal Council eventually
sided with Temiño. However, his untimely death in 1590 left his
ambitions unrealized. His successor, Jeronimo Manrique de Lara,
adeptly reconciled disputes and enhanced the bishop's power.
Manrique facilitated the integration of San Millan as a seminary
under Tridentine guidelines. He also strengthened the cathedral's
religious status by moving the relics of San Segundo there in
1594, positioning the cathedral as the city's spiritual center.
Manrique's actions elevated both Avila's cathedral and his own
episcopal authority. His successors, including Lorenzo Otaduy,
continued expanding the church's influence, capitalizing on the
centralization of religious control initiated by the Council of
Trent.In 1610, Bishop Otaduy sought to elevate the status of San
Pedro del Barco's altar. He organized a ceremony to move the
saint's bones to a grander tomb. This event celebrated the
bishop's authority more than the saint. Otaduy also initiated
proceedings for the beatification of Marl Diaz, a revered local
figure. However, the process stalled at his death, and his
successor struggled with fading memories.

Francisco de Gamarra, Otaduy's successor, continued the effort.
In 1619, he reburied Marl Diaz's remains with much ceremony. Only
Avila's elite attended, contrasting the humble woman's popular
legacy. The church was moving away from popular saint cults
towards official channels for canonization. Maria Vela,
a noblewoman and nun, faced skepticism for her mystical
experiences. Her ascetic practices and claims to divine insight
clashed with her aristocratic peers' values.

Maria's life was marked by a struggle between divine inspiration
and monastic obedience. She adhered to severe penance, which
unnerved her superiors and confessors. Her behavior sparked
controversy, as many saw her as prideful or delusional. Unlike
Teresa of Jesus, who navigated authority carefully, Maria often
seemed at odds with societal norms, straining her relationships
within the convent.During a controversy over frequent communion,
Dona Maria Vela faced heresy suspicions in Avila. Her private
spiritual practices and influence on younger nuns alarmed
authorities. In 1603, she was denounced to the Inquisition, but
her calm defense and influential family led to her acquittal. She
found solace with a supportive confessor, Dr. Miguel Gonzalez
Vaquero, until her death in 1617, which sparked rumors of
sainthood. Bishop Francisco de Gamarra promoted her cult, seeking
prestige for Avila. Despite efforts to start beatification, her
writings remained unpublished and forgotten until 1959. Maria
Vela’s case illustrates the tension between personal spirituality
and institutional control in early modern Avila. After her death,
Avila experienced stagnation, but its association with Saint
Teresa of Avila later revived its significance.Improved transport
links have made Avila more accessible to tourists. Tourism is now
vital to Avila's economy, like wool was in the 16th century.
Teresa of Avila is seen as a real person, not just a saint. Her
Jewish roots are now a popular research subject, especially
post-Franco. Ecumenism and feminist studies offer new insights
into her life. Revisions have deepened the understanding of
Teresa. Understanding her within the context of her time and city
highlights her achievements.
