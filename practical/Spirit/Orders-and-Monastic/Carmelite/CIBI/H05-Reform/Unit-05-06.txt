### Unit 5: Teresa of Ávila

- When Carmelite reform of the 16th century is studied, it's
  usually synonymous with St Teresa of Ávila.
    - In this course we'll examine other attempts at reform and
      renewal, but Teresa is very much an important figure.

- Teresa has been presented in different ways, often as one of
  the great heroes and exemplars of the Catholic Counter
  Reformation and of the Spanish nation.
    - Did she not die saying “I die a daughter of the Church”?

- In `1939` at the end of the Spanish Civil War, Padre Silverio
  published "St Teresa of Avila, Supreme Synthesis of her Race"
  which included a chapter entitled ‘For God and Spain’.[^1]
    - From this viewpoint, she is presented as an old Christian
      aristocrat who unquestioningly accepted all that the
      leaders of the Church decreed.

- In `1946` Alonso Cortes published the ‘Pleitos de los Cepeda’.
    - He had discovered this document which showed Teresa’s
      paternal grandfather Juan Sanchez was actually a wealthy
      _converso_ merchant who was prosecuted by the Toledo
      Inquisition in 1485 for being a `judaizante`, a secret
      practitioner of Jewish customs.
    - He was found guilty and forced with his sons, including
      Teresa’s father Alonso, to do public penance.[^2]

- This prolonged ritual humiliation was traumatic and had long
  lasting effects on the family. Its significance is that Teresa
  was a member of a group regarded with such envy and suspicion
  that the Inquisition was reactivated under the Spanish monarchy
  in 1478 precisely to target conversos.
    - It was such a cause of shame that her beloved father lived
      a lie his whole life-passing as a pure-blooded Old
      Christian noble.

- From about the age of 5 to 7, Teresa would have seen the
  comings and goings of lawyers from her home as her father and
  her uncles engaged in a law suit to be recognised as pure blood
  Christian aristocrats.
    - Their suit was contested by those in Ávila who knew the
      story.
    - Considering that Ávila had a population of between 3,000
      and 5,000 it would be surprising if people didn't know
      their background, especially as her grandfather ran a silk
      shop.
    - In any event it was a hollow victory. None of Teresa’s nine
      brothers could live with honour in Spain. All of them went
      to America as conquistadores in service of the king.
        - Except for Lorenzo they did not succeed in gaining
          wealth and honour.

- Far from being a complacent aristocrat, Teresa had the critical
  eye of one on the margins. She was a prophetic figure.
    - Teresa’s social critique focused on the concept of honour-
      `honra negra`.
    - Honour is the recognition that one’s social legitimacy
      resided in the opinion of others.
    - It was fragile, as easily shattered as proverbial glass.
      Honour could be lost by suspicion of sexual impurity,
      a failure to keep the demeanour appropriate to one’s social
      position, or public acknowledgement of Jewish or Moorish
      ancestry.

- From her _insecure place_ within Church and society Teresa saw
  her times with fresh eyes.
    - Her critical horizon was ultimately shaped by her
      _relationship to Christ_ in interior prayer.
    - Teresa does not simply echo what the leaders of Church or
      state teach or ordained.
    - Because of her marginal status, she was an independent
      woman and more original and important to us for that.
    - Teresa in her own person unites the contemplative, the
      social critic and the prophet within the church.

- She was outspokenly critical of the way women were treated in
  the Church and society of her day.
    - She founded a community out of her _frustration_ with
      religious life that was caught up in the social structures
      of her day.
    - Her own need was for a community that gave her _freedom to
      prayer_ as the Lord had taught her.
    - She also believed she had a mission to contribute to the
      church in its time of crisis.
    - As these were so personal, it's not surprising that her
      experience touched the spiritual hopes and desires of other
      young women. The convents she founded answered a need for
      the women of her time.

- Teresa founded new communities that empowered and protected
  women’s religious experience.
    - This was no small thing as the leaders of the church were
      frightened by the appearance of the `Alumbrados`
      (Illuminist prayer groups) in southern Spain.
    - These people, mostly women, believed that since they had
      reached union with God they had no need of the sacraments
      or of governance by the clergy.

- Teresa was critical of the leaders of the church, the bishops,
  preachers and theologians.
    - Instead of using spiritual means they relied on the use of
      the ‘secular arm’ (coercive state force) in spiritual and
      theological disputes.
    - Orthodoxy was protected by instilling _fear of
      punishments_, which included execution, for crimes against
      the Catholic Church.
    - Similarly faith was protected by the 'Index of prohibited
      books'.
        - For all their knowledge, the leaders of the Church had
          no _personal relationship with Christ_.
        - She was similarly critical of confessors who because
          they were without spiritual experience were frightened,
          and laid burdens on the shoulders of women like her
          whom God had graced.

- Mostly daringly, in the face of the Inquisitorial watchdogs of
  orthodoxy, not only did Teresa write books about interior
  prayer, itself considered a highly suspect activity, she also
  wrote a book of Meditations on the `Song of Songs`.
    - This was the most controversial book in the Bible.
    - Luis de Leon, an Augustinian professor in Salamanca, was to
      suffer imprisonment from 1572 to 1576 for translating the
      Song of Songs into Castilian and circulating his
      manuscript.

- At a time when women were expected to obey the Pauline
  injunction to ‘be silent in the church,’ Teresa assumed the
  role of a teacher of Scripture, recollection and interior
  prayer.
    - It was Jesus himself who guided Teresa, when she thought
      her critics may be right:
        - “Tell them they shouldn’t follow just part of scripture
          but they should look at other parts, and ask them if
          they can by chance tie my hands.” [^3]
    - In many ways Teresa then was a prophetic figure within the
      church. It is in this context that she died marvelling, “at
      the end, I die a daughter of the church.”

- Pause for a moment to reflect on the relationship between
  prophecy and the reform of Religious Life.
    - Would you agree that initiating reform is a form of
      prophecy?


### Unit 5: Doña Teresa de Ahumada y Cepeda

- Teresa was born in Ávila in 1515. This is the early modern
  period, not the medieval world.
    - The world had begun to change through the invention of
      a new technology, the `printing press` in the 1450's and
      the subsequent widespread distribution of books.
    - Reading became a silent, personal and interior act.
    - It changed the sense of who the reader was. The modern
      sense of the individual self, the turn to the subject, had
      arrived.
    - The `Reformation` began in 1517 in Germany with Luther's
      ninety-five theses and his excommunication in 1521 at the
      Diet of Worms.
    - Because of the printed book Luther's ideas spread rapidly
      throughout Europe.
    - The Scientific Revolution began in 1543 when Copernicus's
      `De revolutionibus orbium coelestium` and Vesalius's `De
      humani corporis fabrica` were published.
        - The first challenged how the universe was understood
          and the other began the modern science of anatomy.
        - What began to matter more and more was exact
          observation rather than traditional theories.

- In 1492 the New World was discovered by Columbus with its vast
  treasures of silver and gold.
    - That same year the Moors were expelled from Granada and
      after almost 700 hundred years what we know as the nation
      of Spain began to be formed.
    - In that same year the Jews were given the choice of
      conversion to Catholicism or exile and the first dictionary
      of the Castilian language was printed.
    - A whole new horizon of Spanish literature was opened.
    - Early modern capitalism was also at work in Spain and had
      the effect of creating disastrous social change for both
      peasants and aristocracy.
    - The world in Teresa's time was changing rapidly.

- Alonso, her father, was a devout man much concerned with his
  honour.
    - He lived as a Christian aristocrat helped by the dowries
      provided by his two wives (he was widowed twice).
    - Teresa loved both her parents and from her mother learnt
      the pleasures of reading.
    - This was at a time when illiteracy was considered not a bad
      thing in a woman.
    - In December `1528` Teresa’s mother, Doña Beatriz, died.
    - Her father became worried about his vivacious daughter
      losing her honour and placed her in a boarding school
      within the cloister of the reformed Augustinian nuns of Our
      Lady of Grace near the family home in Ávila in 1531.
    - After 18 months there she became ill and returned home.

- On 2 November, `1535`, Teresa had entered the Carmelite convent
  of the Incarnation in Ávila.
    - She took the habit exactly a year later, in 1536 and made
      her vows on 3 November, 1537.
    - Life at the Incarnation was traumatic for Teresa. [^4]
    - After her profession she became seriously ill in autumn
      1538 and spent time outside the monastery returning to the
      security of her family staying mostly with her father.
    - At one time she was given up for dead. Her recuperation was
      slow and painful. Only gradually did she regain her ability
      to walk and be mobile.

- During a stay at the house of her uncle Pedro she read the
  `Third Spiritual Alphabet` by Franciscan `Francisco de Osuna`.
    - She was thus introduced to the spiritual writing of Spanish
      Franciscan school who promoted recollection and interior
      prayer.
    - The way of interior prayer was opened for her outside her
      own Carmelite monastery. Teresa incorporated what she
      learned from the Franciscans, Dominicans and other
      spiritual writers into practice.
    - For example, Teresa took the practice of two hours daily
      mental prayer from Osuna put it in her Constitutions.[^5]

- It has been speculated that a copy of the `Decem Libri` of the
  14th century Felipe Ribot was read by Teresa and that this also
  formed her spiritual outlook.
    - This is very unlikely.[^6]
    - If she had been inspired by Ribot she'd have recommended
      his volume when she informed the nuns of the books they
      were to have in their library in her Constitutions.
    - However, there is no mention of the `Decem Libri` in her
      list of good books.[^7]
    - In my view Teresa was very influenced by contemporary
      writers and does not display expertise in Carmelite history
      or traditions of spirituality.
    - You who read this text know so much more than she did about
      Carmelite history and tradition.

- Her practice of `interior prayer` (oración mental) waxed and
  waned over the years until in `1544` she, helped by her
  Dominican confessor, committed herself firmly to the practice
  of interior prayer.
    - Her father Alonso had died on 26 December `1543` having
      been nursed by Teresa.
    - After his death Teresa sorted out his legal and financial
      affairs which took some years as he had died bankrupt.
    - In order to preserve his honour he had lived beyond his
      means. Pride and shame, the fruits of the honour system of
      limpieza de sangre (purity of blood, i.e. freedom from the
      taint of Jewish ancestry), were in terrible tension in the
      life of Teresa’s family.
    - This was also evident, perhaps, in Teresa’s need to loved
      and accepted.

- Life in the Convent of the Incarnation, as we have seen, had
  a very particular internal dynamic.
    - It's hard to generalise from the customs of Carmelite
      monasteries of the early modern period elsewhere to the
      conclusion that the nuns of the Incarnation would also have
      done this or that.
    - Teresa’s references to Carmelite tradition are vague:
        - She affirms that she belongs to the Order of the
          Blessed Virgin whose habit she wears and refers to the
          holy fathers of the time of Order’s beginnings.
        - There were certainly good, religious women in the
          community. But there were also too many nuns and
          seculars who were placed there as in a parking lot by
          their families.

- With the excessive numbers came an inequality between the rich
  and the poor members of the community. Among those who were
  wealthy there was a spirit of competitive family pride and envy
  of others good family.
    - There were factions and cliques as much for emotional
      support as for honour and prestige. Much time was spent in
      prayer for the founders and benefactors of the community.
    - The business of survival also meant the nuns spent time
      ingratiating themselves and seeking donations from
      prestigious families.
    - Teresa’s personality and charm made sure she was involved
      in these efforts. She was a favourite in the convent
      parlours and homes of community benefactors.

- Pause for a moment to reflect on St Teresa's life at the
  Incarnation.
    - Would you agree that she, and the whole community, were in
      need of `reform`.
    - What criteria should guide such a conclusion in relation to
      a particular Religious community, in your opinion?


### Unit 5: Her new life

- In this situation, where Teresa herself led a privileged life,
  she struggled to balance her religious and social lives.
  Teresa’s recounts the most important reform she would make in
  Life. [^9]

- Her CONVERSION might also be called the `healing`, restoration,
  reformation, renewal, or transformation of her own life.
    - Previously to this experience she had visions of Christ
      disapproving of her friendships. But now, in Lent 1554, she
      developed the habit of turning to Jesus in anxious
      isolation in the `Garden of Gethsemane`.
    - She did this from the conviction that “as a person in need,
      He had to accept me” (my emphasis) Life, 9.4.
    - Teresa always responded so eagerly to affection that her
      need for it was undoubtedly great.
        - As Rowan Williams writes, “it is hardly fanciful to
          detect the voice of someone who finds it extremely hard
          to _believe she is lovable_.”[^8]
    - She was able to see herself now as welcomed and valued in
      herself simply as a friend whose “mere presence might be
      a grace or comfort in the suffering of another”.[^9]

- Teresa’s conversion was a divine gift that touched her, set her
  free and allowed her to be healed and made whole.
    - The Lord who accepts Teresa as a person also accepts her
      tending his much suffering and wounded Body (that is the
      Church).10
    - Jesus does not just accept Teresa, he has a mission for her
      in the Church. In an age when women could not speak or act
      in public Jesus wants Teresa to do something.
    - It marks the beginning of her new sense of calling and
      mission. Her life now is a companionship with God that
      brings her transformative energy and creativity. She goes
      from being an immobile young woman to a dynamic founder,
      writer and teacher.

- Teresa now entered a time of purification.
    - She was helped by reading the `Confessions` of St Augustine
      to discover Christ dwelling within the soul.
    - She began to receive mystical graces which her shocked
      confessors attributed to the devil.
    - She was rescued by the spiritual direction of a young
      Jesuit confessor, Diego de Cetina, the first of many
      Jesuits who aided Teresa.
    - Unlike many confessors, the early Jesuits, due to their own
      experience of the Spiritual Exercises, were not afraid of
      spiritual experience or mystical graces.
    - Their spiritual direction emphasised the discernment of
      spirits and a turn to interiority. With this assistance and
      the hope it gave Teresa flourished.

- In order to consult with the Jesuits who had come to Ávila to
  open the College of San Gil Teresa lived for an extended period
  with her friend Doña Guiomar de Ulloa11. There she also
  consulted the Discalced Franciscan founder, `St Peter of
  Alcántara`.
    - She does not appear to have had a Carmelite confessor prior
      to St John of the Cross.

- In August `1559` Inquisitor General, Archbishop Fernando Valdez
  published his 'Index of prohibited books' which consigned to
  the bonfire many of the spiritual works that had fed Teresa.
    - The chilling face of the Inquisition at this time can be
      seen in the case of Doctor Cazalla and his friends and
      Archbishop Bartolomé Carranza.

    - In March 1559 the intellectual priest with Erasmian and/or
      Lutheran sympathies `Agustin Cazalla` (1510-59) was brought
      before the Inquisition with his brothers, sisters and four
      nuns who were part of the small group that met with him.
        - Their crime was to believe that we are saved by God's
          grace alone, which is clearly if ironically orthodox
          Catholic doctrine according to the Lutheran, Roman
          Catholic Joint Statement on Justification of 1999.
        - Cazalla and the group were executed in May 1559. Those
          who confessed were shown the “mercy” of being publicly
          strangled before being burnt at the auto de fé.
        - His family home was pulled down so that their memory
          would be banished. In 1820 a street in Valladolid was
          called after Cazalla and a plaque placed on the site of
          the family home. You can still retrace the journey to
          the auto-de-fé courtesy of an historical leaflet.

    - In August that same year, `1559`, Bartolomé `Carranza`
      (1503- 1576) the Dominican Archbishop of Toledo and Primate
      of all Spain was accused by his Dominican brethren of
      heresy, arrested, imprisoned and interrogated.
        - He eventually died under a cloud in Rome after
          a notorious and prolonged process.
        - This was after the Council of Trent had declared his
          suspect book free of heresy in 1563.
        - Pope Gregory XIII allowed a laudatory inscription to be
          place on his grave in Santa Maria sopra Minerva.

- The Inquisition targeting Lutherans, Judaisers, Moorish
  converts and Illuminist mystics none of whom were too clearly
  distinguished one from another.
    - The Inquisition sought to control people through fear of
      torture or execution.
    - The hierarchy of the Church who ran the Inquisition were
      not trusting people. They needed to control.
    - Teresa is living in a time when in important ways her
      spirituality that runs counter to that of the hierarchy.

- Teresa was formed in great measure by the `Erasmian movement`
  in Spain which sought a simpler, more biblical and interior
  Christianity.[^12]
    - To be a Christian humanist is basically to be optimistic
      about humanity. You could trust people and educate them.
    - As both Teresa and John of the Cross believe our vocation
      is spiritual marriage with God they are very optimistic
      about human potential.
    - After the accession of Philip II to the throne in 1556 this
      influence was replaced by a more hard-line approach which
      sought to impose a rigid orthodoxy.

- Teresa was bereft when the books that had nurtured her spirit
  were placed on the Index of Prohibited Books by the
  Inquisition.
    - However Christ himself reassured her in a vision during
      1560 that he would be her living book in future (Life,
      27.2).

- Teresa had a vision of hell (Life, 32.1-5) that same year which
  made a huge impact on her.
    - The effect was to move her to compassion for souls who were
      condemned to that fearsome place.
    - It is important to note that Teresa looks on the lost souls
      or those in danger of being lost with compassion and not
      with fear or condemnation.

- Especially she was moved to prayer for those she termed
  Lutherans but who were in fact French Calvinists.
    - She dedicated her quest for holiness and prayer and her new
      foundation to their salvation.
    - The impetus to her making a foundation was the break-up of
      the unity of the Church caused by the `Protestant
      Reformation`.
    - It is interesting that today there is an ecumenical
      Teresian Carmel which was founded in Finland in 2003.
        - Most of the members are Lutherans. What would Teresa
          think of Lutheran Carmelites?

- The Reformation was the great spiritual battle of her time and
  since she could not be on the front line as a woman then she
  and her sisters would pray for those who were the defenders of
  the church- bishops, priests and theologians.
    - That is, pray for the conversion of these clergymen from an
      orthodox if abstract or ideological faith to a personal
      faith grounded in a life changing _encounter with Christ_
      in interior prayer.
    - Living in palaces, or monasteries that were similar to
      palaces, and involved each day in worldly business the
      clergy and religious too easily lost an interior religious
      sense even while keeping all the externals.

- This motivation was to develop into a strong sense of the
  church and its urgent needs in her time.
    - For Teresa the life of contemplation of her new foundation
      had an essential apostolic and ecclesial dimension
        - (Way, 1.2, 5, 3.1-6).
    - These texts from the 'Way of Perfection' which was written
      to guide the sisters in San José and their successors are
      important in understanding how Teresa saw her new community
      in relation to the Church.
    - They are also illuminating in that we see her articulate
      clearly her opposition to the use of _force and domination_
      in the defence of the church and orthodoxy (Way, 3.1).
    - For her spiritual problems require spiritual solutions.

- Teresa’s awareness of the way the world and church did not
  value women was also expressed clearly in a passage that was
  suppressed and only recently recovered (Way, 3.7).
    - Teresa believed Jesus did not share this anti-feminine
      world-view and she herself trusted that women had
      a valuable contribution to make.

- Pause for a moment to reflect on the situation in the
  Incarnation.
    - If the community to which you belong were to drift into
      a similar situation, how do you think this might manifest
      itself?
    - For you personally, what would have been the positive and
      negative aspects of living in a situation such as that
      found in the Incarnation?

### Unit 6: Founding San José

- It was in `1560` that Teresa had the conversation in her cell
  with family and friends about founding a new community (Life,
  32.10).
    - To me, it's typically Teresian that the impetus to a new
      foundation should come about through a conversation among
      friends.
    - Teresa’s motivation for her wanting to found a new
      community was personal:
        - ‘I was thinking about what I could do for God, and
          I thought that the first thing was to follow the call
          to the religious life, which His Majesty had given me,
          by keeping my rule as perfectly as I could’
            - (Life, 32.9).
        - She was aware of the _compromised way of living_ of the
          Incarnation and also aware the rule followed throughout
          the Order had been mitigated.

- Teresa and her friends sought to model their way of life on the
  example of the discalced Franciscan nuns: [^1]
    - ‘if we couldn’t be nuns like the discalced it would still
      be possible to found a monastery’ (Life, 32,10).
    - The discalced Franciscan nuns were so famous for the
      renewed fervour of their life that Teresa and her friends
      doubted they could follow that path.[^2]
    - The discalced movement began among the Franciscan women and
      men religious and was marked by its Christocentric
      spirituality, its dedication to poverty and prayer.

- The discalced Franciscans were the contemporary benchmark for
  seriously reformed religious life.
    - This fervour came to the Carmelites via St Teresa.
    - Among the most important discalced Franciscans was `St
      Peter of Alcántara` a genuinely holy, if austere and
      mortified, man. [^3]
    - Peter was a very significant spiritual director and
      confessor for Teresa because he understood her from his own
      experience and so was able to explain things, comfort and
      encourage her.[^4]

- This idea of a new, fervent monastery in the contemporary style
  “like the discalced” may have remained a dream had Teresa not
  been determined to make it a reality.
    - She received confirming words from Christ,
        - “He commanded me to strive for this monastery with all
          my powers and he made great promises that it would be
          founded and that He would be highly served in it.”[^5]

- Teresa began to discuss and make plans for a `new monastery`
  with her friend the young widow, Doña Guiomar Ulloa.
    - Doña Guiomar obtained the provincial’s permission for
      a new, small community of thirteen nuns.
    - A petition was sent to Rome at this time but was lost.
    - Teresa was encouraged by Peter of Alcántara but discouraged
      by others and so the matter lapsed for some six months.

- In 1561, Gaspar Salazar, the new Jesuit rector of San Gil,
  encouraged the impulse towards a `new foundation` which was now
  renewed (Life, 32,7-11).
    - A request for papal bull to authorise the foundation was
      sent to Rome by Doña Guiomar.
    - So Teresa, on the advice of Peter of Alcántara, used the
      cover provided by her friend to get around her superiors,
      the prioress of the Incarnation and the Carmelite
      Provincial.
    - St Peter of Alcántara as a spiritual director gave more
      importance to promoting holiness and what we now call the
      charisms of the Holy Spirit than becoming scrupulous about
      seeking approval from unsympathetic superiors.

- Rumours of what was happening began and opposition grew.
    - While at Santo Tomás on the feast of the Assumption 1561,
      Teresa received a vision of the `blessed Virgin` and St
      Joseph who gave her their blessing for new foundation
      (Life, 33.14).
    - While Teresa was strengthened in her resolve it was an
      opportune time for her to be absent from Ávila.
    - Providentially a woman of the high nobility in Toledo, Doña
      Luisa de la Cerda, had been widowed and asked the Carmelite
      provincial for a nun companion in her grief.
    - Teresa received this command on Christmas Eve 1561 (Life,
      34.1). Teresa was to spend over six months in Toledo.

- While there Teresa perceived the demands of aristocratic life
  as being in some ways like _being in prison_.
    - Nobles were not free to act outside certain strict
      conventions, their dress, speech, demeanour and deportment
      had to be appropriate to their status.
    - `Honour` regulated everything. Perhaps Teresa had thought
      wealth brought liberty to do as one wished. Now she knew
      from experience that it did not.

- She also gives us a glimpse of her method in dealing with
  priests and theologians when she tells us how she directed her
  friend the Dominican `Garcia de Toledo` (Life, 34).
    - She listened to his response when she asked what God has
      done with him in America in the ten years since they last
      met.
    - Of course, when he has finished he asks her what God has
      done in her life. From this conversation comes his request
      for her to write her Life.

- Teresa's tells us she
    - 'always thought he had a great mind. I considered the
      wonderful talents and gifts he had for doing much good,
      were he to give himself totally to God' (Life, 34.7).
    - She tells us 'even though I considered him good this didn't
      satisfy me, since I wanted him to be very good; and so
      I said to his majesty: “Lord, you must not deny me this
      favour; see how this individual is fit to be our friend.”'
      (Life 34.8).
    - For Teresa, her friendship was a means of bringing
      theologians to Christ. When they saw what she had in prayer
      they wanted it too.

- On `1562` Pope Pius IV issued the brief, `Ex parte vestra`, for
  the new foundation in Ávila.

- While Teresa was in at the house of Doña Luisa in Toledo she
  spends a fortnight with a `beata, María de Jesús`, a widow who
  had entered a Carmelite monastery in Grenada but had left with
  the intention of founding a new reformed community in Alcalá
  (Life, 35.1).

- What is remarkable about this encounter is that prior to it,
  Teresa confesses herself to have been ignorant of the Carmelite
  Rule, either mitigated or ‘primitive’.
    - The time with the illiterate beata, María de Jesús, had
      enabled Teresa to personally discover the Rule.
    - It's ironic that this existential discovery is made _after_
      the decision was reached to make a new foundation.
    - Previously in preparing for the foundation she had only
      pored over the constitutions.
    - Her spiritual experience resonated with the Rule “even
      before she had any intellectual knowledge of it through
      reading or by other means.” [^6]
    - From this time onward the observance “without mitigation”
      of the “primitive rule” identified the Discalced
      Carmelites:
        - “We observe the rule of our Lady of Mount Carmel and
          keep it without mitigation.”[^7]

- Teresa's understanding of the primitive rule was an
  interpretation coloured by the reform movements of her time and
  country, which focussed on the fervour and austerity of the
  first hermits.
    - It's not clear to me what Teresa would have changed in the
      way of life of her community if she had not discovered the
      'primitive' rule, given that the texts are identical.
    - It is living the 'primitive' rule that distinguishes
      discalced Carmelites.

- From her first real reading of the Rule, Teresa saw at this
  time the word `poverty` and understood this to mean that the
  1247 ‘primitive’ rule of Innocent IV required absolute poverty,
  before its mitigation in 1432.
    - While this is not true, she took this reference as
      a justification for founding her new monastery without the
      _security of an income_.
    - She received the support of Peter of Alcántara in this
      stand. Teresa’s use of the rhetoric of rigorist reform was
      to create problems in the future.
    - In time she was to insist she was not a friend of rigour
      but of virtue. However the future divisions among the
      discalced go back to Teresa. Her own spirit was to grow and
      expand considerably beyond rigorism.

- The night Teresa arrived back in Ávila the papal brief
  authorising the new foundation arrived from Rome.
    - The `bishop, Alvaro de Mendoza`, was opposed to the new
      foundation until he was persuaded by Peter of Alcántara and
      a layman, probably Don Juan Blázquez, to accept it even
      though it was to be _founded in poverty_.[^8]
    - When Mendoza met Teresa he became a friend and remained
      a firm supporter both in Ávila and when he was transferred
      as bishop of Palencia.

- The preparations were made in great secrecy. On 24 August
  `1562` four young women took the habit in San José and the
  Blessed Sacrament reserved so that the foundation was made.
    - Teresa and two other nuns of the Incarnation were present
      (Life, 36.5, 6). The initial peace was replaced by popular
      consternation at this new foundation especially that it had
      been made in poverty and so would be dependent on the alms
      of the citizens of Ávila.

- Since Teresa had decided it was easier to ask forgiveness than
  permission she now had to face the consequences of all her
  _secret activity_ for the new foundation.
    - Teresa had her own doubts about the austerity of the new
      house and the attitude of the Carmelite provincial since
      San José had been founded under the jurisdiction of the
      bishop of Ávila not under the order (Life, 36, 7-8).
    - Teresa found her peace about the former _in prayer_ before
      the tabernacle but her prioress ordered her to return to
      the Incarnation.
    - She gave an account of her activity to the prioress who was
      somewhat placated but who then placed the matter in the
      hands of the provincial (Life, 36.12).

- Teresa had been determined about what she had done and did not
  seek to excuse herself but begged to be pardoned and punished
  and asked that he not be vexed with her.
    - She received a serious reprimand and he ordered Teresa to
      go before the community and to give an account of her
      actions to them.
    - In a conversation afterwards she was promised to be able to
      go the San José if and when the public clamour about it
      died down.

- At a furious city council meeting, the Dominican Domingo Bañez
  defended San José by pointing out that whether or not it should
  be suppressed was a matter for the bishop not the city council.
    - Nonetheless an official denunciation was sent to the royal
      council.
    - Teresa was ordered to have nothing more to do with the new
      foundation. Teresa’s friends, the priest and delegate of
      the bishop in regard to San José, Gaspar Daza and the
      devout layman Francisco Salcedo worked hard to protect San
      José.
    - While another priest Gonzalo de Aranda represented the
      interests of San José community before the royal council.
    - At his death Peter of Alcántara appeared in a dream to
      Teresa urging her to persevere in poverty rather than
      compromise.

- The foundation of San José upset the _pattern of aristocratic
  endowment_ and dependence which signified a family's power,
  wealth and prestige and which tied the community into
  a relationship of mutual obligation and social privilege.

- All in all, these difficulties took two years to resolve.
    - In January 1563, however, Teresa was allowed to become
      prioress of San José. The provincial’s official licence was
      dated 22 August, 1563. When she left the Incarnation, in
      a gesture of great significance, Teresa changed the style
      of her `name` from Doña Teresa de Ahumada y Cepeda to Madre
      Teresa de Jesús.
    - She was no longer an aristocratic lady (doña) but a mother
      (madre).
    - What was important now in regard to her identity and
      mission was not her honourable status but her relationship
      with Jesus.

- The humble manual work required of each nun in the community
  effectively did away with aristocratic privilege and created
  a community of equals.
    - While all were equals it was not a miserable community.
    - The use of the title with the prefix ‘de’ (of) instead of
      a surname showed that each sister was an aristocrat of the
      kingdom of God and its love and not of this world of
      domination.
    - The community pointed beyond this world to the
      eschatological fulfilment of the kingdom in heaven.
    - As a little heaven on earth for all its austerity the
      community was a sign of hope.

- As she walked from the Incarnation to San José Teresa prayed in
  the crypt of the church of San Vicente before the statue of the
  Virgin.
    - Here she left behind another symbol of her old life- her
      shoes.
    - She had now become a discalced Carmelite wearing
      `alpargatas` (rope-soled espadrilles).

- Little by little the chief opponents of the new monastery were
  won over and became ardent supporters.
    - Teresa characterised the way of life of the community as
      being small, strict, enclosed and with some austerity as
      meat was never eaten except in cases of illness and there
      was an eight month fast in accordance with the unmitigated
      Carmelite Rule.
    - The community was committed to live in such solitude and
      silence that the _thought of speaking to others was
      a burden_ unless such conversation had to do with the love
      of Christ and was for spiritual benefit.

- The life the sisters lived was a happy one and they lived it
  with ease. Teresa found that her own `health` and that of the
  other nuns had never been better than in this new community
  (Life, 36).

### Unit 6: A new style of life

- The way of life of San José was clearly different from that of
  the Incarnation and Teresa guided the community for those first
  experimental years before she drafted `Constitutions` for her
  new community as the papal brief of 7 February, 1562 had
  authorised her.
    - In her first draft of the Way of Perfection written in 1566
      she refers to these Constitutions.
    - The aim and spirit of the community is in fact expressed in
      both works.
    - When the prior general John Baptist `Rossi` visited in 1567
      Teresa was able to show him a text of the Constitutions and
      ask his approbation.

- When it came to writing law Teresa was much more reserved,
  spare and frugal with words in comparison to when writing about
  prayer.
    - What were more important were relationships- the primary
      relationship with Christ, the relationships between the
      sisters in the community and relationships with those who
      might be spiritually assisted.
    - Legislation and observance were simply the means to further
      these relationships. Her concern was with being
      a particular kind of person rather with than doing things.

- It should not be presumed that Teresa’s constitutions are
  simply stricter and more demanding than those that existed in
  other monasteries of Carmelite nuns.
    - In some surprising ways she is more ‘liberal’, more
      trusting. She can be this because of her own experience.
    - Alison Weber writes in regard to the Teresian approach to
      enclosure:
        - Although the nuns were to observe stricter enclosure
          than their sisters under the mitigated rule, they were
          permitted broader freedom in seeking out spiritual
          guidance and consolation from laymen as well as
          clerics.
        - The pre-Teresian Constitutions required the presence of
          a third party whenever a nun spoke with outsiders:
            - “Let no sister approach the locutory, or the
              turnstile, or the window or speak with any person
              from the outside, without the special permission of
              the prioress, unless she is accompanied by one or
              two of the oldest and most discreet nuns”
              (Constituciones pre-teresianas, 328).
        - In contrast, article 15 of the 1567 Constitutions
          states simply,
            - [A nun’s] dealings should be with persons who are
              an edification and help for the life of prayer and
              who provide spiritual consolation rather than
              recreation.
            - Another nun should always be present unless one is
              dealing with conscience matter” (3.3, my emphasis).
            - How, when, and with whom such private conversations
              were to take place were left to the discretion of
              the prioress.
            - Once again, Teresa’s previous experience may well
              have influenced her constitutional ideals; laymen,
              notably the pious Francisco de Salcedo, had played
              critical roles in her own spiritual development.[^9]

- The day was to be filled by silence and work was preferably
  done in solitude rather than in a common work-room.
    - The garden of San José had hermitages for the nuns’
      retreats.
    - Teresa wanted a community that was not tied up with
      obligations to say prayers or sing offices for the repose
      of benefactors.
    - She therefore sought a simpler, more sober style of
      liturgy.
        - The sacraments of the church, especially the Eucharist,
          were nonetheless highly valued. The stress on
          simplicity in worship was so that the sisters could
          have time for personal interior prayer.
        - Interior prayer was at the heart of the life of the
          individual and community. Christ, the beloved of the
          soul, would himself strengthen and guide the sisters.

- Interior prayer was controversial because it was seen as
  opening a path of personal spiritual freedom _beyond clerical
  control_.
    - The unity of the church was to be protected by ensuring
      that faith was mediated through sermons and set prayers
      rather than through personal reading of scripture or
      interior prayer.

- Teresa wanted a style of community life that was small, simple
  and austere where all were equal and all were friends:
    - ‘here all must be friends, all must be loved, all must be
      held dear, all must be helped’ (Way, 4.7).
    - They were all sisters, all equal, and none were ever to use
      the title doña or depend on their family for assistance
      (Constitutions, 30).
    - Two hours daily of mental prayer were balanced by two hours
      daily of recreation in common.
    - The sisters were to be good friends of Christ who were good
      friends to one another. They are called to be a poor and
      humble college of Christ joyfully living without worldly
      honour not just as nuns but also as hermits (Way Escorial
      20.1).

- The prioress was _not a prelate_ but to lead by example, in
  taking the first place at sweeping and other manual labour
  (Constitutions, 22).
    - Manual labour is significant as the honour of aristocrats
      would mean such labour was impossible for them.
    - The equality of the Teresian community was a lived parable
      of the reign of God and a critique of the prevailing values
      of society in her time.

- One of the important innovations Teresa introduced affects the
  place of spiritual direction.
    - While she insisted on the liberty of the sisters in regard
      to confessors she also declericalised spiritual direction.
    - She gave a significant role in spiritual direction to the
      prioress.
    - The prioress who lived with the sisters knew them better
      than any visiting confessor and so she could give much more
      sound advice.
    - Each sister is to give her a confidential monthly account
      of how she has done in prayer (Constitutions, 41).
    - Teresa was confident the Lord will enlighten the prioress
      in spiritual discernment and guidance.

- This form of life in San José was a creative recovery of the
  Carmelite order’s eremitical and contemplative origins.[^10]
    - Teresa certainly intended this and in this her concerns
      were different from the priors general from Soreth to Rossi
      and Trent in their attempts to reform the order.
    - It was not a simple restoration of the past but a profound
      _remaking of the past to meet contemporary needs_.
    - It was, for example, a creative response to her own
      difficulties with confessors and expressed her own
      aspirations for a life of prayer free of clerical control.
    - San José and the other discalced communities also met the
      aspirations of many young women for a meaningful spiritual
      life in the Church.

- Her foundations were also intended as a timely response to the
  _needs of the church_ as it faced the crisis of the
  Reformation.
    - Teresa was certainly a Carmelite but a Carmelite in her own
      way with her own spirit.
    - What was important was to return to the observance of the
      primitive Rule which she understood as a way of life
      devoted to the experience of interior prayer.

- What is surprising was the lack of any strong Carmelite support
  or spiritual direction for Teresa in her years in the
  Incarnation and as she began her new foundation.
    - Her reading was wide and eclectic yet she did not mention
      any Carmelite book[^11] among those she requires for the
      spiritual reading of her nuns (Constitutions, art 8).

- Her understanding of interior prayer was shaped by her Jesuit
  spiritual directors and her reading of a variety of spiritual
  books in Spanish but most significantly the `Life of Christ` by
  Ludolf of Saxony (c. 1300-78) which was a version of the
  Gospels with a commentary from the Fathers of the Church.
    - This book is important because it is the only version of
      the New Testament that Teresa could read being a woman and
      not having Latin.
    - The Spanish version, like some modern Bibles, had the words
      of Jesus printed in red ink. For Teresa the `Life of
      Christ` was the Bible.

- Teresa also recommends books by the Spanish espirituales like
  those by
    - the Franciscan Peter of Alcántara,
    - the Dominican Luis de Granada,
    - as well as classics like
    - St Augustine’s Confessions and
    - the Devotio Moderna the Imitation of Christ by Thomas à Kempis.

- Her new community ‘offered a fundamental critique of the
  religious system closely associated with the values of honour
  and family’.[^12]

- Teresa was influenced by the contemporary renewal movements in
  the church:
    - “She drew from many religious traditions for her ideas and
      policies: voluntary poverty and asceticism, egalitarianism,
      religious autonomy, separation from family and society,
      strict obedience and enclosure, and an intense sense of
      mission, and she found models in the clerical and ascetic
      movements active in Ávila in the mid sixteenth century.
      Juan de Ávila, and his local followers, the holy people
      Peter of Alcántara and Mari Díaz and the Jesuits of the
      College of San Gil influenced her and became valued
      supporters.”[^13]

- Teresa developed and adapted the tradition to her times and her
  own needs and found that the new way of life answered to
  similar spiritual needs in others.
    - Teresa considered her time in San José as the happiest and
      most peaceful of her life.

- However her journey as a founder was far from complete. It was
  an evolving vocation, a process which developed and grew as
  long as she lived.
