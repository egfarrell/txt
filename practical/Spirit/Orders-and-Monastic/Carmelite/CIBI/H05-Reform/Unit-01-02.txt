### SUMMARY: 02 Charisms ... History
    1. The 'historical awareness' approach contrasts with
       classical views by emphasizing `change` and diversity over
       `static` norms.

    2. Religious communities often follow lifecycle of
        - growth,
        - consolidation,
        - decline, and
        - potential reinvigoration.

    3. Charisms are `dynamic` and must adapt to remain faithful,
       reflecting the church’s call for continual renewal.

    4. Reflection prompts an adaption of Carmelite and Gospel
       traditions while preserving its core values.


### SUMMARY: 03 Reform ... Teresa
    1. St. Teresa’s role as a reformer is multi-faceted, inviting
       critical exploration rather than simplistic labels.

    2. The concept of reform often implies `restoration` rather
       than `innovation`; Teresa’s approach was more creative and
       forward-looking.

    3. Jesuit historian O’Malley identifies five traditional
       reform methods but notes the absence of `transformation`
       or `revolution`:
            - suppression,
            - addition,
            - revival,
            - accommodation,
            - development

    4. St. Teresa’s work is described as a new and creative
       refounding, distinct from mere restoration or revival.

    5. Different perspectives on Teresa’s role highlight her
       contributions as a reformer, founder, and innovator.

    6. Teresa’s influence extends _beyond her own Order_, serving
       as a prophetic figure calling for spiritual renewal.

    7. The study of Teresa benefits from historical context,
       avoiding simplistic or biased interpretations, and the
       author values modern scholarship in understanding her.

### 02 Charisms and historical consciousness

- The study of history leads us to see how all things change.
  Bernard Lonergan spoke of the difference in interpretation
  between a person shaped by classicism, and someone with an
  historical consciousness.[^1]
    - For the `classicist`, culture is conceived normatively and
      things remain substantially the same over time and
      geography.
    - From an `historical` perspective one accepts change and
      diversity.
    - From one viewpoint a religious 'spirit' or 'charism'
      remains the same, sailing gloriously unchanged down through
      the centuries; for the other it changes, grows and declines
      in different situations and times.

- Historians of religious life show that typically, a newly
  founded community will show great energy and grow rapidly in
  numbers and geographical spread.
    - After some time, it begins to consolidate as the initial
      fervour is channelled into routine work and patterns of
      life.
    - It may well continue to grow but more slowly than before.
      Gradually it begins to _plateau_ and finds institutional
      stability and a recognised role in church and society.
    - Then will come a time of change when it will be slow to
      adapt to new situations with a resulting _lack of vitality_
      and _internal conflict_ and so a decline begins.
    - The internal conflict becomes not just about how things
      should be done but grows to question the very worth of the
      community’s life and mission.
        - Decline begins slowly but when its rate becomes
          exponential a critical point is reached.
        - A community may go out of existence, stagnate at a low
          level or be transformed and re-invigorated.
        - If this latter is the case the process begins again
          once more.
        - It would seem the `laws of entropy`, which describe how
          systems lose energy over time, apply in spiritual
          matters as well.[^2]

- Religious orders that have lasted more than two hundred years
  have probably been through this process more than once.
    - What works well in one period is no longer productive when
      economic, social and cultural systems change.
    - A charism _is not something given once and for all_ and is
      then unchanged, but is a gift of the Spirit that is living
      and calls one who has received it to be creatively
      responsive in different situations.
    - This might be called the church’s pastoral principle, which
      is behind what Pope John XXIII called ‘aggiornamento’,
      bringing up to date.
    - The Spirit calls people to witness to the Gospel and to
      proclaim it in ways that people of different times and
      cultures can understand and come to believe.
        - This does not mean that religious charisms must modify
          to accommodate to the spirit of the age but that they
          need to change in order to remain faithful to their
          calling and mission.
    - Fidelity has a spiritual dynamism, movement and direction.

- Let's pause to reflect on what bringing the Carmelite Order
  'up-to-date' might mean today.
    - How might it be related to the ongoing updating of the
      Gospel message so it responds to the 'signs of the times'.
    - In your view, are there both elements of the Carmelite and
      Gospel traditions that should be changed according to the
      signs of the times and elements that are perennial and
      unchanging?


### 03 Reform, renewal and Teresa

- The use of the terms 'reform' and 'renewal' in the title of the
  course have a variety of meanings. Our module will centre on
  the inspiring story of St Teresa of Ávila.
    - We'll critically explore her role, which has been described
      as reformer, restorer, renewer, _refounder_ and founder.
    - Interpreting the work of St Teresa has been fraught with
      conflict, and controversy. We need to attend to the facts
      and interpret them in a disinterested spirit.
    - My aim is not to precisely define Teresa with one of these
      terms but to invite you to deeper reflection and
      conversation not just about Teresa and her legacy, but about
      living a Carmelite vocation in our own times.

- It's no harm to reflect on the meaning of the words we use. If
  we say St Teresa is a `reformer` of the Carmelite order, what
  do we mean?
    - Did she reform lax morals and religious observances?
    - Or perhaps, did she return to the original intention of the
      Primitive Rule of Albert by renouncing the `mitigation of
      Eugene IV` thereby restoring what was there in the
      beginning?
    - Both of these meanings involve a sense of returning to an
      earlier golden age rather than innovation and adaptation to
      the needs of the present time.
    - The idea of a ‘reformed’ order also seems to imply an
      unhelpful sense of superiority over those who follow
      a ‘mitigated’ version of the rule.

- The Jesuit historian John O’Malley wrote a helpful article on
  the historical meaning of reform in the church[^3].
    - The style of reform that goes with a return to the
      `"uncorrupted past"` is marked by
        - ‘rejuvenation’,
        - ‘revival’,
        - ‘rebirth’ and even
        - ‘renewal’.
    - It assumes historical continuity rather than discontinuity.
      The past provides the substance and norms. This is even
      more so in any attempted restoration.

There are five `reform procedures` prompted by this style of
thinking:
    - (1) reform by excision or `suppression`
        - (remove threats to existing order)

    - (2) reform by `addition` or accretion
        - (add new things alongside existing order)

    - (3) reform by `revival`
        - (breath new life into existing order);

    - (4) reform by `accommodation`
        - (make adjustments for differences in times and places);

    - (5) reform by `development`
        - expand and mature to its final perfection what you have). [^4]

    - To these I would add that `restoration` is a return to the
      norms of the past which have fallen into disuse.

- What is notably absent from this list is reform by
  `transformation` or even by `revolution` for both of these
  imply at least a partial rejection of the past in the hope of
  creating something new.
    - The possibility of something ‘new’ and different is
      distinctively modern. We see it in the thinking of the
      Protestant reformers.
    - A question for us today is: did Teresa seek something _new_
      or merely a _return_ to the past?
    - Obviously while she didn't reject the past, she didn't
      believe in slavish imitation or restoration.
        - We'll explore Teresa’s creative and imaginative
          response to her times.
    - This birthed not a correction, restoration, renewal, or
      revival but something `new` with a life of its own.

- Here are some representatives of different approaches to the
  significance of St. Teresa. I have deliberately chosen them to
  show there're not entrenched O.Carm or OCD positions:

    - First, Fr. Gabriel Barry, OCD: (restore)
        > “We propose therefore, to approach Teresa’s life story
        > from one angle only: Teresa the reformer of the
        > Carmelite Order, keeping in view that her intention was
        > to restore the Rule of St Albert to its original
        > vitality.”

    - Then from Fr. Otger Steggink, O.Carm:
        > “Her work must not be looked upon as a simple reform-
        > the rooting out of certain abuses and the
        > re-organisation of the regular life. To see her work as
        > no more than a rebellion against abuses and
        > organisational shortcomings would be a very inadequate
        > view indeed. The new form of Carmelite life drew its
        > inspiration from a deep evangelical spirit and from the
        > eremitical and contemplative ideal of Carmel and so
        > deserves to be classed as a founding and creative work
        > rather than one of reform. Her reforming activity would
        > appear to be a secondary aspect of her work.”[^6]

- In the present Constitutions of the Discalced Carmelites friars
  and nuns, Teresa is spoken of as the founder who had
  a particular charism.
    - “The origin of our family in Carmel and the very nature of
      its vocation are closely bound up with the life and charism
      of St Teresa.”[^7]
    - “The full measure of the vocation of the Teresian Carmel is
      the fruit of the experience by which Holy Mother was
      `gradually enlightened` about the members still waiting to be
      united to the Mystical Body of Christ….
        - Her heart conceived the purpose of spreading the family
          of the first Discalced Carmelite nuns and extending her
          work by founding the Discalced Carmelite friars.”[^8]

- For the Discalced Carmelite friars, the 1975 formula for the
  renewal of vows approved by the 'Congregation for Religious and
  Secular Institutes' contains the following:
    - “With my whole heart I give myself to this family founded
      by St. Teresa”[^9]

- In the 1999 circular letter ‘Passing Through the Holy Door’,
  Fr. Camilo Maccise, Superior General of the Discalced
  Carmelites and Fr. Joseph Chalmers, Prior General of the
  Carmelites took a `third approach`:
    - “Among all the reform movements, that initiated by Teresa
      of Jesus with the help of John of the Cross is of special
      importance. Being more than a simple ‘reform’, it was
      a genuine `refounding`.
    - Their spiritual teaching and work in founding “reformed
      monasteries and convents” has for centuries been a source
      of inspiration for the entire Carmelite Order.”[^10]

- This last point is certainly true, and it might be argued that
  Teresa has been surprisingly influential in the original
  Carmelite order.
    - The word `refounding` was coined by the New Zealand Marist
      Gerald Arbuckle to denote when a `prophetic person`
      challenges church members to live again the purity of the
      salvation message and make it relevant to today’s world.
    - Such persons are always necessary for the vitality of the
      church and are needed too within religious congregations.
        - “They call us back to the purity of the founding myth
          of the congregation and point out ways in which the
          inner power of this myth can be applied to apostolic
          needs now.”[^11]

- So, there are different ways of understanding what Teresa
  sought to achieve. We need to proceed carefully and critically,
  paying attention to the context of her life.
    - When we have done that, we'll ask what Teresa can offer
      Carmelites and all people today.
    - Teresa is not the possession of any group or Order and
      shouldn't be used to bolster our own esteem and group
      prestige.
    - And as we shall see, there have been a number of refounding
      movements within both the Order of Carmelites and the
      Discalced Carmelites.

- The disinterested study of history is useful in bringing us to
  greater self-awareness. The task for all of us in this field is
  to apply the transcendental precepts of Bernard Lonergan:
    - “Be attentive, Be intelligent, Be reasonable, Be
      responsible.”[^12]

- Of course, I bring my own horizon of interpretation to this
  course. Put simply, I find Teresa and her writings inspiring
  and I've found the study of her to be personally fascinating,
  rich and rewarding. I'm grateful for being a Teresian
  Carmelite.
    - The more I've studied Teresa’s historical context the more
      her character has come alive.
    - Since the discovery in 1946 by Alonso Cortes of the
      `converso origins` of St. Teresa we need to see her in
      historical context rather than in an unchanging, classical
      or hagiographical way.
    - I've found much useful recent writing on St Teresa in both
      English and Spanish. The work of contemporary scholars,
      like _Jodi Bilinkoff_ and _Alison Weber_, is particularly
      stimulating.

- Reflect on how you understood the role of St Teresa in relation
  to the Carmelite Order in the past and how this module may be
  inviting you to clarify that role.
    - Do you think people's pre-conceptions might prevent them
      recognising who St Teresa really was?
        - Could this prevent the true significance of St Teresa
          being discovered and becoming more widely known?


# UNIT 2

### SUMMARY: 01 Carmelite Mitigation and Reform
    - The Carmelite charism evolved through growth, decline, and
      adaptation. Originating as hermits on Mount Carmel in the
      11th century, the Carmelites pursued prayer, penance, and
      Gospel authenticity, reflecting the broader eremitical
      movement reacting to Church corruption.
        - Urban lay communities and mendicant orders, like the
          Franciscans and Dominicans, also sought reform,
          embracing poverty and itinerant preaching.
    - The Carmelite Order transitioned from `hermits` to
      `European mendicant clergy` with Pope Innocent IV’s 1247
      mitigation of their *formula vitae*.
        - This shift aligned with papal efforts to utilize hermit
          groups for reform.
        - By the 14th century, the Order faced crises, including
          the Black Death, Church schisms, and declining
          spiritual fervor.
        - The `observance movement` emerged as a reform response,
          emphasizing adherence to the Rule, poverty, and
          community life.
        - However, this often led to _externalism_ rather than
          true interior renewal.
    - Mitigations like Pope Eugene IV’s `1432 relaxation` of the
      Rule highlighted tensions between adaptation and
      maintaining contemplative roots. Reform efforts aimed to
      balance the charism’s integrity with evolving contexts.

### SUMMARY: 02 The Congregation of Mantua
    - The Mantuan Congregation (est. 1442) sought a return to
      Carmelite origins, `rejecting mitigations` and emphasizing
      silence, cloister, and poverty.
    - Initially fervent, its observance relaxed over time, but it
      produced notable figures like Blessed Baptist of Mantua.
      The congregation was dissolved in 1783.

### SUMMARY: 03 The Reform of Albi
    - In 1499, Bishop Louis of Amboise initiated the Albi reform,
      inspired by the Mantuan Congregation.
    - This movement strengthened Carmelite observance in France
      and significantly contributed to the Order’s renewal.

### SUMMARY: 04 Monte Oliveto
    - Founded by Hugh Marengo, Monte Oliveto aimed to live the
      original Carmelite Rule. Initially a hermitage, it evolved
      into a priory and later a parish before being suppressed in
      1803.

### SUMMARY: 05 Blessed John Soreth’s
    - John Soreth (1395–1471), Carmelite friar from Normandy,
      significantly reformed the Carmelite Order during his
      tenure as Prior General (1451–1471). Emphasized communal
      life, shared prayer, and strict adherence to regulations.
        - His reforms were implemented through pastoral
          visitations, holding friars accountable, and imposing
          penalties for noncompliance.

    - Key achievements:
        1. Monastery and Friary reforms:
            - Instituted stricter communal living and
              renunciation of personal privileges, approved by
              the General Chapter in 1456.
        2. Carmelite Nuns and Third Order Foundation:
            - In `1452`, he welcomed the beguines of “Ten Elsen”
              into the Order, marking the beginning of `Carmelite
              nuns`.
            - A papal bull (Cum Nulla) formalized their status,
              allowing women to fully participate as Carmelites.
        3. Revised Constitutions:
            - His revisions (1462) reinforced observant
              practices.

    - Soreth’s approach often involved tough measures, including
      penalties and support of civil authorities to ensure
      compliance. His work fostered an ideal of enclosed,
      contemplative life for women, aligning with the broader
      church mandates of the era.

    - While his reforms revitalized the Order during his
      lifetime, their long-term impact was limited. The
      Carmelites eventually adapted to the humanist culture of
      the 15th century, diluting their original spiritual focus.

    - Reflection
        - Soreth’s legacy highlights the power of dedicated
          leadership in revitalizing religious life but also the
          challenges of sustaining reforms _imposed from above_.


### Unit 2: 01 Carmelite mitigation and reform

- The Carmelite charism, in fact, has not sailed unchanged
  through history but has had a history of growth and decline, of
  vibrancy and stagnation.
    - The first Carmelites were `pilgrims` who came to Mount
      Carmel to live a life of prayer and penance as hermits, to
      win the land of Christ for Christ.
    - From the 11th century the Church in Western Europe
      experienced a revival of the eremitical movement.
    - This was a creative response to a time of great economic,
      social and political change. The time of settlement was
      being left behind.

- Monastic reforms were led by men such as:
    - Stephen of Muret    founder: Grandmontines
    - Bruno of Cologne    founder: Carthusians
    - Romuald of Ravenna  founder: Camaldolese
    - These men explored an eremitical spirituality which took
      them beyond that of a church _which had settled in the
      world_.
    - This settlement gave the Church great `power`, along with
      privileges and exemptions which led to wealth, luxury and
      corruption.
    - The hermits fled worldly compromise and sought a radical
      Gospel authenticity by a return to the humility and poverty
      of Christ and his disciples.
    - They lived a life of intense prayer combined with the
      occasional street preaching and spiritual direction.
    - There were both monastic hermits and individual lay hermits
      on the margins of Church and society.

- As well as hermits there emerged `urban lay communities` of
  evangelical inspiration who sought to re-live the apostolic
  community based on the word of God, frugality, equality and
  charity.
    - The communities were implicitly critical of the Church
      which they saw as compromised by the world. Some of them
      rejected the teachings of the Church, especially in regard
      to the sacraments.
        - e.g. how could an unspiritual priest mediate the truth
          and holiness of God?
    - The Church discovered that using force against such people
      created martyrs whose fame helped spread their views. New
      times had brought a crisis which needed a new response.

- St Francis of Assisi was a hermit who left his place in the
  settled world to follow Christ and rebuild His Church by living
  as one of the poor in a community of brothers on the margins of
  society.

- St Dominic also left his place in settled society and Church to
  take up an itinerant life of poverty and devotion to Christ
  that he might more effectively preach the Gospel to those who
  had been scandalised by the power and wealth of the Church.

- From Innocent III onwards, the popes used the new mendicant
  friars as they were useful to the Church in its new situation.

- The papacy actively intervened in reorganising groups of
  hermits, first of all in Tuscany and then throughout Italy
  making them Augustinian or Servite friars following the `Rule
  of St Augustine`.

- A similar process took place contemporaneously for the
  Carmelites. The hermit life was at the heart of the emergence
  of the dynamic new lifestyle of the mendicant friars.
    - The apostolic life of the friar was rooted in a life of
      fraternity and prayer at the margins of society.
    - In a short time the friars would move from the margins of
      society into a settled life in the towns.

- The original community on Mount Carmel for whom Albert wrote
  a `formula vitae` between 1206 and 1214 was changed greatly by
  migration to Europe and adaptation to life there.
    - From being a single community of hermits in Palestine they
      became a European order of mendicant clergy.

- In 1247 Pope Innocent IV approved, mitigated and modified
  Albert’s `formula vitae` turning it into their Rule and so
  began their way of life as an Order of mendicant friars.
    - This version of the Rule was later referred to as the
      'primitive rule'.
        - It was papal policy to co-opt the energy of hermit
          groups who were dedicated to the reform of the church
          so that would be useful to the church.
    - Mendicant friars were part of the new and growing cities of
      Europe whose citizens valued education and democracy. They
      were close to the people, particularly the urban poor,
      sharing their values, their new way of life at the service
      of a new evangelisation.

- In their new situation they changed their identity.
    - Their survival was precarious as mendicant orders founded
      after the `Fourth Lateran Council` (1215) were being
      `suppressed`.
    - The Second Council of Lyons in 1274 was a crisis of
      existence and identity for the Carmelites. Both the
      Carmelites and the Augustinians were almost suppressed.
      They were placed under restrictions which forbade new
      entrants however a final decision was left to a future
      pope.

- They retold their own history in ways that supported their
  right to exist. In this crisis the Carmelites claimed
  a distinctive Marian and Elijan spirit and mystique.
    - Not having a saintly founder like the Franciscans or
      Dominicans or even the Augustinians, the Rubrica Prima of
      the Constitutions of 1281 asserted the order went back to
      the prophet Elijah and the schools of the prophets.
    - The Carmelites also refused to give up the rite of the Holy
      Sepulchre. Of course, they also kept the Rule of Albert.

- The Carmelites became known as the `Brothers of St Mary of
  Mount Carmel`.
    - The habit was changed from the striped cloak to a white one
      in 1287 to reflect the claim that they were founded in
      honour of the Blessed Virgin.
    - The Constitutions of 1294 directed that “whenever anyone
      asks about our Order or its name, the name of the Blessed
      Virgin is to be given.”[^13]
    - The white mantle made it clear that the Carmelites were not
      lay-brothers but respectable clerics.

- In 1324 the Rubrica prima has this addition:
    - “After the Incarnation their successors (of the sons of
      Elijah the prophet) built a chapel there (on Mount Carmel)
      in honour of the Blessed Virgin Mary, and chose her title;
      therefore from that time they were by apostolic privilege
      called the Brothers of the Blessed Virgin Mary of Mount
      Carmel.”[^14]
    - The feast of Our Lady of Mount Carmel began to be
      celebrated in England towards the end of the 14th century
      and spread then throughout the order.
    - It was celebrated on 17 July (the date the Carmelites and
      Augustinians were spared by the Council of Lyons in 1274)
      until the feast spread to Europe when it was then
      anticipated on 16 July.

- Only in 1298 did Boniface VIII unconditionally approve the
  Augustinians and Carmelites.
    - In 1317 they were exempted from episcopal jurisdiction and
      in 1326 both orders received all the privileges and the
      exemptions of the Franciscans and Dominicans.
    - This crisis of survival was now over. However the first
      hundred years of Carmelite history had seen a profound
      structural and spiritual transformation.

- The Carmelites adapted successfully to this new situation by
  overwhelmingly becoming `ordained clergy` living in cities and
  began to flourish.
    - There was a cost for such successful adaptation. The need
      to educate friars in the new universities meant that
      privileges were granted to those with degrees.
    - Theology teachers often lived separately and in better
      accommodation than the rest of the community.
    - Privileges and exemptions were a sign and cause of
      a weakening of the spiritual life of the friars.
    - In a short time only those with `degrees` were eligible for
      certain offices and ministries.

- Result: The religious practices of poverty, prayer in common
  and fraternity were undermined.
    - Added to this were the series of crises, which marked the
      14th century. It witnessed:
        - the plague of the Black Death of 1348 which killed
          a third of the population of Europe,
        - the Great Schism of the Western church from 1378 to
          1417 during which time there were two or even three
          rival popes, and
        - the hundred-year-war between France and England.
            - Although it began in 1336 it didn't really end
              until the English left Calais in 1565.
    - These calamities undermined the life and morale of all
      religious communities including the Carmelites. Religious
      life fell into disrepute. Because of scandals, religious
      were often seen to be self-serving or hypocritical.

- In the Canterbury Tales of Geoffrey Chaucer (c1343-1400) the
  Summoner's Tale gives evidence of the contemporary disregard in
  which friars were held.
    - The summoner, an official in ecclesiastical courts, is
      replying with some anger to the story just told by a friar
      which disparaged summoners. Friars and summoners are
      obviously bitter rivals.
    - In the prologue, Chaucer reverses the usual story of the
      Cistercians being in heaven under the veil of the Virgin's
      protection. He has a friar descend to hell where he is
      pleased that no friars are to be seen as they are such
      goodly men.
    - However the angel accompanying him asks Satanas to lift his
      tail to reveal the true dwelling place of the friars.
      They swarm out before returning to the dwelling place.
    - It is a bawdy tale which reflects poorly on all friars who
      are seen to live comfortably in spite of their vow of
      poverty and public begging.
    - It reflects Chaucer's strong criticism of clerical
      corruption. Having once been the solution to the church’s
      problems friars were now part of the problem.

- The response of the religious to this situation was the
  `observance movement`. The motivation of this movement, which
  is found in all the mendicant orders (Dominican, Franciscan,
  Augustinian, Carmelite etc.), can be expressed as follows:
    - ‘Since I am a religious, at the very least, I will be what
      I _appear to be_. I will faithfully observe the rule and
      constitutions of my order.’
    - Members of the observant communities were known as
      reformed. They lived in communities marked by common prayer
      and strict poverty which preserved the unity and simplicity
      of the common life.
    - Often they chose rural locations and did not favour members
      taking degrees as this undermined the humility and equality
      of the members.
    - The various observant reforms were marked by a sense of
      religious fervour. They excised abuses, humbly living the
      common life in rural locations.
    - Yet there is a problematic side to this admirable movement
      in that it focuses on legal observance and a certain
      _externalism_.
    - It was thought that interior renewal followed on from
      keeping of the external, austere rules. Clearly there is
      some truth in this but there are other ways to bring about
      conversion.

- For the Carmelites the loss of fervour which began in the 14th
  century became endemic in the 15th century.[^15]
    - The 15th century reforming General John Soreth remarked:
        - ‘The rule and institutions of the Order lie everywhere
          neglected. Who keeps them, or who knows them?’[^16]
    - Soreth set about raising the order's standards of
      observance. The worst feature was that standards were
      lowered as legislation was modified to take account of the
      desire for easy-going ways.
    - The areas affected were prayer, silence, solitude, and the
      practice of poverty and community life.

- There were many priests in community whose sole role was to say
  a daily Mass for the intentions of donors.
    - Just as priesthood was not understood in terms of ministry
      or service, so priors and provincials were understood as
      prelates (latin: praelatus, one preferred) with attendant
      privileges.
    - Monastic stability came to be accepted over mendicant
      mobility.
    - Stability meant friars would settle into a comfortable
      relationship with the people of their town and not be
      available for transfer to another house to fill a common
      office of the provinces or the Order.
    - Resistance to any change which threatened privileges and
      exemptions was fierce. Those who lived this way of life
      were known as `conventuals`.[^17]

- Considering that for all its turmoil the 14th century was
  a time when Christian mysticism flourished in the Low
  Countries, Germany and England, it is remarkable that the
  Carmelites seem not to have produced any mystics or mystical
  writers.
    - As put by one of my O.Carm teachers, they went from being
      prophetic hermits on Mount Carmel to professional clergy in
      the cities of Europe, until finally some at least came to
      live as pensioners.

- On 15 February `1432` Pope Eugene IV, in the bull `Romani
  Pontificis`, `mitigated` the Carmelite Rule allowing the friars
  to eat meat three times a week and to walk about their
  cloisters and churches at suitable times instead of remaining
  in their cells.
    - Today this mitigation might appear to us to be a sensible
      adaptation to the situation of the order at the time, but
      in fact it became a highly charged symbol for those who
      wanted to reform the order.
    - It was seen as a betrayal of the Carmelite contemplative
      spirit. In 1440 the general chapter imposed it on all
      members of the order.
    - The text of the 'mitigated' and 'primitive' rule is
      identical.

- Pause for a moment to reflect on mitigation and reform.
    - Do you think that mitigation is always wrong or that, in
      certain circumstances, it might be wise?
    - Do you think that reform necessarily means turning the
      clock backwards or do you think it can also be a way of
      enabling the charism to adapt to new circumstances?

### Unit 2: 02 The Congregation of Mantua

- The spirit of reform in the early 15th century produced ‘a
  house of observance’ at `Le Selve` in 1413.
    - Jacobo di Alberto initiated the reform and was followed as
      prior by Blessed Angelus Mazzinghi in 1419.

- `Mantua` was another reformed house but we do not know how, when
  or by whom it was reformed.
    - An interesting light is shone on the culture of reform by
      the story of Thomas Connecte, a French charismatic reformer
      who was burnt at the stake as a heretic in Rome in 1434.
        - He had founded the Carmelite priory of `Geronde` in
          Switzerland as a house of observance in 1428.
    - His followers found refuge in the Carmelite priory in
      Mantua.

- On 3 September `1442` Pope Eugene IV issued the bull, Forma
  laudabilis, which united:
    - Le Selve,
    - Mantua and
    - Geronde
        - as a separate congregation which became known as the
          Mantuan Congregation.

- The spirit of the Mantuan Congregation was that of an observant
  reform seeking to return to the original spirit of the Order,
  free from all corruption.
    - Blessed Baptist of Mantua wrote that it ‘strives to pattern
      its life and customs after the ancient fathers’.[^18]
    - Silence and cloister were observed; the friars were not
      permitted to wander outside the monastery nor were laity to
      enter it.
    - Money was not kept privately, the mitigation regarding
      abstinence was rejected and they wore a habit of rough
      undyed wool. The desire for autonomy and separation from
      the unreformed is a mark of the observant movement.

- Over time, the observance of the Mantuan congregation became
  more relaxed and members of the Ancient Observance claimed they
  were no more observant than they were.
    - It survived for a surprisingly long time, only being
      suppressed as a congregation and united to the order by
      Pius VI in the bull Exigit Apostolci on 21 March, `1783`.
    - In its time and at its best, it produced holy men such as:
        - the humanist Baptist of Mantua and
        - Angelus of Mazzinghi.


### Unit 2: 03 The Reform of Albi

- This reform was initiated by the bishop of Albi, Louis of
  Amboise, who recruited friars from the `Mantuan` Congregation
  and `students from Paris` to begin his new movement.
- In 1499, twenty-two candidates received the habit in the
  episcopal palace.
- In 1502 the house in Albi and the monastery of Melun which
  associated with it were formed into the `Congregation of Albi`.
    - Louis De Lire was the first leader of this Congregation.
- In 1532 at the Carmelite General Chapter, the prior general
  Nicholas Audet officially recognised the Albi Congregation.
- Albi then helped renew the Order in France by lifting the
  standard of observance in the house of studies in Paris and by
  the holy men who were its members.


### Unit 2: 04 Monte Oliveto

- Monte Oliveto was not so much an attempt to reform the order as
  an attempt to live the `primitive Rule` as it was originally
  written for hermits.
    - The founder was `Hugh Marengo` who had been a member of the
      Mantuan Congregation but having been expelled he obtained
      papal authorisation for the new foundation of which he was
      the vicar apostolic.
    - Monte Oliveto survived as a hermitage and was received into
      the Carmelite order in 1565. In time became a more ordinary
      priory and then a parish until suppressed in 1803.


### Unit 2: 05 Blessed John Soreth

- With the Generalate of John Soreth, reform found a home at the
  centre of the Order.
    - Earlier reforms had begun at the grassroots level, but
      reforms were now imposed. Abuses were suppressed and
      offenders punished.
    - The model for this reform was the regular observance in
      communities of shared life and prayer. Soreth worked
      tirelessly through pastoral visitations, which were the
      most effective means of achieving reform.
    - Not only did he initiate reforms in houses and provinces,
      he also held the `unreformed conventual friars` accountable
      for keeping their laws and regulations.

- Most significantly Soreth oversaw and promoted the coming into
  being of the first `Carmelite nuns` and the `Carmelite Third
  Order` in 1452.
    - This seems to have been a new, expansive innovation, rather
      than a reforming act per se.
    - It's worth remarking that only after 250 years from when
      the first Carmelites came to live in the Wadi-es-Siah were
      women finally able to be Carmelite religious, rather than
      sisters in a `confraternity`.
    - It could be argued that most of the creative spirit of the
      order since 1452 has been associated with women Carmelites.
      Certainly the Carmelite story since would be unrecognisable
      without women.

- Soreth was born in `1395` near Caen in Normandy which was then
  under English rule.
    - He entered the order there and studied at the University of
      Paris where he received a doctorate in `1438`.
    - In 1440 he was elected provincial of Francia and in 1451
      elected Carmelite prior general. He actively encouraged the
      spontaneous forces of reform already at work in the order.
    - He worked first in Lower Germany and then Italy and in
      France. Soreth did not visit England or Ireland and there's
      no information on the state of the order in those
      countries.

- The visitation of a general of the order was a formal affair.
    - The general was a prelate, who in Soreth’s case travelled
      humbly enough on a mule, but he did not fraternise with the
      local community, nor eat or worship with them.
    - He kept himself separate, presumably to resist being unduly
      influenced in his judgements.
    - He began the visitation by reading a lengthy charge to the
      community setting out for some hours what he expected. He
      then had the community accounts audited while he began to
      interview each religious.

- In `1456` statutes drawn up for reformed monasteries of the
  order were approved at the general chapter.
    - Members renounced their temporal goods, privileges and
      exemptions. They ate at the common table and outside masses
      were not generally accepted.
    - In `1462` Soreth promulgated his revision of the
      Constitutions of the Order (at the Brussels general
      chapter); these were approved again in 1469 and printed in
      1499.
    - Smet summarises his legacy as:
        - “Practically single-handed and through sheer force of
          personality he lifted the Order from the slough of
          indifference and vice to something approaching
          dedicated living.”[^19]
        - To eliminate abuses he had to be tough. When faced with
          recalcitrant opposition he would impose penalties.
          Friars were obliged to renounce benefices and resume
          community life or give up the habit.
        - Such was the opposition to his reforms in some places
          that in 1459 he obtained from Pope Pius II powers to
          appeal to local ordinaries to have miscreants
          imprisoned and if necessary tortured.”[^20]

- On 10 May 1452 he received the beguines of “Ten Elsen”,
  Guelders, into the Order and thus `Carmelite nuns` came into
  being.
    - Women had previously been affiliated with the Order but not
      in a way that was recognised by the church as being nuns.
    - A bull, Cum Nulla was obtained from Nicholas V, which was
      dated 7 October, 1452 which granted to the prior general
      and provincials of the Carmelite Order the same privileges
      as the Dominicans and Augustinians in regard to the
      reception, way of life, admission and protection of
      religious virgins, widows, beguines and mantellates who
      singly, or in groups (conventus), were living or in the
      future would present themselves under the protection and
      habit of the Carmelite Order.[^21]

- Smet’s comments on why Soreth worked for the founding of
  Carmelite nuns and the impact this made on the form of their
  life bears reflection:
    - For John Soreth, the institution of the sisters was part of
      his programme for the renewal of the Order.
    - This explains the cloistered nature of their life, for
      a return to a more retired and recollected existence was
      one of the features of his observance for the friars.
    - The convents of friars founded during Soreth’s time were
      all of the observance; it was inevitable that the
      institution of the sisters took the form it did.
    - However in an age of spiritual decline, maintaining the
      original spirit of the new institution was a serious
      one. [^22]

- It appears Soreth wanted the nuns to model for the friars what
  the enclosed observant life was for Carmelites.

- It might be noted that the nuns associated with the mendicant
  orders, Poor Clares, Dominican nuns etc., were all enclosed
  contemplatives with more strict enclosure than the older
  monastic orders.
    - The emphasis on strict, papal enclosure did not come from
      the Carmelite tradition itself.
    - It came from the decree Periculoso of Pope Boniface VIII in
      `1298` which made female enclosure much stricter than that
      of monks:
        - Wishing to provide for the dangerous and abominable
          situation of certain nuns, who, casting off the reins
          of respectability and impudently abandoning nunnish
          modesty and the natural bashfulness of their sex […] we
          do firmly decree […] that nuns collectively and
          individually, both at present and in future, of
          whatsoever community or order, in whatever part of the
          world they may be, ought henceforth to remain
          perpetually cloistered in their monasteries […] so that
          [the nuns] be able to serve God more freely, wholly
          separated from the public and worldly gaze and,
          occasions for lasciviousness having been removed, may
          most diligently safeguard their hearts and bodies in
          complete chastity.[^23]

- There is clearly an idealisation of women in this text which
  governs expectations of the behaviour of nuns.
    - It is worth mentioning that it was the disedifying conduct
      of some nuns that brought about this stricter legislation
      on all nuns.
    - It was to be an ideal more honoured than strictly observed
      until the Council of Trent when cloisters became vital to
      the church's understanding of women's religious life and
      provided stiff penalties for any violation.

- Blessed John Soreth died at Angers on 25 July `1471`, with
  a reputation for holiness.
    - However, the Carmelite Order on the whole did not continue
      on or even grow in a reformed way. This seems to be par for
      the course when observant reform was imposed from above.
    - Today Soreth is honoured for his reforms, but for all his
      energy they do not seem to have had lasting impact.
    - Carmelites accommodated themselves to the humanist culture
      of the 15th century and in good part the order lost its
      spirit, direction and vitality.

- Let's reflect on the reform movement of St John Soreth.
    - What, in your view, are the positive elements of his
      reforms and also the less than positive elements?
    - In what ways might he be regarded as a model of reform and
      in what ways might his approach be less than ideal in
      present day circumstances?
