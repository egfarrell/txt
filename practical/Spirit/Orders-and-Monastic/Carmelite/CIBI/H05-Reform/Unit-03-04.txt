### Nicholas Audet
- Following the great reformer Blessed John Soreth (d. 1471), the
  Carmelites didn't have vigorous leadership until Nicholas Audet
  (1481-1562) became prior general in 1524.

- So with Audet we enter into the era of Carmelite reform and
  renewal in the 16th century. Audet was born on Cyprus in 1481.
  While his family was lesser French nobility they spoke
  a version of Greek. He joined the Carmelites at Famagusta and
  studied first at Nicosia and then at Padua and Parma.
    - He became provincial of the Holy Land province in 1514.
    - In 1523 he was nominated vicar general of the order by Pope
      Adrian VI on the death of the prior general Bernardine
      Landucci.
- On assuming the office of `vicar general` Audet was shocked at
  the state of the Order.
    - In a programme of action, the `Isagogicon` (introductory
      text in theology) written when he was still vicar general
      and published late in 1523. He wrote:
        - We learned of what sordid conduct many of our brethren
          are guilty and what great threat hangs over the good
          because of their bad example, unless all of us together
          quickly come to our senses and reform our conduct.[^1]
    - Such calls for reform were common in the 15th century but
      Audet stands out because he was not content with rhetoric
      but intended to take practical action.
    - Indeed he realistically said ‘we have not undertaken to
      reform the order but to prepare it for reform.’[^2]
    - His call is that of a return to the `observance` of the
      constitutions, a removal of abuses and a lifting of overall
      standards.
    - He not only sent out his call, he prepared to set out
      himself in order to visit and reform and so ensure the
      constitutions were observed.

- Audet was elected the Carmelite prior general at the general
  chapter in Venice in May 1524. His reform programme was
  endorsed by the chapter whose decrees were collected in
  a document referred to as `Caput Unicum`.
    - The lines of his reform were an observant return to the
      common life as prescribed by the constitutions of the
      order.
    - The constitutions of John Soreth were revised and made
      stricter in regard to exemptions from the common table for
      the teachers of theology and in similar matters.
    - A particular concern of the reform as well as observance
      was the promotion of studies in the order and the worthy
      celebration of the traditional Carmelite liturgy.
    - These revised constitutions, the `Isagogicon` and the
      `Caput Unicam` along with a list of the privileges of the
      order were printed and published so ignorance was no longer
      an excuse. Audet’s reform came to Spain in 1530.

- Audet sought to incorporate the reform congregations of Albi
  and Mantua into his programme of reform for the order. He
  succeeded in France with the Congregation of Albi but in Italy
  his dealings with the Mantua congregation degenerated into
  a long running quarrel (1532-1538).
    - The Mantuan congregation was as much in need of reform as
      the rest of the order but was protected by a wall of
      privileges which hampered Audet. Unlike some his
      predecessors, he didn't turn a blind eye to the situation.

- For the Mantuans their autonomy was non-negotiable.
    - They were nervous about his desire to intervene in the life
      and Smet describes their response as ‘acute panic.’[^3]
    - What resulted was a lengthy duel played with papal bulls
      and a case before the Roman Rota.
    - The unity of the order was severely compromised in a way
      that undermined the project of reform of the whole order.
      The privileges of the Mantuans (e.g. private ownership)
      were used as a precedent to undermine Audet’s reform.

- Audet lost the legal battle and from then on his energy for
  reform was spent. He had lost confidence in papal legal
  procedures. He didn't leave Italy thereafter except to attend
  the Council of Trent.
    - In the thirty years after 1532 he only called two general
      chapters one in 1539 and also in 1548.[^4]
    - The autonomous status of the Mantuan congregation within
      the order was understandably judged an anomaly by Audet and
      his successor as prior general John Baptist Rossi.
        - They had no desire for any other autonomous
          congregations within the Carmelite order.

- At the same time as Audet was having difficulties with internal
  reform, external events such as the Protestant Reformation,
  were having a severe impact as well.
    - Sometime after 1550 an account of the state of the order
      was drawn up. It revealed that out of 30 provinces (with
      120 priories), six had been destroyed by the Lutherans.
    - The best provinces were found outside Italy with houses of
      study, students and monasteries of nuns.
    - The provinces of Lower Germany, Francia, Narbonne, Castile,
      and Portugal `belonged to the observance`.
    - The rest were reformed or disposed to reform. The degree of
      observance varied from place to place but each province had
      at least some reformed or observant houses.[^5]
    - Audet died in Rome in December 1562.

- Pause for a moment to reflect on the reforms of Audet.
    - In what ways might his approach be relevant today and in
      what ways might a very different approach be needed, in
      your view?

### Reform in Castile

- We now begin to focus on the Carmelites in Spain.

- The first Carmelite foundation in Spain was made in `1277` at
  Sangüesa. Valencia followed in `1281`.
    - By the time of Audet there were provinces in Catalonia,
      Aragon and Castile.[^6]
    - These Spanish provinces were isolated from the centre of
      the Order and in need of reform.

- The Spanish monarchs, Ferdinand and Isabella, were also deeply
  concerned with reforming religious life. Alongside reform
  initiatives led by the prior general in Rome, a separate
  process of reform was initiated within Spain itself under royal
  sponsorship.

- At the beginning of the 16th century (1502-1503) the greatest
  conflict was in Castile where Cardinal Ximénes de Cisnerso,
  appointed by Queen Isabella. was the visitator and reformer of
  the houses of the four mendicant orders,
    - He intervened with a firm hand to bring about reform.
    - The crucial point was the priory of Toledo which had
      problems not just with relaxed observance but also
      scandalous sexual misconduct.
        - Those who didn't want the observance were forcibly
          evicted. However, the friars resisted by appealing to
          Rome and surprisingly to us were successful.[^7]

- These appeals to the pope undermined the royal campaign for
  mendicant reform and renewal, just as it had undermined Audet’s
  desire to re-incorporate the Mantuan congregation.
    - If the papacy was not reformed and did not support reform
      how was the church to be reformed?
    - For reform minded Spaniards this lead to a lack of trust,
      even disdain, for the Roman curia and Italian prelates.
    - This attitude will affect later Roman attempts at reform,
      even the Tridentine reforms, in Spain.[^8]
    - This antipathy will have a number of consequences in our
      story.

- An important development for the order in Spain was the
  separation of the houses in Andalusia from the Castile province
  in 1500.
    - The creation of the new province resolved a crisis of ill
      feeling and distrust that had been brewing between the
      Castilians and Andalusians.
    - The new province was to have a distinctive spirit and a set
      of on-going problems which were to cast a long shadow
      beyond Andalusia.
        - It grew rapidly in numbers but its religious observance
          left much to be desired.
    - The Castile province was reduced to only six houses. After
      `1530` it was to be dramatically reduced in the number of
      religious.


- Charles V acted on the reform of the religious orders of Spain
  which visitation was carried out from 1531-1532.
    - He had papal authority for this work, and for him the
      choice was clear: REFORM or SUPPRESSION.
    - Charles was also the Holy Roman Emperor from 1519. As such
      he came face to face with Martin Luther and the Protestant
      Reformation which began in 1517.
    - As a layman he urged the popes to respond to the challenge
      by calling a council for the reform of the Church.
        - In this he was frustrated by popes who were more
          Renaissance figures embroiled in Italian politics than
          spiritual leaders until the Council began at Trent,
          geographically in Italy but part of the Empire and so
          in German lands, at Charles insistence, in 1543.

- Meanwhile from 1530 Audet appointed his own visitators for the
  Carmelites of Castile, IN ADDITION to the reform programme of
  Charles V.
    - The Carmelite visitation in Castile could from one point of
      view be considered a resounding success. The observance was
      eventually established in the entire province, after all
      the usual abuses had been dealt with.

- The reality was that many of the friars could not face living
  the rigour of the observance. The result was a mass exodus;
  according to Audet the greater part of the friars left.[^9]
    - This exodus severely weakened the province of Castile.
      Although they slowly rebuilt their numbers to around 100 at
      the time of St Teresa, and had also made three new
      foundations.
    - As a result not many of the friars were free to pursue
      further studies. This situation was to have consequences.
      St Teresa remarks on this situation in `Foundations` 2.5:
        - “Seeing how few friars were in this province, made me
          wonder whether or not they were going to die out,
          I prayed to the Lord over the matter very much, and
          wrote to Father General.”

- The situation in the rest of Spain seems to have been that
  reform was undertaken EXCEPT in the province of Andalusia where
  the friars creatively and successfully resisted reform.

- Pause for a moment to reflect on the reform in Castile. Do you
  think a situation like Castile could happen today and, if so,
  what form would it take?
    - Do you think that a situation such as that in Castile
      requires a different kind of approach than is needed when
      the need for reform is less evident?

### The Monastery of the Incarnation, Ávila

- The beginnings of the Spanish Carmelite monasteries of nuns and
  beaterios of pious lay women who came to support one another by
  living in community date from the middle 15th century after Cum
  Nulla.
    - Beaterios became popular in the highly charged religious
      atmosphere of 15th century Spain. Holy women, many of whom
      took private vows of chastity and wore a religious habit of
      some kind, lived alone or in groups and engaged in prayer,
      penance and works of charity _without formally becoming
      religious_.

- In 1477 a project to begin a `house of prayer` and recollection
  in Ávila was initiated by Doña Elvira González de Medina.
    - She sought to have the beatas, herself, her daughter and
      two friends, live under the title of the Blessed Virgin
      either under the Rule of Carmel or that of St Dominic.
    - Doña Elvira had been the partner of Don Nuño González del
      Aguila, a cathedral canon and archdeacon of Ávila, to whom
      she bore four children.[^10]
    - Such clerical concubinage was not that scandalous at the
      time. However Doña Elvira apparently repented and now she
      sought to dedicate herself to prayer and penance.


- The Beaterio of the Incarnation was situated in the family
  palace of Don Nuño within the walls of Ávila next to the San
  Vicente gate which he had sold shortly before his death in 1467
  to his ‘widow’. Their sons contested this sale in court which
  has given us a paper trail to glimpse these aspects of
  aristocratic clerical life of the time.
    - Doña Elvira was the ‘madre, patrona y administradora’
      (mother, patron and administrator) of the new community and
      she received the habit from the Carmelite provincial in
      1479.
    - Her daughter Catalina del Aguila succeeded her mother in
      this role on her death in 1486. The community moved to
      another site, a nearby chapel which was formerly
      a synagogue, in 1485.

- On the death of Doña Catalina in 1495 the community elected
  Doña Beatriz Guiera as superior.
    - She had entered the community at the age of 14 but had left
      it to live in the Dominican monastery of Alba only
      returning on the death of Doña Catalina.
    - Doña Beatriz proceeded to refound the community according
      to the regular life of nuns which she had learned from her
      years with the Dominicans.
    - In April `1515` the nuns of the Incarnation finally moved
      their monastery to the site of the old Jewish cemetery
      outside the walls of Ávila where it is to this day.

- This transfer, while it brought the benefits of space and air,
  exhausted the community’s economic resources.
    - In a response to this situation the community accepted many
      aspirants who came with their dowries and so the community
      grew greatly in numbers.
    - It was also a home to many daughters of the nobility who
      could not marry due to dowry inflation.
    - So the Incarnation was a mixed community of religious and
      seculars. Some religious had apartments (with servants or
      even a slave) while others had to live in dormitories and
      dine in a common refectory where food was not in abundant
      supply.
    - There was the ever pressing need to obtain donations from
      wealthy friends, relatives and benefactors to support the
      life of the community.

- The inopportune visits of Carmelite friars to their favourites
  in the community were another problem.
    - In 1521 Doña Beatriz petitioned the prior general to exempt
      the Incarnation from visitation by the provincial of
      Castile and to give them freedom to choose their own
      confessors.
    - Audet confirmed these means once more in 1526 which
      specifically forbade the provincial or any of the friars to
      visit the Incarnation under the gravest penalties.[^11]
    - It was to this community, with these very particular
      characteristics, that in November 1535 Doña Teresa de
      Ahumada y Cepeda entered.

- Pause for a moment to reflect on the situation in the
  Incarnation.
    - If a Religious community were to drift into a similar
      situation, how do you think that might manifest itself?
    - For you personally, what would have been the positive and
      negative aspects of living in a situation such as that
      found in the Incarnation?


### John Baptist Rossi

- John Baptist Rossi was born in Ravenna in 1507. Rossi was known
  in Spain as `Rubeo` and so he is called in the works of St
  Teresa.
    - After joining the Carmelites in his native city he studied
      theology at Padua and received his doctorate in 1540.
    - In 1546 he was procurator general of the order and
      a professor at the Sapienza University in Rome. After the
      death of Audet, Pius IV named Rossi vicar general of the
      order on 16 December 1562. 
    - He began his reform work immediately. The following January
      he obtain faculties from the Holy See ‘to visit, reform and
      correct’ the houses of the order.[^12] 
    - The general chapter opened in Rome on 21 May, 1564, and
      elected Rossi prior general the first day. 
    - The chapter issued a number of decrees concerning the
      divine cult, the government and administration of the
      houses and reform. Thus began a new era that of the
      Catholic Counter Reformation within the Carmelite
      order.[^13] 

- Rossi was a man of learning and integrity who sought to bring
  the order to a level of authenticity in its life. Religious
  were to observe the obligations of the religious life within
  the framework they were accustomed to. 
    - He sought to remove abuses, reorganise the regular life and
      remove anything that would offend public decency. 
    - He did not seek to go back to a primitive observance of the
      rule or any idealised imitation of the first hermits on
      Mount Carmel. 
    - Instead Rossi sought to bring the life of the friars into
      line with what the Church expected in the reforms coming
      from the Council of Trent. 
    - He left the accustomed system of privileges intact so that
      while he fought to have the friars live up to their
      personal and public commitments, according to the
      Tridentine reform decrees, the Carmelites were not
      seriously considered as an order to be either reformed or
      observant.[^14] 

- In Spain the movement towards reform had already received
  a stimulus from the highest authorities. 
    - Since his accession to the throne in 1556 Philip II had
      sought to reform the religious orders in his realm. 
    - Trent had entrusted this task to the superiors of the
      orders but Philip judged that on past experience this had
      not had satisfactory results. 
    - After death of Audet he promoted a scheme whereby the
      Carmelites in Spain would be governed by a vicar general in
      a way analogous to that of the reform congregations of
      Mantua and Albi. 
    - Rossi and the chapter were opposed to this proposal which
      would undermine once more the unity of the order. Rossi got
      the agreement of the King by agreeing to the new structure
      only if Rossi himself did not begin a visitation in Spain
      within two years.

- Rossi just made the deadline he had set. Affairs in Italy kept
  him busy. 
    - Among these was the incorporation of the hermitage of Monte
      Oliveto and its 20 hermits into the order in 1565. 
    - Rossi visited the community in April 1566 on his way to
      Spain. He arrived in Madrid on 10 June, 1566 where he
      received the approval of Philip II to make the visitation.
    - This approval was necessary as even papal bulls and the
      decrees of Trent could not take effect in Spain without the
      placet of the monarch. 
    - The order in Spain had four provinces: 
        - Catalonia, 
        - Aragon,
        - Castile and 
        - Andalusia 
        - with forty eight houses and about 550 religious.[^15]  

- Rossi began his visitation with the hardest nut to crack, the
  province of Andalusia.
    - Three brothers, Casper, Baltasar and Melchior Nieto whose
      splendid names ironically recalled the Magi of Matthew’s
      Gospel covered complicated characters who were at the heart
      of the problem. 
    - Rossi later declared, “When we came to Andalusia we
      strongly rebuked these three brothers for their petulant
      and libidinous behaviour by which they had each befouled
      themselves.”[^16] 
    - Their behaviour more fitted gangsters than religious. 
    - They abused their offices, living luxuriously while others
      lacked necessities; they were given to favouritism; used
      violence or its threat to get their way; and at least one
      was suspected of sexual misconduct.

- The provincial chapter began on 22 September 1566. 
    - Rossi presented a slate of candidates for provincial which
      excluded the Nieto brothers and their supporters. 
    - Rossi himself, as was his custom when corporal punishment
      was to be administered as part of the reform process,
      administered the discipline on the naked shoulders of the
      shackled and disgraced Baltasar.[^17] 
    - While one friar was condemned to the galleys for living
      with a prostitute for more than six years Rossi considered
      he had acted with restraint in the face of the grave
      situation. 
    - He sought to remove the causes of dissent without giving
      reason for complaint due to excessive punishment. At the
      end of the chapter Rossi had published his `Institutiones
      et Ordinationes` to govern the future reform of the
      Province. 
    - His reforming leadership should have pointed the way to
      a healthier future.

- After the visitation of Andalusia, Rossi visited the province
  of Portugal and then began his visit to Castile. 
    - In Salamanca, he visitated the college of San Andres, where
      among the small number of students was the future St John
      of the Cross, then a third year arts student. 
    - While in Ávila he visitated the convent of the Incarnation
      in February `1567` and found conditions there difficult.
    - There were 180 nuns along with various seculars in the
      community. The divisions between the rich and poor nuns
      meant there was a fundamental inequality in the community.
    - Nuns asked for dispensations either to keep their servants
      or slaves, or from the common refectory and/or from the
      celebration of the office in choir. 
    - The hard work of trying to survive meant there was little
      time for prayer and recollection.

- At the conclusion of the visitation of Castile, Rossi found
  that the friars of Castile were, on the whole, ‘very
  good’.[^18]  
    - The province had a modest enough profile but it had been
      slowly building itself both up in numbers and spiritually.
    - There was none of the chaos associated with the order in
      Andalusia. 
    - This contemporary evaluation of the Castilian province is
      worth remembering when we come to the work of foundation
      undertaken by St Teresa.

- The visitation for which Rossi is most remembered was at the
  convent of San José in Ávila where he met the founder, Madre
  Teresa de Jesús between 16 and 18 February `1567`.
    - Teresa gives us a lively account of her meeting with Rossi,
      whom the Spaniards called Rubeo, in `Foundations chapter 2`.
    - Teresa had founded San José in 1562 outside the
      jurisdiction of the order and she was fearful the general
      would not be pleased and would order her back to the
      Incarnation. 
    - She was, as ever, candid and winning with him. He reassured
      and consoled her much. Teresa assures us, ‘He rejoiced to
      see our manner of life, a portrait, although an imperfect
      one, of the beginnings of our order.’[^19] 

- She presents it as his desire that this beginning go forward,
  although it was clearly her desire after the catalyst of her
  meeting Alonso Maldonado. 
    - He therefore presented Teresa with extensive letters patent
      so that more such monasteries could be founded ‘along with
      censures preventing any provincial from restraining
      me.’[^20] 
    - Some days after the meeting, Teresa reflects how necessary
      it would be if there were to be more monasteries, to have
      friars who would also observe the same ‘primitive’ rule as
      the nuns of San José and any new convents. 
    - So she writes asking Rossi for these permissions. From
      Valencia, Rossi sent the documents granting her requests.

- Teresa certainly was grateful to Rossi and the feeling was
  mutual. In 1569 he wrote to the Discalced Carmelites of Medina
  del Campo about Teresa: 
    - “I give infinite thanks to the divine Majesty for the great
      favour given to this religion by the diligence and goodness
      of our reverend mother Teresa de Jesús. She has brought
      more benefit to the order than all the friars of Spain. May
      God give her many years of life!”[^21] 

- Pause for a moment to reflect on Rossi's attitude to reform.
  What, in your view, would be his strengths and his weakness in
  this regard?


### 'Reform of the General' v 'Reform of the King'

- In May 1567, just as his visitations seemed to be going
  successfully, Rossi went to Madrid to appear before the court.
    - With the support of influential nobles the friars of
      Andalusia who had lost out at the visitation and chapter
      appealed to the king in a memorial against Rossi and his
      activities as apostolic visitator. 
    - The king sent the matter to the judgement of the Royal
      Council presided over by Cardinal Espinosa.

- The reform decrees of Rossi were found to be admirable but the
  Council was prejudiced against an Italian general. They
  insisted on seeing the original of the brief which gave him
  apostolic authority for the visitation. 
    - They insisted the excommunications and other punishments be
      revoked and those who had been dismissed were reinstated
      while a new investigation took place. 
    - Through intrigue the rebels had won an important victory.
      They managed to frustrate what would have been
      a significant reform of their province. 
    - The reform of the king, far stricter in its intentions, was
      used to prevent the moderate reform of the prior general
      and to allow dubious practices to remain in place.

- To consolidate the shift in power, a papal brief Superioribus
  mensibus which put the reform of the religious of Spain into
  the hands of the king had been despatched from Rome on 16 April
  1567. 
    - The machinations of the Andalusian friars were responsible
      for the inclusion of the Carmelites among the orders to
      whom Superioribus mensibus applied.

- Reform of the Carmelites in Spain was thus taken out of Rossi’s
  hands as general of the order and the responsibility placed
  into the hands of the king and his officials. 
    - The conflict of jurisdictions and the need for reform in
      Andalusia was to create more mischief and misunderstanding
      in the years to come for the apostolic visitators appointed
      by the king and the nuncio. Teresa and the Discalced
      Carmelites were also to be drawn into this quagmire.

- Pause for a moment to reflect on the advantages and
  disadvantages of Reform from within and Reform imposed from
  outside. 
    - What, if any, are the advantages of having Religious Life
      reformed by the civic leaders of the country? 
    - What, if any, are the disadvantages of having Religious
      Life reformed by its own Religious leaders?
