# Units 7
    - Expanding vision
    - Teresa’s foundations of nuns and friars

### Unit 7: An expanding vision

- Teresa founded San José on 24 August 1562.
    - She lived there happily as prioress of the small new
      community from January 1563 (Foundations, 1.1).
    - At first she and the sisters of the community slowly
      experimented on how to order their way of life.
    - Teresa adapted Constitutions for the community.
    - In the spring of 1565 she completed the second redaction of
      The Book of her Life.
        - This had begun as an account of her conscience and her
          spiritual experience so that her confessors could judge
          the state of her soul.
    - Teresa expanded it as a treatise on interior prayer
      initially for her confessors and judges.
        - She wrote to persuade them that interior prayer was an
          efficacious means of advance in virtue and holiness.
        - It was to be encouraged not discouraged. While it has
          autobiographical content it is not really an
          autobiography as we know them today.

- Teresa was proud of what she termed ‘my book’ and was sure it
  contained material which would help anyone who desired to pray.
    - However Domingo Bañez forbade the sisters in San José from
      reading it. He did not share Teresa’s trust in women.
    - So she determined then to write another book, ‘my little
      book’, for her nuns.
    - In 1565 or 1566 she finished the first draft of the 'Way of
      Perfection' written for her sisters in San José.
    - In an analogous way to which Post-Vatican II Constitutions
      have a doctrinal and a normative part, the Way of Perfection
      is the spiritual part of her Constitutions.

- 'The Way of Perfection' is an instruction on prayer in which
  before she writes on prayer she enlarges on why she founded San
  José and consequently how the sisters were to live together in
  community in light of their mission in the Church.
    - As she come to the end of the book she urges the sisters to
      think rightly about God who ‘doesn’t look at trifles as
      much as you think’ (Way, 41.8).
    - She urges the sisters not to be tense or constrained which
      leads to scruples (41.5) but ‘to be affable and
      understanding so that everyone you talk to will love your
      conversation and desire your manner of living and acting,
      and _not be frightened or intimidated by virtue_. This is
      very important for religious, the holier they are the _more
      sociable_ they are with their sisters.’ (41.7).
    - It strikes me that this is a remarkable example of
      Christian Humanist spirituality.

- The community is to be small where all share the same solitude,
  simplicity and poverty (Way, 2).
    - It is to be a house of joy where all are friends and mutual
      love and support are the norm (Way, 4.7).
    - In her account of why she made the foundation she portrays
      it as a spiritual response to the crisis of the Reformation
      (Way, 1. 2,5; 3.1-6.).
    - Teresa wanted her nuns to pray for the defenders of the
      church, the theologians and preachers, that they might have
      a _real experience of God_, that they might be not just
      learned but also truly spiritual.
    - She saw the Reformation as a spiritual challenge which
      required a spiritual response. Her own motivation was
      compassion for those who would lose their souls through
      their crimes against the faith and the Church.

- The heart of the Teresian experience is not a theory or
  philosophy but something much more open ended-relationships.
    - She is usually understood as an affective mystic but to
      call her a _relational one_ seems more apt.
    - For her prayer was a relationship of intimate friendship
      with Christ which grew over time.
    - For her community life also was based on friendship.
    - These friendships were not merely those who shared common
      interests but were virtuous. The virtuous friendship was
      one which was not exclusive, all were to be included, and
      all were to be loved.
    - The sisters were not to be rivals but were to seek the will
      of God and to desire what God desired for them and the
      world.

- In the crisis of her time, her communities were models of a new
  way of being church:
    - where the spiritual was given priority and people were free
      to grow in evangelical friendship of mutuality.
    - This way of being was also a model for a new way of being
      in society:
        - where all are equal and all are helped and loved.

- In the summer of 1566 Teresa was to have her horizons expanded
  once more.
    - A charismatic Franciscan, Alonso Maldonado, who had been
      a missionary in Mexico (1551-1561) visited Teresa San José.
    - He told her stories of the many millions of souls there and
      throughout Latin America who had not been baptised and who
      would therefore be lost to hell.
    - Teresa was overwhelmed at the thought of the enormity of
      this tragedy. She pleaded with the Lord to give her the
      means to do something to win some souls to his service
      (Foundation, 1.7).
    - She had great desires to make more foundations. The Book of
      the Foundations is her fascinating account of this work.

- The Lord told her to be patient:
    - “Wait a little daughter, and you will see great things.”
      (Foundations, 1.8)
    - Teresa’s motive is compassion for those who have not heard
      the Gospel, her spirit is apostolic and missionary.
    - Her heart goes out to all the people of the world she is
      intent on making a difference.
    - In the aphorism of Peter Drucker, the management guru,
      “everything degenerates into work”.
    - Teresa’s life will now be devoted to the labour of bringing
      new communities to birth and guiding and nurturing their
      early years.

- Pause for a moment to reflect on the broader significance of
  the transformation that took place in St Teresa.
    - Would you agree that what happened to her was significant
      for many people during her lifetime and that it continues
      to be significant?
    - Do you think that everybody's conversion journey has
      a similar, broader significance?

### Unit 7: Teresa and the General (Rossi)

- The catalyst for transforming Teresa’s hopes into reality was
  the visit of the `Carmelite General`, John Baptist Rossi on his
  visitation to Spain in 1566-7.
    - Teresa looked forward to his visitation with trepidation as
      she had founded San José under the jurisdiction of the
      bishop of Ávila rather than of the Order.
    - She feared Rossi would take offence at this and would send
      her back to the Incarnation.
    - Teresa explained herself to him and how the foundation came
      about as it did.
    - He rejoiced, Teresa tells us, in their manner of life,
      a portrait, albeit imperfect, of the beginnings of the
      Order (Foundations, 2.3).

- Rossi was so impressed with Teresa and her community that he
  desired that this beginning go forward.
    - He gave her ‘very extensive patent letters, so that more
      monasteries could be founded, along with censures to
      prevent any provincial from restraining me’ (ibid.).
    - These letters paten were dated 27 April 1567.
    - Rossi wrote another letter forbidding foundations in
      Andalusia 16 May 1567.
    - She affirmed that she did not ask for these, but that Rossi
      understood from her way of prayer, of her desire to help
      souls come closer to God.

- Teresa tells us she felt daunted by this commission but
  remembered the words of the Lord that she would see great
  things.
    - ‘I now perceived some beginning to what before I could not
      understand’ (Foundations, 2.4).
    - But still though the work of foundation was daunting ‘it
      seemed to me I saw them founded’ (ibid.).

- Alvaro de Mendoza, the `bishop of Ávila`, asked permission for
  some monasteries of discalced friars in his diocese.
    - There were similar requests from others, which suggests
      strongly that this idea had been discussed previously.
    - In fact, it is most likely that it was Teresa's diplomatic
      way of getting the friars she wanted.
    - Rossi consulted but found disagreement among the friars and
      decided not to disturb the province.
    - This tendency of Rossi’s not to upset the brethren by
      _approving something new_ was to cause major difficulties
      for the discalced.

- Teresa pressed the case by writing to Rossi shortly after he
  had left Ávila, begging for this permission to have friars who
  would observe the same rule as the nuns.
    - He replied in a letter from Barcelona dated 10 August, 1567
      with permission for two such houses of friars in Castile
      (Foundations, 2.5).
    - Her problem now was there were no friars in the Castile
      Province that she knew who were interested in joining her
      project.
        - “Neither courage nor hope failed, for since the Lord
          had given the one thing, He would give the other.
          Everything now seemed very possible, and so I set to
          work.”[^1]

- In August 1567 Teresa made her second foundation of nuns at
  `Medina del Campo`.
    - She will go on the make 17 foundations of nuns (15 of these
      personally) and two of friars (Duruelo and Pastrana).
    - The 'Book of the Foundations' provides a narrative of these
      foundations and much else of interest.
    - For our purposes however, we'll focus on the structural
      element of her work, rather than the story of each
      foundation.

- Pause for a moment to reflect on the role of the General
  (Rossi) in the establishment of Teresa's new foundations.
    - How would you sum up the role of the General and the role
      of Teresa in these new developments?
    - In what ways might the General have helped Teresa in
      bringing about those new foundations?


### Unit 7: Discalced Friars

- During August and September she had conversations with the
  newly ordained Carmelite `John of St Mathias` who had come to
  Medina to celebrate his first mass with his family.
    - He confided he wanted to leave the Carmelites for the more
      rigorous Carthusians.
    - John was a sensitive, hurt and angry young man.
    - Teresa first of all listened to him as he told her of his
      deepest desires. Teresa begged him to remain in his own
      Order and join `her new project` for friars.
    - John promised he would, provided he didn’t have to wait
      long (Foundations, 3.17).

- Teresa also recruited the Carmelite prior of Medina, `Anthony
  of Jesus` (de Heredia) at this time.
    - As Carole Slade notes, “Teresa portrays herself as founder,
      the one who conceives what Edward Said calls the ‘beginning
      intention’ the ‘created inclusiveness within which the work
      develops.’”[^2]

- Teresa was offered a disused, remote, small farm building at
  `Duruelo`.
    - On her way to founding the Valladolid monastery, she
      visited Duruelo and was suitably impressed by its rustic
      austerity.
    - In Medina she spoke with Anthony and John, both of whom
      agreed to be part of the foundation at Duruelo.
    - She obtained the necessary permissions from the provincials
      and took John of the Cross with her to Valladolid, so that
      before the enclosure was established she might take
        - [The] opportunity to teach Father Fray John of the
          Cross about our way of life, so that he'd have a clear
          understanding of everything, whether it concerned
          mortification or the style of both our community life
          and the recreation we have together.
        - The recreation is taken with such moderation that it
          only serves to reveal the Sisters’ faults and to
          provide a little relief so that the rule may be kept in
          its strictness.
        - He was so good that I, at least, could have learned
          much more from him than he from me. Yet this is not
          what I did, but taught him about the lifestyle of the
          Sisters (Foundations, 13.5).

  - November 1568.
    - John had received the Discalced habit from the provincial
      during the summer, much to the annoyance of Anthony.
    - Anthony was the prior and along with John there were two
      others _who did not persevere_.

- The following Lent on her way to make a foundation in Toledo,
  Teresa dropped in on Duruelo.
    - She was surprised to discover Anthony sweeping the porch
      and asked him the pointed question, “What’s this, my
      Father, what has become of your honour?”
    - He reassured her of his happiness by saying “I curse the
      day I had any (ibid. 14,6).”
    - The friars’ way of life impressed her and the local people.
    - She showed her authority as founder by urging them not to
      be so rigorous in their severe penitential practices.
    - However they paid little attention to her concerns.
    - She was happy and prayed that “things will continue as they
      are now and that my plan will indeed be realized (ibid.
      14.11 emphasis added).”
    - Teresa understood “this foundation was a much greater grace
      than the favour he granted me to found houses of nuns
      (ibid. 14.12).”

- The residence at `Duruelo` (not a priory, and so unable to
  receive novices) was transferred from its remote location to
  `Mancera` in 1570.
    - Later in her life `Ana de Jesús` recalled visiting Mancera
      in `1570` while on her way to make the Salamanca
      foundation:
        - All of us visited the Discalced friars and they told us
          what Mother Teresa and her companion Antonia del
          Espiritu Santo had taught them to do when they made the
          foundation.
        - There were then only the first two Discalced friars
          ever, namely Father Anthony of Jesus (prior) and Father
          John of the Cross (subprior).
        - These had been instructed in everything that concerned
          their lifestyle by the Holy Mother.
        - In fact, she loved to tell us of the details they used
          to ask her about, and the way God had brought our
          sisters these two fathers some five years after the
          first convent had been founded.
        - They told me a lot about their early days too, and from
          what they said I'm convinced the Holy Mother is as much
          their foundress as she was ours; that is how they
          regarded her and always will.[^3]

- However Anthony recollected the relationship very differently
  in later years:
    - “I was not governed by Mother Teresa but by the
      constitutions of the order and by the reform laid down by
      a general chapter in Venice in 1524.
        - It was from that I took the present form of our habit
          and mantle, the wearing of sandals, and all the rest.
        - And it is with this that I defended myself afterwards
          against the accusation of having introduced new
          things.” [^4]
    - Anthony had begun to style himself as the founder of the
      Discalced Carmelite friars- a group in his view that
      _offered nothing new_ beyond the reform of Audet laid down.

- In his relations with Teresa he showed a jealous immaturity.
    - Although he esteemed her, he resented that she preferred
      others who had entered after him.
    - He never saved any of her letters to him.
    - He ordered Teresa at the end of her life to go to Alba
      rather than Avila for the profession of her niece Teresita.
    - Anthony, although learned and a good preacher had poor
      judgement and was given to ascetical excesses.
    - At the 1581 chapter he was forbidden to go barefoot but
      wear sandals like everyone else.
    - Teresa was to have moments when she regretted founding the
      friars (Foundations, 23.12).
    - Anthony's last years were spent in Granada. He died in
      Malaga, at the age of 91, in March 1601.

- Teresa was to have no shortage of frustrations and
  disappointments in her life as a founder of new monasteries.
    - She had to deal with ecclesiastical authorities who rarely
      welcomed the prospect of a new community in their midst.
    - She needed all her social graces to bring to birth what she
      believed would allow women to make a valuable contribution.

- If her charm was legendary, so too was her determination and
  courage.
    - She was not afraid of confrontation, as at `Toledo` where
      she spoke boldly to the ecclesiastical governor of the 15).
    - The `Archbishop of Toledo`, Bartolomé Caranza, was
      literally otherwise detained as he was a prisoner of the
      Inquisition.
    - His crime was to advocate interior prayer, even for women.
        - His sad fate tells us much about the mood of the
          hierarchy and his brother Dominicans at the time.
    - Teresa was emboldened by some words from the Lord in this
      situation:
        - ‘You will grow _very foolish_, daughter, if you look at
          the world’s laws. Fix your eyes on me, poor and
          despised by the world.’ [^5]
    - It is also interesting that Jesus in this locution equates
      the _world with the hierarchy_ telling Teresa to fix her
      eyes on him alone.

- Teresa was criticised for her founding activity so unbecoming
  an enclosed nun.
    - She wondered if her critics might be right and that she
      would be better occupied staying at home devoting herself
      to prayer.
    - She then heard these words:
        - ‘While one is alive, progress does not come from trying
          to enjoy me more, but by trying `to do my will`.’ [^6]
        - When reflecting on what St Paul said about women’s
          enclosure (Titus 2.5 and 1Cor. 14.34) she heard the
          Lord say:
            - ‘Tell them they shouldn’t follow just one part of
              Scripture but should look at other parts, and ask
              them if by chance they can tie my hands’ (ibid).
        - Teresa’s Jesus is very much an enabler of the rights of
          women.

- It is also worth noting that just as she objected to the time
  wasted in socialising in the parlours of the Incarnation, so
  after her foundation she was not afraid to spend time in the
  parlours of convents.
    - The difference was her socialising was now spent for the
      spiritual benefit of others or for mutual benefit.
    - Similarly while regretting time spent outside the enclosure
      at the Incarnation, Teresa is not afraid to become
      a tireless traveller throughout Spain on the bad roads of
      her day in the service of a her great spiritual adventure.
    - The difference is her `sense of mission` in making her
      foundations.
    - Teresa is not someone who thinks beautiful thoughts are
      enough. What is needed is love in `action`, not dreams:
        - How does the soul acquire this love? By being
          determined to work and to suffer, and to do so when the
          occasion arises.
        - It's true that a soul’s determination grows, by
          thinking of what we owe the Lord, of who He is, and
          what we are  and that this thinking is very meritorious
          and appropriate for beginners.
        - But it must be understood that this is true provided
          nothing interferes with _obedience_ or benefit to
          _one’s neighbour_.

- Pause for a moment to reflect on the role of a Religious
  Founder/Foundress.
    - Do you think that, like St Francis and the Franciscans for
      example, all Religious Founders must have followed the same
      lifestyle as the group they founded?
    - Even though she lived the life of a Discalced Carmelite
      Nun, do you think St Teresa could, nevertheless, could be
      regarded as the Founder of the Discalced Carmelite Friars?

### Unit 8: John of the Cross

- As we have seen, John of the Cross was the first Discalced
  Carmelite friar.
    - He was born in Fontiveros in Avila in `1542`.
    - While there are a number of stories and new theses put
      forward in recent times, it is difficult to know with
      certitude about his family background beyond the fact of
      their abject poverty.
    - Such poverty was more typical of old Catholic peasant
      families than Judeo-conversos.
    - Teofanes Egido, OCD, the eminent professor of history in
      Valladolid, holds that it is difficult to write a properly
      critical biography of John.

- We do know his `father`, Gonzalo de Yepes, and a brother died
  when he was young.
    - His mother Catalina Alvarez brought her two surviving sons
      first to Arévalo and then in about 1551 to Medina del
      Campo, begging and looking for work in order to survive.
    - They were part of the _pilgrimage of hunger_ when peasant
      left the countryside for the city to look for work to
      support and feed themselves.
    - This dislocation came about because aristocrats could earn
      more cash from `Merino sheep` on broad acres than from
      peasant working their land. Economic change brought social
      dislocation then as it does now.

- John worked from 1552 in a Medina hospital for the victims of
  venereal disease.
    - There he impressed his benefactor who made it possible for
      him to study at a Jesuit night school from 1559 and so
      matriculate.

- John joined the newly founded Carmelite priory in Medina in
  1563 at the age of 21.
    - He was professed as `John of St Matthias` after his year’s
      novitiate in 1564. He studied arts at the University of
      Salamanca and was ordained in 1567.
    - At the time of his return to Medina for his first mass, he
      met Teresa and conversed with her.
    - Teresa persuaded him not to leave the order but to join her
      new group. These conversations are the beginning of
      a fruitful relationship from which we all have benefited.

- With the prior Anthony of Jesus, he was part of the first house
  of Discalced friars at `Duruelo` begun on 28 November, 1568.
    - John accompanied Teresa on the foundation of Alba de Tormes
      in 1571.
    - However in April that year he was appointed the first
      rector of the first College of the Discalced Carmelites at
      the University of Alcalá.

- In 1572 when Teresa had been obliged to become the prioress of
  her old community of the `Incarnation in Avila` she asked that
  John of the Cross support her by becoming the confessor of the
  community.
    - This was a time of close spiritual partnership and mutual
      benefit between Teresa and John.
    - John worked at this time as vicar for the nuns and was
      involved in their affairs in a number of ways.
    - He accompanied Teresa on the foundation in Segovia in 1574.

- Teresa could joke and tease John while making a serious point.
    - In the satirical critique she takes issue with John's
      perfectionism,
        - “Seeking God would be very costly if we could not do so
          until we were dead to the world.” [^1]
        - For Teresa, God calls sinners rather than the perfect.
        - Yet Teresa truly regarded him as a saint,
            - ”Friar John of the Cross is considered by
              everybody, and by all the sisters, as a saint, and
              I don't think they are rating him too highly.
            - In my opinion he is a tower of strength.”[^2]

- Teresa wrote to Ann of Jesus at Beas:
    - ”He is a divine heavenly man. I assure you my daughter,
      since he left us I have not found another like him in the
      whole of Castile, nor anyone else who inspires souls with
      such fervour to journey to heaven.
    - You would never believe how lonely I feel without him.
      Consider what a treasure you have in that saint”
    - (Letter 277, to Ann of Jesus, Nov.-Dec., 1578).
    - She was also concerned enough about his happiness to
      intercede for him with Gracián in regard to his desire to
      return from Andalusia to Castile
        - (Letter 384 to Gracián, 23-24 March, 1581).

- John habitually referred to Teresa as ‘nuestra madre’, ‘our
  mother’, (cf. John's first Letter to Maria de Jesús, blood
  sister of Catalina de Jesús, 6/7/1581) because, as Kavanagh
  notes, she was the ‘foundress and spiritual mother of the
  Discalced Carmelites.’ [^3]
    - In the Spiritual Canticle, John when talking of the
      different kinds of raptures writes,
        - ‘the blessed Teresa of Jesus, our Mother, left writings
          about these spiritual matters that are admirably done
          and which I hope will soon be printed and brought to
          light’ (Spiritual Canticle, 13.7).
        - John then highly values Teresa as a spiritual teacher.

- In the 'Living Flame of Love', John writes of mystical gifts
  which have a bodily effect.
    - He discusses the piercing of the heart by a seraph in what
      appears to be a clear reference to the experience of St
      Teresa (Life, 29, 13-14).
    - As to why some may be gifted with this grace John writes:
        - ‘Few persons have reached these heights. Some have,
          however, especially those whose virtue and spirit were
          to be diffused among _their children_.
        - With respect to the first fruits of the spirit, God
          accords to `founders` wealth and value commensurate
          with the greater or lesser following they will have in
          their doctrine and spirituality’ (Living Flame, 2.12).
        - John clearly considered Teresa the founder and mother
          of a religious family.

- John shared with Teresa a basic optimism in human potential,
  which characterises the spirit of the Christian humanism in
  Spain, influenced by Erasmus.
    - I am struck by his acceptance of the `Copernican theory`
      that it was the earth not sun which moved (Living Flame,
      4.4) fifty years before the condemnation of Galileo in Rome
      for similar views.
        - This was thanks to his education at the University of
          Salamanca.
    - In his dealings with his rigorous brethren he was active in
      moderating excessive penance.
    - He was a wise guide to novices refreshing their spirits
      through prayer made in the midst of nature. He was kind and
      generous to those who were sick.

- His gifts were many.
    - His _poetry_ is among the greatest lyric poetry in Spanish.
    - He wrote much of the poem The Spiritual Canticle while in
      a monastic prison.
    - He was a gentle, _self-effacing_ man who had a gift for
      friendship.
    - He gave personalised _spiritual direction_ to rich and
      poor, religious and laity, the learned and simple.
    - He wrote out sayings specially to help individual penitents
      which were later collected and preserved.
    - From his own experience he coined the symbol of the '_Dark
      Night_ of the Soul'.
    - His prose works were begun as commentaries on his poems to
      unlock their profound mystical sense.

- John strikes me a strikingly complete human being.
    - He is not dependent on anyone. His approach to the
      spiritual life is personal and assured.
    - He has his own style and approach. While he shares
      important terms with Teresa, notably spiritual betrothal
      and spiritual marriage, his insights, images and
      understanding of the mystical life are his own.
    - For his contribution to the church’s understanding of the
      mystical life he was named a _doctor of the church_ in
      1926.
    - He held a number of responsible ministries within the
      discalced friars from novice master, college rector, prior,
      provincial, to definitor and membership of the governing
      consulta he was never the leader or founder of the friars,
      nor the leader of a faction within the community.

- While he did not have great power he certainly did have immense
  inner strength and in the midst of persecutions remained free
  and flourished as a person.

- In examining his relationship to Teresa and his life we might
  begin to appreciate the creative way he lived what has since
  been called the _Teresian charism_.


### Unit 8: Teresa as writer & teacher

- Teresa had always been a reader and a lover of books.
    - When she was having difficulties with her confessors they
      asked her to write an account of her life and spiritual
      experiences so that they could judge her spirit.
    - She wrote or rewrote a number of these accounts which
      became amplified into the book of her Life.
    - While written for her confessors as well as being
      a spiritual autobiography, it describes the different
      stages of prayer.
    - In writing for her first audience of theologians and
      clergy, Teresa wants to convert them so they no longer are
      frightened by interior prayer but can see how, in the case
      of Teresa, it changed her and made her more virtuous and
      loyal to the Church.
    - Teresa came to think that if she had to write about prayer
      it would have to be of benefit to her sisters in San José
      and indeed to anyone who was called to interior prayer.
    - Very effectively she used the image of the four ways of
      watering a garden as _four types of prayer_.

- What is striking is that Teresa, in a way similar to Ignatius
  and his Spiritual Exercises, uses her `personal experience` as
  the source for her much of her teaching.
    - She learnt from books but most significantly it was the
      Lord who taught her.
    - And so she, and a woman at that, sets out to replace the
      books banned in the 1559 Index of Valdez with a new set of
      spiritual writings which were _theologically orthodox_ in
      the eyes of the officials of the Inquisition.
    - Teresa believed that she has been given a gift from God
      that she was to share so as to help others and to make her
      contribution to the reform and growth of the church.
    - Her writing was a way of sharing of her spirit.
    - She used her own experience as a key to guide others. She
      did not write so that the reader may desire to have similar
      experiences to her own but that the reader might come to
      understand their _own experiences_.
    - It was, above all, as a writer that Teresa became
      a spiritual mother guiding the spiritual lives of her
      children.

- In the 'Life' she contrasts her old life with her new life
  after her conversion through her `commitment to interior
  prayer` (Life, 23).
    - As a result, Teresa insisted on a _proper spiritual
      autonomy_ of her communities and instituted times of
      interior prayer (oración mental).
    - Julián de Avila, the chaplain and companion on many of the
      foundations, believed that this was a requirement “no other
      religious order has.”[^4]

- In the 'Way of Perfection' Teresa wrote for her nuns at San
  José.
    - They had asked for help in their prayer and Teresa sets out
      to provide it.
    - Given that they were forbidden to read the 'Life', Teresa
      has to write another book. She does this within the context
      of the meaning of their lives and how she founded the
      convent as a response to the Reformation (Way, 1) and as
      a way for women to respond to this crisis.
    - The ways of passive prayer, so misunderstood by many of the
      clergy, were made clear for her nuns in the familiar tone
      of voice with which Teresa teaches them.
        - In this way she further makes relative the authority of
          the clergy.

- Moreover before speaking about interior prayer in the Way
  Teresa spends some time telling the sisters how they are to
  live together.
    - Their communities are to be small and poor. The community
      is not a place of factions but a place where ‘all must be
      friends, all must be loved, all must be held dear, all must
      be helped.’ [^5]
    - The journey is made together in love, detachment/freedom
      and humility understood as walking in the truth of who one
      is (Way 4.4f).

- Teresian asceticism is about becoming more human,
    - ‘the holier they are the more sociable they are with their
      sisters… be affable, agreeable, and pleasing to persons
      with whom we deal.’ [^6]
    - Teresa warns against having an idea of God that leads
      people to anxiety and worry:
        - “strive to think rightly about God, for he doesn't look
          at trifles as much as you think, and don't lose your
          courage or allow your soul to be constrained for many
          blessings could be lost.”[^7]

- Teresa may have thought that the 'Life' could benefit anyone
  who sought to pray, but it was being examined by the
  Inquisition and unavailable.
    - In 1577, while in the Carmel in Toledo and at a time when
      the whole discalced movement was fighting for its
      independence and survival, she was asked by Jerome Gracián
      to write a book on prayer that was less obviously
      autobiographical than the Life.
    - This was opportune as Teresa had matured in her experience
      of God and had time to reflect on it and understand it
      better.
    - It must also be said that Teresa enjoyed writing books.
    - She was proud of the `Life` which she called ‘my book’ and
      the `Way` which she called ‘my little book’.

- The Interior Castle was to be another attempt to answer the
  questions of her nuns in the different Teresian foundations
  about prayer.
    - So she adopts a familiar tone while laying claim to divine
      inspiration,
        - “if I managed to say something well, the sisters will
          understand that this does not come from me since there
          would be no foundation for it, unless the Lord gave it
          to me” (Interior Castle, prologue. 4).
    - It is an assured synthesis.

- While Teresa stresses that mystical experiences are always
  a gift of God, she appears to believe that God is generous with
  his gifts.
    - She was confident that many of the nuns in her communities
      were gifted in this way.
    - That she was a mystic did not mean that she believed she
      was exceptional.
    - Mysticism was, she held, more ordinary than unusual.
    - She writes of the help, rather than criticism, that sisters
      receive from their community. [^8]
    - Such community support is a sacrament of joyous communion
      with all creation. [^9]
    - Teresa’s optimism about human potential under grace is
      clearly shown. She believed the mystical graces given to
      her were not only for her own benefit, but to build up the
      communities she had founded and the church itself.

- For Teresa, Christ dwells in the centre of the human person.
    - In this sometimes confusing spiritual quest, we need to
      keep oriented, 'turn your eyes towards the centre,'[^10]
      and 'we should keep our eyes on Christ, our Good.' [^11]
    - This divine indwelling takes many forms,
        - 'You should not think in terms of just a few rooms but
          in terms of a million'. [^12]
        - Those she wrote of were from her own experience.

- The journey to union with God was also a journey in self
  knowledge and _personal integration_.
    - Problems come from 'fears [which] come from not
      understanding ourselves completely.' [^13]
    - It is a homecoming to our centre, our truest self.
    - To live from our centre where God dwells means to be open
      to the Holy Spirit in our lives.
    - Teresa moved beyond a purely Christocentric focus to
      a `Trinitarian` understanding of God at work in her as
      a result of a number of experiences of the Trinity.  [^14]
    - She places these experiences of the Trinity in the seventh
      dwelling place.[^15]

- Her great metaphor for the spiritual life was that of that of
  a relationship of friendship that grows into Spiritual marriage
  where the human and divine wills are united.
    - In doing this Teresa relativised and demythologised
      extraordinary mystical phenomena.
    - For her they were not the end of the journey but
      transitional signs of God at work in the soul’s
      purification and transformation.
    - The purpose of spiritual marriage and the prayer that goes
      with it is ‘the birth always of good works, good works’ (IC
      VII, 4.6).
    - For her true union with God is a friendship which leads us
      to desire what God desires for the world and for us and to
      do what God wants done in the world.
    - It is about the flow of being at one with the Holy Spirit
      creatively at work in us, our families and communities and
      in our world.

- In the Interior Castle Teresa goes beyond her Life.

- The goal of the Interior Castle is not raptures or intense
  experiences. It is not a self-help book “Mysticism for
  Dummies”. It is a seven-step spiritual recovery programme whose
  starting point is the assurance of being loved and that one’s
  true worth is a gift.
    - It leads us to discover our real self as loved by God.
    - Through a variety of experiences we are taken beyond the
      “good rule-obeying Catholic.”
    - We are changed by allowing ourselves to be loved and to
      rest through which experiences we find we are not in
      control nor do we have to be.
    - We are called to be stretched by both the experience of the
      cross and spiritual gifts in the intensification, not
      lessening, of desire.
    - We come to discover our `unique identity` and vocation in
      ordinary life. We are able to love in creative ways that
      bring the birth surprising new life for ourselves and those
      who are in need.

- The Teresian scholar and parish priest in Zaragoza, Antonio
  Mas, testifies that he sees the pattern Teresa sets out in her
  finest book in the experience of recovering alcoholics, drug
  addicts and prostitutes as well as gay people dying with AIDS.
    - The Holy Spirit moves people to union with God in unlikely
      circumstances. Teresa’s Interior Castle helps us discover
      the signs of God’s work in us and others and how to best
      respond to that gift.

- God led different souls by different routes at different times
  (Foundations, 5.5).
    - Not all are called to be contemplatives and for all of us
      God walks among ‘the pots and pans’ of daily life (ibid.,
      5.8)
    - But all are called to `want what God wills` for them and
      for the world. In this understanding a shared Christian
      discipleship unites mystic and the most humble believer.
    - What is shared in common is far more important than what
      separates.
    - This is exemplified in Teresa’s account of her own
      experience of spiritual marriage. Jesus when he takes her
      as his bride gives her as a symbol of their covenant not
      a wedding ring but a nail (Spiritual Testimonies, 31).
    - Spiritual marriage is not sharing the glory of the Lord
      without taking up one’s cross and following him in ways
      that are creative and life giving.

- Differences in the way the Lord leads different people led
  Teresa to give attention to how and by whom authority is
  wielded.
    - The prioress had an important role in spiritual direction
      of the sisters of the community.
    - In fact the prioress was to be the primary spiritual
      director of the community rather than confessors or other
      clergy.
    - Teresa was well aware of the medicine of her day and so she
      took Galenic psychology for the discernment of spirits,
      particularly in regard to ‘melancholia’ (Foundations, 7).
    - She is also aware that law should not become a burden as ‘a
      weighed down soul cannot serve God well’ (Letter 376 to
      Gracián, 21/2/1581).
    - Teresa went as far as to write a guide 'On Making the
      Visitation' for Gracián on how to conduct visitations of
      the nuns.
    - While his tendency may have been to be too kind and
      charitable, others were more problematic by interfering in
      the life of the community by imposing new laws and fresh
      penances.

- While her writings were intended for anyone interested in the
  spiritual life, they had a practical concern to guide the lives
  of those who lived in the communities founded by her.
    - In them she shared her spirit and elaborated a distinctive
      spiritual path. She also communicates her own personality
      through the informal way in which she writes.
    - Teresa comes across as a woman who was a saint but also
      a mature and integrated human being with a lively sense of
      humour and an ability to laugh at the absurd in life.
    - In many ways these human qualities become more apparent in
      her Letters. We come to know the warmth of her personality
      and her sense of daring and adventure in the face of
      tremendous obstacles.
    - We also see her crotchety and frustrated, sad and very
      human. To paraphrase St Irenaeus, Teresa is a human person
      fully alive.

- Given how gullible I am when faced with clever advertising, it
  seems that Rene Girard is right and that we desire what others
  desire.
    - For him this is an intrinsic part of being human.
    - We are made by our desires.
    - I not only want the flash car or tropical holiday, I also
      want the being of the other who has these things, their
      effortless superiority and success.
    - Rather than enter the world of rivalry and competition for
      scarce resources our way out of violence and into peace is
      to desire what God desires and to do what God wants.

- For most of us it is easier to relate to a human person than an
  ideal.
    - So we imitate Christ and the saints and founders of
      religious communities. Saints like St Francis, inspire
      imitation and devotion.
    - Given Teresa’s warm humanity she is a wonderful model to
      imitate, especially for those who look to her as a founder
      of a particular way of being Christian.
