1. How would you describe St Teresa's life in the Monastery of
   the Incarnation?

St Teresa's life at the Monastery of the Incarnation was a mix of
having some comforts and dealing with spiritual challenges. She
joined in 1535 and took her vows two years later, but faced
serious illnesses that sometimes required her to leave the
monastery (Source-01). The convent was crowded and had social
inequalities, with the rich and poor forming groups (Source-01).
Nuns often had to win the favor of wealthy donors to get support.
Though Teresa had some privileges, she was unhappy with the lack
of spiritual passion and was critical of how social status
influenced the convent (Source-01). Coming from a lower social
background, she had a unique view and sought a deeper connection
with Christ through prayer (Source-01). Her experiences made her
want to reform the convent to focus more on spiritual values than
social ones. During her time at the Incarnation, Teresa struggled
with needing acceptance but also realized she wanted to change
religious life, leading her to start a new community focused on
spirituality over social status (Source-01). This mix of comfort
and inner struggle defined her time there.

2. How do you understand what St Teresa describes as her
   'conversion'?

St Teresa's 'conversion' was a big change in her spiritual life,
happening around Lent in 1554. Before this, she had trouble with
her faith and felt a strong need for love. During this time, she
started praying more to Jesus, feeling very alone. She felt
assured of Jesus' acceptance, which was crucial because she often
doubted her own worth (Source-01). Her conversion was more of
a process than a single event, involving a renewed commitment to
prayer and seeing herself as a friend of Jesus (Source-01). This
change marked the start of a new mission for her within the
Church, even though women were often not allowed to speak
publicly or take active roles (Source-01). She also began to
receive spiritual visions and found guidance from a new Jesuit
confessor who supported her spiritual experiences (Source-01).
This period made her feel complete, reinforced her mission, and
led to the creation of new communities focusing on spirituality
rather than social status. Her conversion was a significant
turning point, freeing her from societal limits and launching her
into active work within the Church (Source-01).

3. What were the most significant features of the new life of St
   Teresa following her conversion?

After her conversion, St Teresa dedicated herself to a lively and
meaningful spiritual life centered around her relationship with
Christ. Her new life focused on deep prayer and a strong sense of
mission. She aimed to reform religious life by founding new
convents, like San José, which focused on poverty, simplicity,
and equality among nuns, moving away from the lifestyle at the
Monastery of the Incarnation (Source-02, Source-03). She received
spiritual visions and felt supported by spiritual advisors like
the Jesuits, who encouraged her (Source-01). Her new life
included a strong commitment to living simply and without luxury,
inspired by religious movements at the time (Source-02). She
wasn't just about strict rules but also about building
relationships with Christ and among the sisters (Source-03).
Teresa wrote many spiritual books, like 'The Way of Perfection'
and 'The Interior Castle,' to guide others (Source-04). As
a founder, she focused on living a spiritual life with deep
meaning, not just for herself but for those she led.

4. What was the model for the new monastery of San José?

San José, the first new convent Teresa started, was based on
ideas of simplicity, poverty, and strong spirituality, similar to
the discalced Franciscans (Source-02). Teresa and her friends
didn't think they could fully match them but were inspired by
their focus on Christ and strong spiritual life (Source-02). She
wanted the convent to move away from the relaxed rules of her old
convent to one that followed the strict Carmelite Rule
(Source-02). Teresa aimed for a return to living simply and
without luxury, which was a big part of the discalced movement
(Source-02). Her idea for San José wasn't just to go back to old
ways but to adapt them for current times. She wanted the convent
to be a place of prayer where the sisters could pray deeply
without needing to follow many of the distractions of coexisting
with the benefactor's prayers, making worship simpler and purer
(Source-02). San José was meant to be a small, joyful place
focused on friendship and equality among the nuns (Source-03).

5. What were the elements of the life of the sisters at San José?

The life of the sisters at San José was simple and equal,
focusing mainly on prayer. It moved away from traditional
hierarchy, where each sister was seen as equal (Source-02).
Teresa set up rules that emphasized relationships: with Christ,
with each other, and with the world in spiritual service
(Source-02). They practiced strict poverty, reflecting Teresa's
ideas of the Carmelite Rule and the reform movements of her time
(Source-02). Manual labor was a big part, removing class
differences and promoting shared responsibility and community
(Source-02). San José’s nuns lived a contemplative life, focusing
on prayer and limiting outside interference (Source-02). Although
they lived simply—often avoiding meat and fasting for eight
months—Teresa emphasized that the community should be joyful and
loving, fostering friendships and cherishing life (Source-03).
Interior prayer was at the center of their community life,
helping each sister grow both spiritually and in their
relationships (Source-02).

6. How were Teresa's horizons expanded so that she wanted to make
   more foundations?

Teresa of Ávila wanted to create more convents after founding San
José because her vision expanded significantly. Initially, Teresa
had started San José because she was unhappy with the relaxed
lifestyle at the Carmelite convent of the Incarnation. Her drive
for more foundations was sparked further in the summer of 1566
when she spoke with a passionate Franciscan missionary, Alonso
Maldonado, who told her about many unbaptized souls in Mexico and
Latin America, inspiring her compassion and desire to act
(Source-03). Saddened by the thought of these souls being lost,
she prayed to God for help in promoting His service, reigniting
her desire to create more Carmelite communities. Teresa was
driven by a spirit of mission and a desire to make a difference
in the world (Source-03). Her conviction was strengthened by
a divine assurance she received: "Wait a little daughter, and you
will see great things" (Source-03). These experiences convinced
Teresa to continue establishing new communities, seeing it as an
essential contribution to the Church during a time of spiritual
challenges.

7. Why did Teresa found the discalced friars?

Teresa founded the discalced friars because she wanted to extend
her reform efforts to the Carmelite friars, following her success
with the nuns. At first, she focused on setting up reformed
female communities like San José. But as her spiritual insights
grew and practical needs arose, she saw the benefit in a similar
reform for friars. There were many requests sent to Carmelite
General John Baptist Rossi for discalced friars in different
areas, suggesting ongoing plans and discussions for this
expansion (Source-03). Teresa used diplomatic means, like writing
to Rossi, to explain her intentions and get permission. She
wanted friars who would follow the same strict observance and
poverty as the nuns (Source-03). John of St Mathias, who later
became John of the Cross, was among the first to join her effort
after Teresa convinced him not to leave the Carmelites
(Source-03). Her vision was to establish communities dedicated to
deep prayer and simple living to support and renew the Church
spiritually. The discalced friars helped put into practice
a disciplined Carmelite lifestyle that aligned with her ideals,
furthering her reforms.

8. How did Teresa prepare John of the Cross for the new
   foundation?

When Teresa met John of the Cross, then called John of St
Mathias, she saw a chance to bring him into her reform project.
John was thinking about leaving the Carmelites for a more
demanding life with the Carthusians (Source-03). Teresa had deep
conversations with him to encourage him to direct his spiritual
goals towards joining her new venture with the discalced friars.
She promised John it wouldn't be long before he could start
living this new way. To prepare him, she explained the practices
and lifestyle of her reformed community during their travels to
Valladolid (Source-03). This included the community life,
discipline, and practices followed by the discalced nuns, which
she wanted to replicate for the friars (Source-03). Even though
John was already spiritually mature, Teresa thought it was
crucial to guide him towards her vision for the new foundation.
Her teaching and guidance ensured John was ready for the
disciplined life of the newly formed discalced friars in Duruelo,
aligning his goals with her reform efforts.

9. How would you characterize the relationship between John of
   the Cross and Teresa?

The relationship between John of the Cross and Teresa of Ávila
was a strong spiritual partnership built on mutual respect and
shared goals in religion. Teresa saw John as a holy figure and
greatly valued his spiritual wisdom and enthusiasm. She relied on
him for support, especially when she faced challenges as the
leader of the Incarnation (Source-04). Their partnership was
mutually beneficial, with Teresa recognizing John's spiritual
abilities, calling him a ‘tower of strength,’ and missing him
when he was not around (Source-04). John, in return, regarded
Teresa highly, calling her "our mother" and acknowledging her as
the foundress and spiritual guide of the Discalced Carmelites
(Source-04). He valued her spiritual teachings and eagerly
awaited her writings, considering them important for spiritual
growth (Source-04). This respect shows the depth of their
friendship and strong bond. Despite having their spiritual
journeys and duties, John and Teresa’s relationship was rooted in
their dedication to reforming the Carmelite Order and making
spiritual lives better for those they influenced.

10. For whom did Teresa write? Why did Teresa assume the male
    role of a teacher and writer?

Teresa of Ávila was deeply motivated to write mainly for her nuns
at San José and anyone interested in deepening their
understanding of prayer. Her writings aimed to guide her sisters
in spiritual development, offering practical advice based on her
own experiences (Source-04). Initially, she wrote at the request
of her confessors to explain her spiritual experiences, but she
soon realized her writings could help others beyond just
evaluating her spirit (Source-04). Taking on the role of
a teacher and writer, which was typically held by men, was
a significant step for Teresa in an era when women were expected
to remain silent on religious topics. She was confident that God
guided her teachings and experiences, giving her the authority to
share them (Source-04). Teresa wrote because she had to; the
spiritual books she valued were banned at the time, so she aimed
to fill this gap with her writings. This not only helped bring
back spiritual freedom for women and backed her reform mission
but also challenged the male-led church hierarchy that often
pushed back against deep personal prayer.
