1. How would you describe St Teresa's life in the Monastery of
   the Incarnation?

St Teresa's life at the Monastery of the Incarnation was a mix of
social privilege and spiritual struggle. After joining in 1535,
she took her vows in 1537 but suffered from serious illnesses,
a traumatic event for her that saw her outside the monastery at
times (Source-01). The convent was overcrowded and faced social
inequality, with rich and poor members creating divisions and
cliques (Source-01). Nuns often had to ingratiate themselves with
wealthy benefactors to secure donations. Despite living
a relatively privileged life within the community, Teresa was
frustrated by the lack of spiritual vigor and critical of the way
the convent was influenced by social structures (Source-01). Her
background as someone on the margins gave her a unique
perspective, and she sought a deeper relationship with Christ
through interior prayer (Source-01). Teresa's experience in this
environment ultimately fuelled her desire for reform, seeking
a community that prioritised spiritual over social values. Her
life at the Incarnation was marked by her need for affection and
acceptance, yet it was also a period of discernment, leading to
her resolve to reform religious life and establish a new
community (Source-01). This duality of comfort and inner conflict
defined much of her time at the Monastery of the Incarnation.

2. How do you understand what St Teresa describes as her
   'conversion'?

St Teresa's 'conversion' was a transformative spiritual
awakening, often linked to an experience in Lent 1554.
Previously, she had been struggling with issues of faith and her
personal need for affection. During this period, she began to
turn to Jesus in prayer amidst feelings of isolation. She felt
assured of being accepted by Christ, which was pivotal because
she often doubted her own lovability (Source-01). Her conversion
was not just a single moment but a process that entailed
a renewed commitment to interior prayer, helping her to envision
herself as a valued friend of Jesus (Source-01). This shift
marked the beginning of a new calling and mission for her within
the Church, despite being a woman in an era when public speaking
or active roles were limited for women (Source-01). She also
started receiving mystical graces and found guidance from her new
Jesuit confessor, who affirmed her spiritual experiences
(Source-01). This period helped her to feel whole, nurtured her
mission, and led to the foundation of new communities that
emphasised spiritual over social constructs. Her conversion was
a profound coming into her own spiritual identity, freeing her
from societal constraints and launching her into a life of
dynamic action and creativity within the Church (Source-01).

3. What were the most significant features of the new life of St
   Teresa following her conversion?

After her conversion, St Teresa embraced a dynamic and purposeful
spiritual life driven by her relationship with Christ. Her new
life was marked by a commitment to interior prayer and a deep
sense of mission. Following her conversion, Teresa sought reform
in religious life, founding new convents such as San José that
emphasised poverty, simplicity, and equality among nuns, breaking
away from the compromised lifestyle at the Incarnation
(Source-02, Source-03). She received mystical graces and felt
supported by spiritual directors like the Jesuits, who encouraged
her spiritual experiences (Source-01). Teresa's new life also
entailed a radical commitment to poverty and asceticism, inspired
by reformed movements of her time, such as those by the discalced
Franciscans (Source-02). Her approach was less about strict
observance of rules and more about fostering
relationships—personal ones with Christ and communal ones among
the sisters (Source-03). Teresa also became a prolific writer,
sharing her spiritual insights and becoming a teacher and guide,
particularly through works like 'The Way of Perfection' and 'The
Interior Castle' (Source-04). In her role as a founder, Teresa
emphasized living a spiritual life that was meaningful and
impactful, not only for herself but for those she guided.

4. What was the model for the new monastery of San José?

San José, the first of Teresa’s new foundations, was modelled on
the ideals of simplicity, poverty, and fervent spirituality akin
to the discalced Franciscans (Source-02). Teresa and her friends
did not think they could fully match the discalced Franciscans
but were inspired by their renewed fervor and Christocentric
spirituality (Source-02). She envisioned a monastery that moved
away from the mitigated rule practices which compromised the
spiritual integrity of her former convent, aiming instead for
a community that embraced the Carmelite Rule without mitigation
(Source-02). Teresa sought a return to the primitive observance
focusing on absolute poverty, which defined the discalced
movement (Source-02). Her approach with San José was not merely
restoring the past but creatively adapting it for contemporary
needs. Teresa wanted this community to act as a house of prayer,
where interior prayer could be practised without the encumbrance
of mandatory prayers for benefactors, allowing for a simpler and
purer form of worship (Source-02). Ultimately, San José was
a little "heaven on earth," meant to exist in austerity yet
foster joy, equality, and friendship among a small number of nuns
(Source-03).

5. What were the elements of the life of the sisters at San José?

The life of the sisters at San José was characterized by
simplicity, equality, and a focus on prayer. It was a departure
from the traditional hierarchies, emphasizing a communal life
where each sister was regarded equally (Source-02). Teresa
established rules that focused on relationships: relationships
with Christ, with one another, and with the world in spiritual
service (Source-02). The community practiced strict poverty,
reflecting Teresa's understanding of the Carmelite Rule and the
fervent reform movements of her time (Source-02). Manual labour
was seen as an equalizing force, eliminating the disparities of
aristocratic privilege, and creating a sense of shared
responsibility and community (Source-02). San José required the
nuns to adopt a life of contemplative prayer, allowing them
liberty in spiritual direction and reducing clerical control
(Source-02). While austerity was a significant aspect—meat was
usually avoided, and an eight-month fast was observed—Teresa
emphasized that the community should be one of joy and love,
where friendships were fostered and life was cherished
(Source-03). Interior prayer lay at the heart of community life,
allowing each sister to grow spiritually and relationally both
within the convent's walls and in her broader context
(Source-02).

6. How were Teresa's horizons expanded so that she wanted to make
   more foundations?

Teresa of Ávila's horizons expanded significantly after the
founding of San José and through interactions that heightened her
spiritual and missionary motivations. Initially, Teresa was
motivated by her dissatisfaction with the compromised living
conditions at the Carmelite convent of the Incarnation and sought
a purer religious life through the foundation of San José. Her
desire for more foundations was also ignited by a conversation in
summer 1566 with a charismatic Franciscan missionary, Alonso
Maldonado, who visited her at San José. Maldonado shared stories
about millions of unbaptised souls in Mexico and Latin America,
stirring Teresa’s compassion and desire to act (Source-03).
Grieved by the thought of these souls being lost, she pleaded
with the Lord to enable her to do something to promote His
service, which kindled her desire to create more Carmel
communities. Teresa's spirit was both apostolic and missionary,
driven by a willingness to make a difference in the world
(Source-03). Her conviction was further solidified by a divine
assurance she received: “Wait a little daughter, and you will see
great things" (Source-03). These factors collectively inspired
Teresa to devote herself to establishing new communities, which
she viewed as an important contribution to the Church during
a time of significant spiritual and religious crises.

7. Why did Teresa found the discalced friars?

Teresa founded the discalced friars out of a desire to extend her
reform movement to the Carmelite friars, mirroring her
experiences with the nuns. Initially, her focus was on reforming
the female communities with the foundation of San José. However,
both Teresa's spiritual vision and the practical needs for
supporting these convents led her to consider a similar reform
for friars. Many requests were sent to Carmelite General John
Baptist Rossi for discalced friars in different dioceses, which
indicates underlying discussions and plans for this expansion
(Source-03). Teresa’s diplomatic methods, such as writing to
Rossi and explaining her intent, were crucial for getting the
necessary permission. She wanted friars who would observe the
same rule as the nuns, maintaining a life of strict observance
and poverty (Source-03). John of St Mathias, later known as John
of the Cross, became one of the first to join her project after
Teresa successfully persuaded him not to leave the Carmelites
(Source-03). Teresa’s vision involved creating communities that
lived devoutly, focusing on contemplative prayer to support and
rejuvenate the Church spiritually. Thus, the discalced friars
helped implement a rigorous Carmelite lifestyle that aligned
closely with her ideals, allowing them to further her reforms and
mission.

8. How did Teresa prepare John of the Cross for the new
   foundation?

When Teresa met John of the Cross, then known as John of St
Mathias, she saw an opportunity to recruit him for her reform
project. John was initially considering leaving the Carmelites
for the Carthusians, seeking a more rigorous religious life
(Source-03). Teresa engaged in meaningful conversations with him,
persuading him to channel his spiritual aspirations into joining
her new venture with the discalced friars. She promised John that
he would not have to wait long before he could begin living this
new way of life. To prepare him, she took the opportunity to
educate him about the specific practices and lifestyle of her
reformed community while travelling to Valladolid (Source-03).
This included a thorough briefing on the communal life,
mortification, and recreation — elements of the lifestyle
followed by the discalced nuns, which she wished to replicate
with the friars (Source-03). Despite John's already admirable
qualities and spiritual maturity, Teresa felt it was essential to
guide him according to her vision for the new foundation. The
learning and guidance provided by Teresa ensured that John of the
Cross was well-prepared for the ascetic life of the newly
established discalced friars in Duruelo, aligning his spiritual
aspirations with her reform goals.

9. How would you characterise the relationship between John of
   the Cross and Teresa?

The relationship between John of the Cross and Teresa of Ávila
can be characterised as a deep spiritual partnership based on
mutual respect and shared religious goals. Teresa viewed John as
a saintly figure and found great value in his spiritual fervour
and wisdom. She relied on him for support, especially during her
challenging tenure as prioress of the Incarnation (Source-04).
Their partnership was one of collaboration and mutual benefit,
with Teresa recognising John’s spiritual gifts, referring to him
as a ‘tower of strength’ and deeply missing his presence when he
was away (Source-04). John, in turn, held Teresa in high esteem,
referring to her as “our mother” and acknowledging her as the
spiritual mother and founder of the Discalced Carmelites
(Source-04). He valued her spiritual teachings and looked forward
to the publication of her writings, considering them to be of
great spiritual importance (Source-04). This recognition
signifies the depth of his admiration and the strong bond between
them. Despite their individual spiritual paths and
responsibilities, John and Teresa’s relationship was rooted in
shared dedication to the reform of the Carmelite Order and mutual
aspirations to enhance the spiritual lives of those around them.

10. For whom did Teresa write? Why did Teresa assume the male
    role of a teacher and writer?

Teresa of Ávila was driven by a deep spiritual calling to write
primarily for her community of nuns at San José and for anyone
seeking a deeper understanding of interior prayer. Her writings
were meant to guide her sisters in spiritual growth and
understanding, offering them practical advice and insights
derived from her own experiences (Source-04). Although initially
Teresa wrote at the request of her confessors to describe her
spiritual experiences, she soon realised the immense value her
writings could offer to others beyond judging her spirit
(Source-04). Assuming the role of a teacher and writer,
traditionally held by men, was a significant step for Teresa
during an era when women were expected to remain silent in
religious matters. Her confidence was reinforced by her
conviction that the Lord had guided her teachings and
experiences, providing her with the authority to share them
(Source-04). Teresa’s assumption of the male role of writer and
teacher arose from necessity; the books she valued for spiritual
guidance were banned at the time, thus she aimed to fill this
void with theologically sound texts. This not only helped reclaim
spiritual autonomy for women and supported her reform mission but
also acted as a rebuttal to the male-dominated ecclesiastical
hierarchy that often resisted interior prayer.
