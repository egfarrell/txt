
Contemporary Reviews of Daniel A. Helminiak's The Same Jesus

I dug quite a bit deeper. The picture is actually more interesting than I expected: there were a number of contemporary reviews, and the overall reception was quite favorable, although reviewers differed over exactly how successfully Helminiak reconciled Jesus' full humanity with his divinity.

One caveat: I do not have direct login access to every JSTOR/ProQuest/Google Books item, so where I could verify only the bibliographic record, I will say so rather than pretend I have read the full review. I also cross-checked a specialist Lonergan Studies bibliography, which is particularly useful for tracking reviews that do not surface well in ordinary searches.

1. The Christian Century - 1986

This is probably the most enthusiastic contemporary reaction
I found.

The review describes the book as:

"It's a complete restatement of Catholic orthodoxy"

while emphasizing that Helminiak incorporates contemporary biblical interpretation and philosophical theology. It concludes memorably that "Jesus lives on these pages."

What does that mean for humanity/divinity?
This reviewer apparently sees no fundamental contradiction between Helminiak's contemporary account of Jesus and Catholic orthodoxy.

In other words:

Modern historical Jesus + genuine humanity + traditional Christian faith in divinity = possible.

2. Donal G. Dawe - Theology Today, 1987
Dawe's review is described by Helminiak's own bibliography as calling the book:

"a work of thoughtful scholarship"

and a helpful guide to contemporary Roman Catholic Christology and New Testament scholarship.

His basic reaction
Again, this is positive. Dawe seems to appreciate Helminiak's attempt to bring biblical scholarship and systematic theology together rather than treating them as competing enterprises.

For the particular question of humanity and divinity, this suggests Dawe was comfortable with Helminiak's attempt to start with the historically accessible Jesus and move toward a theological understanding of Christ.

3. Gerald Fagin, S.J. - Theological Studies, 1987
Fagin thinks Helminiak has produced an impressive synthesis and praises his creative soteriology. But he worries about the functional status of Jesus' divinity.

That's the central criticism:

If Jesus voluntarily lives entirely through his human consciousness and powers during his earthly life, what does it mean to say that he is simultaneously divine?

And then a second question follows:

If salvation is primarily Jesus' human divinization, what specifically does Jesus' own divine nature contribute to salvation?

Fagin's criticism is therefore not that Helminiak denies Jesus' divinity. Rather, he thinks Helminiak affirms it in a way that leaves its theological significance insufficiently explained.

This is, in my view, the most important criticism in the contemporary reviews.

4. Robert A. Krieg - America, 1987
Krieg's review is especially interesting because it places Helminiak's work within the huge theological problem created by modernity.

His assessment is:

"This presentation on Jesus Christ deserves serious consideration."

He praises Helminiak for taking up the issues that theologians have wrestled with since the Enlightenment.

What is significant here?
Krieg does not appear to regard Helminiak as simply throwing out traditional Christology.

Rather, Helminiak is trying to reconstruct traditional Christology so that it can survive modern historical consciousness.

That distinction is crucial.

5. Mary T. Rattigan - Horizons, 1988
This one is definitely a genuine independent scholarly review.

Citation:

Mary T. Rattigan, "The Same Jesus: A Contemporary Christology," Horizons 15/1 (Spring 1988), pp. 174-175.

Cambridge gives the DOI and bibliographic record, but the review itself is currently behind access controls.

I therefore would not attribute a particular judgment to Rattigan until the review itself can be inspected.

6. William P. Loewe - Living Light, 1988
This one is also independently confirmed.

William P. Loewe's CV lists:

"The Same Jesus, by D. Helminiak," Living Light 25 (1988), 82-83.

I have not found the actual text online, so again I would not want to manufacture a summary. But it is another genuine contemporary review.

7. Terry J. Tekippe - Review for Religious, 1989
This one is particularly interesting for the humanity/divinity question.

Tekippe says Helminiak's retrieval and defense of the concepts of "nature" and "person" is a "masterpiece of clarity." He also finds Helminiak's personal account of confronting and affirming the divinity of Christ very moving.

Why this matters
Tekippe seems to think Helminiak actually succeeds where Fagin worries he does not.

For Tekippe, Helminiak is not simply reducing Jesus to an exceptionally holy human being.

He is trying to show how:

Jesus is genuinely human;

Jesus' humanity is not a disguise;

Jesus genuinely possesses human consciousness and limitations;

and yet Jesus remains the divine Son.

So Tekippe is much more confident than Fagin that Helminiak's philosophical distinction between person and nature makes this possible.

8. Robert P. Imbelli - Church, 1990
This is another particularly valuable assessment.

Imbelli says Helminiak argues for a new Christological paradigm, but importantly:

he does not abandon the conciliar tradition.

Instead, Helminiak builds on that tradition while incorporating a renewed appreciation of Jesus' full humanity. Imbelli concludes that we should acknowledge the service Helminiak rendered in clearly identifying the issues that a new Christological paradigm must address.

This is perhaps the clearest middle position
Imbelli seems to say:

Yes, we need a new way of talking about Jesus.
No, we do not need to abandon Chalcedon.

That is essentially Helminiak's project.

10. A particularly important later discussion: John P. Galvin, 1994
This is not a review of the book, but it may actually be more useful than some of the reviews.

Galvin's article is:

John P. Galvin, "From the Humanity of Christ to the Jesus of History: A Paradigm Shift in Catholic Christology," Theological Studies 55 (1994).

Galvin explicitly discusses Helminiak and cites The Same Jesus, including Helminiak's statement that contemporary Christology needs to root Christian claims in "the historical Jesus, in the human Jesus."

Galvin identifies a potential problem in equating "historical Jesus" with "human Jesus." He notes that this can lead to the judgment that Christology "from below" cannot adequately account for Jesus' divinity as traditionally affirmed.

This is very close to Fagin's concern.

Galvin's larger argument is that contemporary Christology has shifted:

Old framework:

divinity <-> humanity
toward:

historical Jesus <-> Christ of faith
He argues that this is not merely a change of terminology. It changes the questions theology asks about Jesus.

That is extremely relevant to understanding what Helminiak was doing.

The bigger pattern
After putting all of these together, I think the reception falls into three groups.

Group 1 - "Helminiak successfully reconciles humanity and divinity"
These reviewers are basically impressed that he can take modern historical scholarship seriously without giving up orthodox Christianity.

The Christian Century

Donal Dawe

Terry Tekippe

Robert Imbelli

Their implicit answer is:

Jesus can be genuinely human without ceasing to be genuinely divine.

And they think Helminiak has made significant progress toward explaining how.

Group 2 - "Excellent project, but there is a theological gap"
This is where I would put Gerald Fagin.

Fagin is not rejecting the project. In fact, he is impressed by it.

But he asks:

If Jesus' earthly consciousness and activity are entirely human, how does his divine identity actually operate?

And:

How does Jesus' divinity specifically contribute to salvation?

That is a much more serious objection than simply saying "I do not like Christology from below."

Group 3 - "The methodology itself needs examination"
This is where John Galvin's later work becomes important.

Galvin is not simply asking whether Helminiak's conclusion is orthodox.

He is asking whether the shift from "humanity of Christ" to "historical Jesus" changes the theological problem itself.

That makes the debate much deeper.

One fascinating piece of evidence from Helminiak himself
Helminiak's own description of the book says that his goal was to reconcile:

methodology -> biblical scholarship -> conciliar teaching -> speculative Christology.

He explicitly says the book is intended to be solidly traditional while challenging contemporary opinion. He also identifies his use of Bernard Lonergan's epistemology as the book's integrating principle.

Helminiak later published an article called "Jesus' Humanity and Human Salvation" in Worship 63 (1989), pp. 429-446. Galvin explicitly cites this article in his 1994 discussion.

That article is potentially the missing link in understanding the Fagin criticism, because it appears to be where Helminiak develops the relationship between Jesus' humanity and salvation more explicitly.

Overall assessment of the reception
If you put the reviews together, I would summarize the scholarly reception like this:

Most reviewers thought Helminiak had produced an unusually ambitious and valuable attempt to reconcile modern historical-critical Christology with traditional Catholic belief.

The major unresolved question was not whether he affirmed Jesus' divinity, but whether his account gave Jesus' divinity enough explanatory and salvific significance while insisting so strongly on Jesus' complete humanity.

That is a much more nuanced issue than "Helminiak believed Jesus was only human."

He did not.

The real question is what it means for the eternal divine Son to be fully human, genuinely live a human life, and yet remain God - and how that divine identity actually matters for salvation.

That, I think, is the central theological fault line running through the reviews.
