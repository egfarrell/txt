Divine Agent
    - Cambridge Companion to Jesus
        > Discussion of Jesus’s self-conception has been much
        > affected by theo- logical – as well as anti-theological
        > – interests. Many have desired to bring his ideas as
        > close to later creedal orthodoxy as possible. Others
        > have wished to do the opposite. The truth seems to be
        > that Jesus had an exalted self-perception, which is
        > best understood not in Arian or Athanasian terms but
        > via comparison with divine agents in Second Temple
        > Jewish texts, such as Melchizedek in 11QMelchizedek and
        > the Son of Man in 1 Enoch 37–71.

    - ## What “divine agents” means in Second Temple Judaism
        - Second Temple Jewish thought was strongly monotheistic: there is one God, the God of Israel. But that didn’t mean God was imagined as acting alone.
        - Instead, God often rules and acts through **authorized heavenly agents**. These figures:
            - act on God’s behalf
            - bear God’s authority and sometimes God’s name
            - may sit on a throne, judge, or rule
            - can receive reverence without being identified as God himself
        - They are **not independent gods**, but neither are they mere humans.
        - Think of this as a spectrum rather than a binary: human ← exalted agent ← God.

    - ## Typical features of divine agents
        - Across Second Temple texts, divine agents often share these traits:

            1. Delegated authority
            - They do what only God normally does: judge,
              forgive, rule, defeat evil.

            2. Heavenly status
            - They are located in heaven, pre-exist the present
              age, or are enthroned.

            3. Mediating role
            - They stand between God and humanity, revealing
              God’s will or executing it.

            4. Scriptural identity
            - They are often identified by interpreting older
              biblical texts (especially Psalms and Daniel) in
              new ways.

            5. Worship-adjacent reverence
            - People may bow, praise, or invoke them, but the
              ultimate source of power remains God.

        - ### Melchizedek in 11QMelchizedek
        - In this Qumran text, Melchizedek is far more than the brief priest-king of Genesis.
        - He is portrayed as:
            - a heavenly deliverer at the end of time
            - the one who proclaims liberty (jubilee)
            - a judge over Belial and the spirits of darkness
            - possibly identified with “elohim” in Psalm 82
        - Yet God is still above him. Melchizedek acts as God’s chief agent in the final judgment.
        - This shows how elastic Jewish categories could be: an ancient human figure can be reimagined as a cosmic, almost angelic ruler.

        - ### The Son of Man in 1 Enoch 37–71
        - Here the “Son of Man” (also called the Chosen One or Righteous One) is:
            - pre-existent
            - hidden with God before creation
            - enthroned beside God
            - judge of kings and nations
            - ruler of the coming age
        - He receives glory and authority that look shockingly close to God’s own.
        - But again: he is distinguished from God. God grants him authority; he executes it.
        - This figure grows out of Daniel 7 but becomes vastly more developed.

        - ## Why this matters for Jesus
            - The quotation you gave is pushing back against a false choice:
            - Either Jesus thought he was “just a human teacher”
            - or Jesus thought he was “God in later creedal terms”

            - Second Temple divine-agent traditions open a **third option**.
            - Within this Jewish conceptual world, Jesus could plausibly see himself as:
                - God’s uniquely authorized representative
                - the coming Son of Man
                - the one through whom God judges and restores Israel
                - exalted above angels, yet obedient to God
        - That would be an **extremely high self-conception**, without being Nicene Trinitarian theology avant la lettre.
        - In other words: Jesus doesn’t need to think in Arian or Athanasian categories, because those categories didn’t exist yet.

        - ## The big takeaway
        - Second Temple Judaism allowed for figures who were:
            - distinct from God
            - yet sharing God’s authority, throne, and functions

        - “Divine agent” language helps modern readers take
          Jewish monotheism seriously while also explaining how
          early followers could speak of Jesus in strikingly
          exalted ways without thinking they had abandoned it.

### Jeshua the Man. Waiting, listening
    - This is brilliant:
    - $MY_NOTES/practical/Spirit/Jeshua-Jesus/von-Speyr-Jeshua.txt
    - Von Speyr's intuition: She frequently portrays Jesus as
      genuinely asking, waiting, and listening in prayer.
    - This is especially strong in:
        - his `nocturnal` prayer
        - his `retreats` into solitude
        - moments of `silence` before decisive action
    - Jesus’ prayer is not theatrical or pedagogical first; it is
      `necessary`.
    - Also confidence!
        - Von Speyr stresses that Jesus’ filial relation is
          marked by `confidence`, not constant consolation.
        - This allows her to say things like:
            - Jesus believes in the Father
            - Jesus entrusts himself to the Father
            - Jesus lives from promise rather than visible
              fulfillment
    - And lots more, especially:
        - One of her most striking emphases is how Jesus
          encounters human suffering.
        - For von Speyr:
            - Jesus does not merely see suffering; he enters it.
            - He does not remain at a safe distance, even
              emotionally.
            - Compassion means inward participation.
        - Examples she dwells on:
            - encounters with the sick: Jesus interiorly takes on
              their condition
            - exorcisms: Jesus allows the darkness he confronts
              to touch him
            - sinners: Jesus exposes himself to contamination,
              misunderstanding, and rejection
        - This is not sin, but `vulnerability`.
        - She often describes Jesus as allowing the weight of
          others’ situations to _impress_ themselves upon his
          soul, forming an `interior burden` that accumulates
          over time.

### Temptation
    - Jesus The Holy Fool -- Elizabeth- Anne Stewart
    - While the earlier mockeries were directed at his person and
      attacked his prophetic gifts and his perceived claims to
      “kingship,” the mockery of the crucified Christ struck at
      his relationship with God.
    - Just as Jesus _was tempted_ to base his messiahship on
      power, wonder-working and self-aggrandizement, so, on the
      cross, he was tempted to “do the spectacular” and save
      himself (Mt 4:1-11).
    - Even as he hung in agony, then, Jesus was _tempted_ to
      abandon his trust in God and to trust in his own powers
      instead.
    - While he had taught his disciples to be willing to take up
      their crosses and follow him, those mocking him suggested
      he should exempt himself from his own teachings. The
      mockeries tempted Jesus to see himself as one forsaken by
      God.

### The Same Jesus: A Contemporary Christology
    - by Daniel A. Helminiak (Author)
    - https://www.amazon.com/Same-Jesus-Contemporary-Christology/dp/0829405216
    - interesting: "contingencies" (see page 245)
    - interesting: human fidelity see page 167
    - In the third part, "A Contemporary Christology," an
      understanding of Jesus is constructed which entails
      a confluence of two approaches:
        - incarnational and resurrectional;
        - the movement of God to humanity and the movement of
          a human to God.
    - The ideal fulfillment of these movements occurs in Jesus'
      resurrection.
    - Then, for the first time in human history `a human being
      was divinized`.
        - In the resurrection Jesus' humanity was transformed so
          that it could share in divine qualities; his human mind
          and heart shared in ideal measure the knowledge and
          love of God.
    - The final chapter deals with the _soteriology_ integral to
      this position by treating four issues:
        1. Jesus' role in salvation;
        2. `contingencies` within the plan of salvation;
        3. what Jesus revealed to us;
        4. ... the salvation of non-Christians.
    - For the redemptive work of Jesus, both reconciliation and
      divinization are seen to be complementary aspects.
        - Through his _fidelity_ unto death Jesus reversed the
          themes of the human story; thus reconciling us with the
          Father. And the divinization of Jesus introduced a new
          possibility for all humankind because of the solidarity
          of the human race.
    - With respect to the contingency of salvation, the author
      argues that being human does not automatically or
      necessarily imply divinization.
        - Instead, such historical events as the Incarnation and
          the giving of the Holy Spirit must intervene before it
          becomes a concrete possibility.
    - Turning to the role of Jesus as revealer, Helminiak
      presents the claim that in Jesus we are provided with the
      prototype of true child of God.
        - Moreover, Jesus is the definitive manifestation of
          God's saving plan insofar as he is the anticipatory
          culmination of that plan within history.
        - Finally, the particular scandal of Jesus' unique
          divinity is affirmed in relation to non-Christian
          religions.
        - This christology requires the conclusion that Jesus'
          redemption affects every human being, whether each
          knows it or not. Through explicit knowledge and
          deliberate choice, however, Christians have the
          possibility to live the same saving reality that all
          live but to a degree of perfection not possible to
          others

### The fool
    - $MY_BOOKS/Spirit/Jesus-the-fool-Michael-Frost.pdf
    - If Jesus was always completely certain of the outcome of
      his life—if he knew everything that was to happen before it
      happened and if he had no doubt about his final
      vindication—then where is there room for faith?
        - How can we talk about the faith of Jesus if he was
          always aware of the events of every next moment?
        - If he was completely and thoroughly sure, without the
          shadow of any doubt, that after his trial, humiliation,
          torture, and death, he would be resurrected to the
          right hand of God and all power both in heaven and on
          earth was to be given to him, there would be no need
          for him to exercise faith.
        - And Gethsemane would not make sense. The events in that
          garden can only be sensible if there was an element of
          risk involved. Faith is risk.
            - And at Gethsemane, Jesus showed he was faced with
              a terrible risk— the offering of his own life.
    - The fact that he was prepared to, in spite of his fears, go
      through with his commitment to be sacrificed for our
      inadequacy makes the cross the supreme symbol of faith.
        - Not only do we place our faith in it, but we celebrate
          Jesus’ faith that drove him to it.
        - Gethsemane is proof that Jesus was undoubtedly foolish.
          Here he was, an innocent man, prepared to stick to his
          decision to go to the cross out of loyalty to his God
          and to this world.
        - His dogged, relentless loyalty was shaken in that small
          garden, but it didn’t fall.

### Son of God
    - In Christian theology, the phrase "Son of God" holds
      significant theological meaning, particularly within the
      context of the doctrine of the Trinity.
        - It refers to the belief that Jesus Christ, the second
          person of the Holy Trinity, is both fully God and also
          distinct from God the Father.
    - The doctrine of the Trinity teaches that there is one God
      who exists in three persons: God the Father, God the Son
      (Jesus Christ), and God the Holy Spirit. Each person is
      fully and equally God, yet they are distinct in their roles
      and relationships within the Godhead.
    - When Christians refer to Jesus as the "Son of God," they
      are affirming his divinity and his unique relationship with
      God the Father. This title emphasizes Jesus' eternal
      existence as the second person of the Trinity, begotten by
      the Father before all time.
        - It doesn't mean that Jesus is a literal biological son
          in the way humans understand parent-child
          relationships; rather, it speaks to their inseparable
          divine nature.
    - The term "Son of God" also carries implications about
      Jesus' role in salvation. It reflects his incarnation—his
      taking on human flesh—and his mission to reconcile humanity
      with God through his life, death, and resurrection.
        - This understanding is central to Christian belief in
          Jesus as both fully God and fully human, known as the
          doctrine of the Incarnation.
    - In summary, within Christian theology, the phrase "Son of
      God" signifies Jesus' divinity, his unique relationship
      with God the Father, his role in the Trinity, and his
      mission as the incarnate Savior.
        - It doesn't imply that Jesus is a separate God from the
          Father, but rather that he is one with the Father and
          the Holy Spirit in the unity of the Godhead.


### Jeshua as a "fire wire"
    - https://baptistnews.com/article/jesus-the-man-of-violence/
    - (I remember reading Marks Gospel in the Aberdeen church,
      and there was something about Jeshua that day, he was not
      a violent man but I remember thinking there was some sort
      of  "fire" in him... but a pure, brave fire, not
      passive-aggressive... with a bit of frustration too, like
      he was short of time)
    - Not all violence is the same. The violence employed by the
      marginalized to overcome oppression, is in reality
      self-defense to the oppressor’s institutionalized violence
      designed to sustain and maintain subjugation.
        - Unconditional love for the very least might lead to the
          unselfish act of standing in solidarity with the
          oppressed in their battle for self-preservation.
        - Protecting a “nonperson” might invite a violent
          confrontation as the oppressor uses all police and
          military tools at their disposal to maintain the status
          quo.
    - To make a preferential option of love for the oppressed
      means harming the oppressor who has a vested interest on
      insisting solely upon the use of nonviolence as the only
      ethically acceptable methodology employed by those s/he
      oppresses.
    - The conflict and disruption that comes with following
      Jesus, whose consequence at times is violence, illustrates
      the need for an ethical praxis for the disenfranchised
      lacking the physical or military power to confront or
      overcome oppressors.
    - Because the usage of violence all too often becomes the
      oppressor’s excuse to unleash greater violent retaliation;
      a need to be as wise as serpents but gentle as doves is
      required. How does one create ethical acts that disrupt
      structures that support and maintain oppression?

    - In several of my books I have suggested the need to
      "joder". To joder is a Spanish verb, a word one would never
      use in polite conversation, basically meaning “to screw
      with.”
    - An ethics 'para joder' is an ethics that “screws” with the
      prevailing power structures. Those standing before the
      vastness of domination have few ethical alternatives. When
      those who are disenfranchised start to joder, they play the
      trickster role, literally creating instability, upsetting
      the prevailing social order designed to protect the power
      and privilege of the few.

    - To joder refuses to play by the rules established by those
      who provide a space for orderly dissent, pacifying the need
      to vent, but designed not to change the power relationships
      within the existing social structures.
    - If the goal of praxis is to bring about change, then it is
      crucial to go beyond the rules created by the dominant
      culture, to move beyond what is expected, to push beyond
      their universalized experiences. This is an ethics that
      refuses to go to the police department, to get permit from
      the police department, providing permission from the police
      department, to protest the police brutality of the police
      department.
    - When Jesus overturned those tables in the Temple that day,
      he was, I argue, participating in an ethics 'para joder'.
