- In an interview with Diario Vasco (October 16, 2007), JA Pagola
  stated that he is interested in Jesus because he is
  a compassionate man who reaches out to the marginalized and
  seeks the dignity of women. "The most important features of his
  profile portray a compassionate man, a defender of the
  marginalized, who was primarily concerned with people's health
  (some say he was a religious therapist), and who, in contrast
  to a legalistic view, introduces compassion as a guiding
  principle."

- This is the quest Pagola undertakes for Jesus. In truth, it's
  an ambitious work, demonstrating a profound understanding of
  the cultural, economic, and religious environment of Jesus's
  time. The author's immense erudition in this regard cannot be
  denied. His language is direct and evocative. His method leads
  him to reconstruct the experience of the world in which Jesus
  lived and to communicate to us the very experience that Jesus
  lived. 
- Jesus was an itinerant prophet who attracted people through the
  force of his personality and the originality of his message.
  And so, Pagola seeks to recapture Jesus's personal appeal. 
- In the same interview mentioned, he says that "preaching that
  emphasizes doctrine in a cold manner and confines Jesus to
  a very sublime but very abstract doctrine prevents us from
  reaching the concrete Jesus. Jesus can be `greatly deified`,
  but then he remains very `distant` from us."

- And this search for the real Jesus, the only one that interests
  him, will lead him to confess that "at no time did Jesus make
  any claim to be God: neither Jesus nor his followers in his
  lifetime used the title of 'Son of God' to confess his divine
  condition" (379).

- Therefore, we will continue Pagola's quest by asking ourselves
  what he thinks of Jesus: is he an itinerant prophet who speaks
  to us of God as Father, or the Son of God in person? And we
  will do so by delving into the crucial themes of his theology
  and engaging in dialogue with him.

### 1. The baptism of Jesus

- When Jesus left his home in Nazareth, he went to meet John the
  Baptist, who had begun a movement of conversion and repentance
  in the desert. The entire nation was called to repentance. 
- The Baptist, with his understanding of God as judge, sought to
  convert his people from sin and rebellion against God, urging
  them to return to the Covenant. 
- And in this atmosphere, they awaited a figure who was to come
  and baptize with fire (Mark 1:7). Jesus went there and was
  baptized by the Baptist. But it was at that moment that he
  experienced a complete transformation in his life; it was there
  that he had the experience of God that would mark his
  preaching. 
- He experienced the definitive irruption of God into history;
  not the God of judgment, but the God of salvation. 
    - God comes as Father to give a dignified life to all people.
      This is the Kingdom of God that has arrived.

- Mark's text speaks of that strange experience that Jesus had:
    - the heavens opened and he saw the Spirit of God descending
      on him "like a dove" and he heard a voice saying from
      heaven: "You are my beloved Son" (Mk 1:9-10).

- Pagola says that we undoubtedly find literary elements in the
  narration of this scene in this text (305). Indeed, reading the
  text, we find certain literary elements. The opening of the
  heavens seems to be inspired by Isaiah 64:1: the God of heaven
  is asked to rend the heavens and come down. The dove, for its
  part, reminds us of the Spirit that hovered over the waters of
  the first creation (Genesis 1:2), appearing here in the prelude
  to the new creation. These elements can undoubtedly be
  literary. But Pagola reduces everything to an "experience,"
  forgetting that a `Theophany` takes place here, proclaiming the
  identity of Jesus and his mission. 
    - The historical core is the voice of the Father ( bat quol
      : the echo of the voice), which, in rabbinic literature,
      was considered the formula for the manifestation of the
      Divine Will in times when God no longer sent prophets.

- Perhaps this voice is the core historical element of the
  Theophany if we consider, by analogy, that in another Theophany
  (that of the Transfiguration) there are witnesses to the same
  voice of the Father. Peter recalls that "we ourselves heard the
  voice that came from heaven, when we were with him (Jesus) on
  the holy mountain" (2 Peter 1:16-18).

- Jesus' condition as a servant who bears the sins of humanity is
  also evident in the Theophany: "You are my beloved Son, in whom
  I am well pleased" (Mt 1:11), a faithful echo of the words
  about the Servant of Yahweh: "Here is my servant... my chosen
  one, in whom I am well pleased; I have put my Spirit on him."
  Now, therefore, the Spirit descends upon Christ, sending him on
  his mission of redemption. 
- The scene of Christ in solidarity with the sinners about to be
  baptized evokes the image of the Servant of Yahweh, who,
  innocent, has borne our sins and who, through his suffering,
  will obtain forgiveness for many (all) (Is 53:4-11). 
- John develops this interpretation further when he presents
  Christ as "the Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world"
  (Jn 1:29, 36).

- This scene, taken as a whole, presents Jesus' identity as the
  Son and his mission of redemption. The words of the Theophany
  present Jesus as the true Servant sent by God. 
- Here begins the prophetic mystery of Jesus in the tradition of
  the Servant of Yahweh, but God does not call any prophet
  "beloved Son." In every purely prophetic mission, God appears
  sending: "I am sending you," but not proclaiming the identity
  of the one sent in these terms: "You are my beloved Son."

- Here, the text speaks of the Son and the beloved Son, which has
  a transcendent meaning as the only Son, considering that Mark
  himself speaks of the "beloved" Son whom the Father sends into
  his vineyard (Mark 12:6), the only Son because he is the sole
  heir. 
- Furthermore, the term "beloved" ( agapetos ) appears seven
  times in the Septuagint translation with the meaning of only
  Son (Genesis 22:2, 12, 16; Jeremiah 6:26; Amos 5:10; Zechariah
  12:10).

- The scene thus proclaims the identity of Jesus and manifests
  his consecration by the Spirit and his redemptive mission in
  the line of the Servant. Thus begins the prophetic ministry of
  Jesus.

- However, in this first scene that Pagola discusses, everything
  is reduced to an "experience." This is his method, which will
  always reduce everything transcendent to a purely inner
  experience based on an interpretation of Scripture that is
  inherently biased and tendentious.
        - (tendentious: expressing or intending to promote
          a particular cause or point of view, especially
          a controversial one)

### 2. The arrival of the Kingdom

- No one today disputes that Jesus Christ preached the arrival of
  the Kingdom of God as his central theme. He did so in the
  countryside and in the synagogues. "The Kingdom of God has come
  near, repent" (Mark 1:15).

- In the Jewish world, a Kingdom was expected whose purpose would
  be the subjugation of all peoples to the will of Yahweh (the
  reign of God), and at the same time, the triumph of Israel. 
- But here, the Kingdom does not appear in a spectacular way.

  Jesus is aware that the event prepared by God in the history of
  Israel has arrived: "the time has come." He said this in his
  hometown, commenting on Isaiah 61:1-2, a text that spoke of the
  coming of the Kingdom. And he noted: "Today this Scripture
  passage that you have just heard has been fulfilled" (Luke
  4:21). 
- But Jesus is aware that the Kingdom has come with him. The
  Kingdom of God is personally identified with Jesus himself.
  There is a constant equivalence between giving everything for
  Christ or for the sake of the Kingdom, between following Christ
  or accepting the Kingdom (Luke 18:29; Matthew 19:29; Mark
  10:29). With his arrival, preaching, and miracles, the Kingdom
  has definitively arrived: “Tell John: The blind receive their
  sight, the lame walk, lepers are cleansed, the dead are raised,
  and the good news is proclaimed to the poor” (Lk 7:22-23; Mt
  11:5). 
- There is an idea in Origen that expresses this precisely:
  Christ is the `autobasileia` , that is, he himself is the
  Kingdom in person. Whoever welcomes him, whoever converts to
  him, has received the Kingdom.

- Christ himself is salvation. The Kingdom is manifested in his
  preaching and miracles. And it implies a new notion of God: God
  is Father. 
- This stands in stark contrast to the Pharisees' idea that they
  achieved righteousness (salvation-holiness) through the exact
  observance of the law, excluding from salvation those who did
  not follow it as they did—sinners, tax collectors, and
  prostitutes. 
- Christ comes, and in the parable of the prodigal son, he speaks
  to us of the Father who delights in forgiving and who
  scandalizes the older brother, who represents the Pharisee. God
  loves people regardless of their merits because he is a God who
  delights in forgiving: "There will be more joy in heaven over
  one sinner who repents than over ninety-nine who do not need to
  repent" (Luke 15:7). This is the Father of Christ. 
- Now, the punishment of hell is for those who despise the
  Father's love by refusing conversion and the grace offered to
  them (Mt 11:20-29), because those who stubbornly refuse to
  believe, those who mock God's merciful love, will die in their
  sin (Jn 8:12, 21-24). 
    - Those who obstinately close themselves off to God's
      merciful invitation (Jn 3:16-21; 5:24) and refuse to change
      their lives are condemned.

- But the Pharisee's logic has been shattered. The Father loves
  regardless of one's merits. Even tax collectors are saved (the
  Pharisees said that not even God himself could save them). God
  delights in forgiving. 
- In the parable of the Pharisee and the tax collector, the tax
  collector could not present merits like the Pharisee, but he
  asks for forgiveness (Luke 18:9-14) and therefore left the
  temple justified. I believe it should be said, consequently,
  that the first commandment is to allow oneself to be loved by
  God. One enters the Kingdom, therefore, through conversion and
  faith.

- And the Kingdom has two dimensions (like grace): on the one
  hand, it makes us children in Christ, and on the other, it
  frees us from sin, suffering, and death. 
- Logically, the Kingdom cannot be limited to the inner dimension
  of grace; rather, by its very nature, it must eliminate
  injustice and be concerned with the social well-being of
  humanity.

- For Pagola, the Kingdom is reduced exclusively to the lowest
  dimension. Pagola rebels against those who make the Kingdom of
  God something private and spiritual, occurring only in the
  depths of a person's being when they open themselves to God's
  love (95).
- No, the Kingdom is a liberating force that seeks to heal
  suffering, illness, and poverty. The enemy to be combated is
  the evil that reigns in the world. 
- Jesus proclaims God's salvation through healing. 
- God is a friend of life and wants to create a healthier
  society: to heal, to liberate from evil, to lift people out of
  despair, to heal religion. That is the Kingdom (101). 
- God comes to eliminate misery, so that people may recover their
  dignity. God does not tolerate the suffering of the poor. And
  things have to change.

- As we see, Pagola reduces the Kingdom to its social dimension
  (which it has), but he forgets that when St. Paul says that
  even if I give all my possessions to others, if I do not have
  love, it profits me nothing (1 Corinthians 13:3), it means that
  if one is concerned with healing the ills of society and lives
  in sin, one does not belong to the Kingdom.

- Pagola forgets that the Kingdom is identified with the person
  of Christ, because to admit this would be to confess Christ's
  divinity. He also forgets that the Kingdom is born within us
  through conversion to the person of Christ. He says that the
  Kingdom is not brought about by explicit adherence to Jesus but
  by helping those in need (193), so he doesn't speak of the
  adoptive sonship that the Spirit produces in us, which makes us
  exclaim, "Abba, Father!" (Rom 8:15). Christ gave his life so
  that we might receive adoptive sonship (Gal 4:5). But how can
  Christ divinize us if he is not God? Pagola consequently
  forgets the supernatural dimension of the Kingdom. When
  speaking of the Kingdom, he never speaks of grace. That the
  Kingdom must change society is something no one can doubt, but
  that the Kingdom can be reduced to that is something no one can
  accept. It would be a betrayal of the essence of Christianity.
  To bring about a revolution that seeks the dignity of
  humankind, it is not necessary to be a Christian; the
  principles of the Enlightenment suffice.

3. God's forgiveness

Pagola goes on to explain that God is good, that his goodness
fills everything, that his mercy has already broken into life.
But when meditating on the parable of the prodigal son (127 ff.),
he distorts it by forgetting that the son returns repentant:
“Father, I have sinned against heaven and against you. I am no
longer worthy to be called your son. Treat me like one of your
hired servants” (Lk 15:21). And Pagola says that the father
interrupted his son’s confession (130) when in reality the son
had already made that confession of repentance while he was still
far from home. In the parable there is conversion. God does
forgive, but a son who has returned repentant. The Gospel is
distorted when it is said that God forgives without conversion;
It is another thing to say that the Father delights in forgiving:
“There is more joy in heaven over one sinner who repents than
over ninety-nine who do not need to repent” (Lk 15:7). There is
also the parable of the Pharisee and the tax collector. The tax
collector was justified because he asked for forgiveness.

Pagola reminds us that God welcomes tax collectors and sinners
unconditionally (199). Jesus shares a meal with them, and they
feel welcomed by God, and thus a sense of their own dignity
awakens within them. God is a friend who offers his friendship,
and thus, little by little, the sinner's sense of dignity
awakens. Sinners can open themselves to God's forgiveness and
change, but no declaration is made, they are not absolved of
their sins; he simply welcomes them as a friend. Jesus teaches
that God comes to the sinner not as a judge who pronounces
sentence, but as a father who seeks to win back his lost
children. In the Old Testament, those who have previously
repented are forgiven; Jesus does not demand prior repentance.
Jesus welcomes sinners as they are: sinners. It is a forgiveness
not conditioned on repentance.

“This forgiveness that Jesus offers is unconditional. His
therapeutic approach does not follow the paths of the law:
defining guilt, calling for repentance, achieving change, and
offering forgiveness conditioned on a subsequent positive
response. Jesus follows the paths of the Kingdom: he offers
welcome and friendship, bestows God’s forgiveness, and trusts in
His mercy, which will know how to recover His lost sons and
daughters. He draws near, welcomes them, and begins with them
a journey to God that is sustained only by His infinite
compassion. No one on this earth has performed a sign more laden
with hope, a more freely given and more absolute sign of God’s
forgiveness.”

Jesus places everyone, sinners and the righteous, before the
unfathomable abyss of God's forgiveness. There are no longer
righteous people with rights versus sinners without rights. From
the compassion of God, Jesus presents everything differently: the
Kingdom of God is offered to all; only those who do not take
refuge in his mercy are excluded" (208).

If I understand correctly, Pagola means that God forgives
unconditionally, without the obligation of a subsequent positive
response. The Kingdom is offered to everyone. Only those who do
not accept His mercy are condemned. Therefore, one can accept His
mercy without a commitment to change. But what kind of repentance
is that? How can one accept God's mercy without repenting and
resolving to change one's life? Is there a certain Lutheran
flavor here? The prodigal son will not repeat his past actions.
Only then can the father celebrate. Otherwise, it would be
self-deception.

It is true that Jesus eats with sinners and brings them the
message that God the Father heals them. But it is also true that
Jesus forgives the adulteress and tells her, “Go and sin no more”
(Jn 8:11). He forgives the good thief because he has asked for
forgiveness and tells him, “Today you will be with me in
paradise” (Lk 23:19). But he doesn't say this to the other thief
who doesn't ask for forgiveness. Pagola always downplays the
existence of hell and thus forgets the parable in which one of
the last guests was thrown outside into darkness because he
wasn't wearing the wedding garment (grace) (Mt 23:13). And we
cannot forget that Jesus appears in the Gospels as a judge.
Speaking of the last hour, Jesus says: “The hour has come when
all who are in the tombs will hear his voice and rise—those who
have done good to live, and those who have done evil to be
condemned” (John 5:28-29). Then, Christ will come “in his glory,
and all the angels with him… All the nations will be gathered
before him, and he will separate the people one from another as
a shepherd separates the sheep from the goats. He will put the
sheep on his right and the goats on his left… Then they will go
away to eternal punishment, but the righteous to eternal life”
(Matthew 25:31-32, 46).

Finally, Pagola offers a commentary on an important text in which
Jesus forgives the sins of a paralytic (Mark 2:5). He states that
Jesus here grants forgiveness and absolution in the name of God,
thus appearing as judge. However, Pagola adds that this was not
the welcoming attitude Jesus had toward sinners (206), concluding
that the historicity of this account cannot be guaranteed. The
truth is that the text says even more, something Pagola omits.
Jesus forgives the paralytic in his own name, not in the name of
God, which implies his divinity, since only God can forgive sins.
Therein lies the divinity of Christ. When a text clearly speaks
of his divinity, Pagola responds by saying that it is probably
not authentic. But the fact remains that this account, in which
Jesus is accused of blasphemy, could not have been invented by
the early Christian community (criterion of discontinuity).

4. The miracles of Christ

Pagola never uses the term "miracles" when discussing Jesus'
healings. Already in his first work on Christology ( Jesus of
Nazareth , San Sebastián 1981), he maintained that miracles of
nature (multiplication of the loaves, walking on water, etc.) had
little guarantee of historicity (274-275). Indeed, he once again
excludes everything that does not fit his preconceived view of
Christ. In this work, he completely omits these miracles.

He doesn't speak of miracles; he prefers to speak of healings.
What concerns God is the suffering of the people, and so Jesus
proclaims the Kingdom of God by healing. Furthermore, illness
meant exclusion from society, as in the case of lepers. It was
considered a punishment from God for sin or infidelity. Christ
shatters (and in this, Pagola is right) all taboos.

So, what do his healings consist of? Christ, through them, wants
to show the Father's compassionate love. Other prophets, such as
Elisha and Elijah, had also performed healings, and Jesus does so
as a sign of the coming of the Kingdom of God. In reality, what
Christ does is heal by the power of his word and the gestures of
his hands: he touches and transmits confidence (166), and thus
Christ awakens trust in God, rescues the sick from isolation and
despair, and it is this trust in God that Jesus transmits that
heals (167). "His power to awaken unknown energies in the human
being created the conditions that made recovery of health
possible" (165). Faith, therefore, belongs to the same healing
process. When trust is awakened in a sick person, conversion
takes place. It is faith that awakens unknown possibilities.
Jesus worked in the hearts of the sick so that they would trust
in God (167).

Jesus also performs exorcisms. Those people believed in demonic
possession, but “possession was a complex strategy used in
a morbid way by oppressed people to defend themselves against an
unbearable situation” (170). It was a morbid way of rebelling
against Roman subjugation and the rule of the powerful (170). And
logically, the Kingdom of God must heal the evil that manifests
itself in this world.

Miracles, in any case, are not proof of God's power.

Well then, if Pagola will allow me, I will turn to my Bible (I've
long thought I possess a different Bible) in which Jesus says:
“If you do not believe me because of what I say, believe at least
because of the works I do, and you will know that I am in the
Father and the Father is in me” (John 10:37-38). “If I had not
done the works that no one else did, they would not have sin; but
now they have seen them and hate both me and my Father” (John
15:24). And Nicodemus says to Jesus: “Rabbi, we know that you are
from God, for no one can do the works you do” (John 3:2). Therein
lies the apologetic sense of miracles, as it does in the common
sense of the man born blind: “It has never been heard that anyone
has given sight to a man born blind; Therefore, the one who
healed me is from God” (Jn 9, 32-33).

Personally, I have never found a reason to doubt the historicity
and apologetic value of miracles; what I have found are
prejudices that ultimately come from Protestantism, which does
not know how to integrate reason within the framework of faith.

Moreover, Pagola's explanation is ridiculous. How could he
inspire confidence in the Canaanite woman's daughter, whom he
didn't see and who was many miles away? Or how could he resurrect
Jairus's daughter or Lazarus, who had been dead for four days and
smelled, inspiring them to trust him?

But in any case, what cannot be asserted is what Pagola claims
when he states that Jesus did not travel the roads of Galilee to
convert sinners, but to heal people by freeing them from their
suffering (174-175). Jesus seeks conversion precisely through his
miracles: “Woe to you, Chorazin! Woe to you, Bethsaida! For if
the miracles I performed in your sight had been performed in
Sodom and Gomorrah, they would have repented long ago” (Mt
11:23). The salvific and apologetic dimensions are always united
in the miracles of Christ.

5. The identity of Christ

At the outset, we quoted Pagola, who stated that Jesus never
claimed to be God. Indeed, for him, Jesus is a man who had
a unique experience of God as Father. God is at the center of his
life (303), and thus Pagola titles chapter eleven "Faithful
Believer." The God of Jesus Christ is the God of Israel, whom he
has now discovered as a compassionate Father through the
experience of baptism. He calls him Abba (Father). He prays the
Shema twice a day, as every Jew did. But the designation as
Father, which existed in the Old Testament with respect to Israel
and the king, was not central. Now Christ has discovered the
Father in his goodness. He is good to everyone and forgives
everyone. This is the Kingdom of God. "The better people live,
the better the Kingdom of God is realized" (324). And no one is
excluded from the Kingdom.

Today, there is much talk about the faith of Christ. The fact is,
when one searches the Bible, one will not find a single passage
that says Christ believed in God. The perspective of the Gospel
of John is this: only Christ sees the Father and bears witness to
what he sees (John 1:18; 6:46). There are numerous passages in
which Christ says, as in John 3:11: “We speak of what we know,
and bear witness to what we have seen, but you do not accept our
testimony.”

But it is also the case that, at the same time, there are
countless texts in which Christ asks for the same faith for
himself as for the Father: “You believe in God; believe also in
me” (John 14:1). How can someone who asks for divine faith in
himself have faith? Here again is the divinity of Jesus Christ.

It is futile to turn to Hebrews 12:2, which says that Christ "is
the author and perfecter of faith." Father Iglesias, in his New
Testament , reminds us that Christ is the initiator and perfecter
of our faith because, from beginning to end, our faith depends on
him—an idea repeated throughout the letter. The proof that Christ
does not have faith in this letter is that its author, in seeking
examples of faith in Abraham, Moses, and others, does not present
Christ as a model of faith. In the New Testament, the model of
faith is Mary, not Christ.

Pagola does not use a method that has proven highly effective in
studying the divinity of Christ: implicit Christology. Christ
implicitly presents himself as God constantly. He places himself
at the center of faith and salvation in sayings such as: “Whoever
seeks their life will lose it, but whoever loses their life for
me will find it” (Mt 10:39). “You will be hated by all because of
me, but the one who stands firm to the end will be saved” (Mt
10:18-22). Guardini, in * The Essence of Christianity* (Madrid
1984), offers a profound reflection on all these sayings,
highlighting that Jesus Christ does what no other founder of
religion dared to do: place himself at the center of religious
life and ask for the same faith that only God can ask for. In his
recent book, Jesus of Nazareth , J. Ratzinger recounts the story
of Rabbi J. Neusner, who tells another rabbi that Jesus upholds
the law, that he has not removed any precept from it, but that he
has placed himself at its center, above the law. Jesus, he says,
has demands of me that only God can make. This is what prevents
me from being a Christian.

Jesus identifies with the Kingdom, as we have already seen:
salvation is found in his person. And if he systematically places
himself above the law, the Sabbath, and the temple, it is because
he is conscious of being God. He even claims to forgive sins in
his own name. None of this has been thoroughly analyzed by
Pagola, who even overlooks texts in which Christ is accused of
blasphemy for claiming the name of God: “I am” (Jn 8:24, 28, 58).
“If you do not believe that I am he, you will die in your sins”
(Jn 8:24). And he was accused of blasphemy. There is a passage in
the Gospel of John in which the Jews say to him: “We are not
going to stone you for any human work, but for blasphemy, because
you, a mere man, claim to be God” (Jn 10:33).

Jesus Christ reveals himself as God when he affirms that he is
the Son of Man who comes on the clouds of heaven (Mt 26:64),
adopting the vision of Daniel (7:9-14), which presents the Son of
Man as Messiah (with the mission of gathering the children of the
Most High), but as a transcendent Messiah who does not come from
the sea like the human kingdoms, but from heaven; he is
pre-existent and shares the power of the Ancient of Days (God).
This title, which Jesus uses some eighty times and which appears
in all the sources that make up the Gospels, was used exclusively
by Christ. Therefore, it is comical that Pagola, who does not
dedicate a chapter to studying this title and to it only a short
paragraph, claims that what happened is that Jesus understood
"Son of Man" in a common sense (a man) and that the Church
transformed it into a divine title in light of Daniel 7:9-14
(452-453). How could the Church do that when it never used or
understood this title? The early Church never called Jesus the
Son of Man. Even today, we do not have a single prayer in the
liturgy that addresses Christ as the Son of Man.

Jesus Christ also presented himself as the Son of God in a divine
sense. There are many texts we could present here, which we have
studied in our work Lord and Christ (Palabra, Madrid 2005).
I will limit myself to citing one. In Mark 12:1-9, we have the
parable of the tenants. In it, Jesus presents himself as the only
Son in Jerusalem, a few days before his death. Jesus Christ
proclaimed this parable to make people understand the magnitude
of the crime they were about to commit by killing him: by killing
him, they were not killing just another prophet (the servants),
but the only Son. We also see this in Matthew 23:30, where Jesus
says to the Pharisees: “You say that if you had lived in the days
of your ancestors, you would not have killed the prophets. Thus
you testify that you are descendants of those who murdered the
prophets. Fill up, then, the measure of your ancestors.”

In the Gospel of John, the title Son of God appears constantly in
a divine sense. Now, Pagola will say that the designation of God
as Father in the Old Testament was given in an adoptive sense.
And indeed, Jesus is the Son, the most beloved of God. And he
affirms that Jesus is true man and true God, because the true God
is present in him (460). If we notice, Pagola says that God is
present in Jesus, but was also present in the prophet through his
actions and his words. What Pagola does not say is that Jesus is
God, the Son of God, in a singular sense.

6. The Passion

Before discussing the Passion, Pagola explains the episode of the
cleansing of the Temple; an episode of paramount importance for
the wealthy and privileged priestly class who lived off the
Temple and did so in collusion with Rome. I find this all very
accurate. It was a challenge to the Temple aristocracy. Jesus'
actions were, moreover, a challenge to the Temple itself. And in
this respect, Pagola overlooks something of utmost importance in
the Gospel of John: that Christ foretold the destruction of the
Temple and said that he would raise it up in three days. And John
adds: "He was referring to his resurrected body" (John 2:21),
which, as we know, is present in the Eucharist. The Shekinah
Yahweh is no longer in Jerusalem; it is in every tabernacle of
our churches. And the Temple can no longer be built on the Temple
Mount because it is occupied by two mosques. Pagola also forgets
that Jesus Christ, who said he was greater than the Temple, is
the true Temple present now in the Eucharist.

But the explanation of Jesus' condemnation as simply the
cleansing of the temple is unconvincing. Even less so is Pilate's
condemnation. Pilate appears hesitant during the Passion: "Are
you a king?" he asks Jesus, who had no appearance of being one.
And Jesus replies: "Yes, but my kingdom is not of this world"
(John 18:36). Pilate didn't want to condemn him and tried to use
Barabbas as an excuse, but this didn't work out. However, the
Pharisees, who knew Pilate well, told him: "If you release this
man, you are an enemy of Caesar" (John 19:12). This struck
a nerve: his career was at stake. And Pilate condemned Christ out
of cowardice.

But the Jews brought Jesus to Pilate because “he claimed to be
the Son of God” (John 19:7). That is the reason for the Jews'
condemnation: blasphemy. And that is why Caiaphas condemned him:
“He has blasphemed,” when Jesus told him that he was the Son of
Man who would come on the cloud. Now everything was clear to
Caiaphas; he had a compelling reason to get rid of Jesus, who had
subverted the social and religious order.

And so Pagola, seeking to forget Jesus' condemnation as
a blasphemer because it would imply that he had affirmed his
divinity, explains that the Sanhedrin meeting did not take place
(377). The Mishnah did indeed prohibit Sanhedrin meetings at
night. What did occur was an informal, private meeting at the
house of Annas. Now, as Father Iglesias rightly notes ( New
Testament , 160), Matthew combines two meetings: the nighttime
meeting before Annas (Jn 18:13) and the one that took place at
dawn in the Sanhedrin (Lk 22:66). Luke specifies that they met in
the Sanhedrin "as soon as it was day." And the Jerusalem Bible
notes that it undoubtedly took place in the court building, near
the Temple.

Pagola's only remaining argument is that the combination of these
three titles—Messiah, Son of God, and Son of Man—at Jesus' trial
is not historical, but rather an expression of the Church's faith
(376). However, one would have to respond that the combination of
Messiah and Son of God in Caiaphas's mouth is logical, given that
a Jew can understand the Messiah as Son of God in an adoptive
sense. Messiah and Son of God, in this case, are synonymous. But
the title Son of Man in Jesus's mouth cannot have originated in
the early Church because it never referred to Jesus in that way.

Jesus, therefore, was condemned as a blasphemer.

In any case, Pagola goes on to say that Jesus ends up on the
cross not by the Father's will nor to perform an expiatory
sacrifice. He did not come to make amends to a God offended by
sin, but to give himself completely for the Kingdom of God (350).
Jesus died as he lived. The Father does not demand reparation.
The Father does not want his beloved Son to be killed, and what
he does is accompany him to the cross. The Father does not seek
the ignominious death of his Son, nor does Jesus offer his blood
to the Father knowing that it will be pleasing to him (440-441).
The Father and the Son at the crucifixion are united, facing evil
together to the very end, so that, in the Resurrection, God has
shown that he was with the Crucified One. This is not, therefore,
a God of justice who does not forgive unless his offended honor
is restored. There is no expiatory sacrifice. We cannot see sin
as an offense against God except in the people who are dying of
hunger, as Pagola said in the interview we have already cited.

As we see, in this way the entire mystery of Christ's redemption
disappears. Everything is explained naturally. But the fact is
that Scripture constantly tells us that it was the Father's will
that Christ go to the cross. I will cite only three texts from
the many that appear. Christ asks the Father in the garden to
take the cup of suffering from him and adds: "Yet not my will,
but yours be done" (Mt 26:39). In Jn 12:27 we read: "Father, save
me from this hour, for this is why I have come." We also read in
Phil 2:6-8 that Christ, though he was in the form of God, humbled
himself by becoming obedient to death—even death on a cross.
There are many more texts we could have cited.

Is sin not a personal offense against God? The Old Testament
already contains a term for sin: zanah (marital infidelity). It
appears in many texts, but especially in one of the most
beautiful passages of the Old Testament (Ezekiel 16:1ff.): the
story of a young woman abandoned in the countryside, naked and
repulsive, with whom a passerby (God) falls in love, dresses her
in silk and jewels, and marries her. But she, captivated by her
beauty, later turns to prostitution. The Jewish people not only
have an ethical understanding of sin, but also a religious one,
as an offense against God. Given their conception of a personal
God who has made a covenant with his people, sin is first and
foremost an offense against this God, friend and Father.

Another of the most beautiful passages in the Old Testament is
the figure of the Servant of Yahweh (Isaiah 53), which speaks of
the atonement for sins made by an innocent man, without sin, who
does not open his mouth to complain about his situation. It is
the passage that converted Rabbi E. Zolli of Rome to the
Christian faith. This figure is said to have made atonement for
the sins of the many (all). We will see later how Christ makes
this sacrifice of the Servant of Yahweh his own. For now, and as
a summary of the Church's faith on this point, we quote the New
Catechism.

The Catechism of the Church presents Christ's sacrifice on the
cross as the sacrifice of the Servant of Yahweh who "gave himself
in expiation" and by which he satisfies the Father for our sins
(n. 615). It has the value of "reparation, expiation, and
satisfaction" (n. 616). It is a sacrifice by which our
disobedience is repaired (n. 614).

In this regard, it is significant that John Paul II himself
taught that sin personally affects the Father even though it does
not destroy him in his most perfect being, so that Christ
answered for us, making amends for our disobedience. The
International Theological Commission also echoes the fact that
popular Christian piety has always rejected the idea of
​​an unfeeling God and has recognized compassion in
him. For its part, the New Catechism also speaks of sin as
a personal offense against God (nos. 1140, 1850, 431, 397),
something directed against God's love for us, a rebellion against
God, a disobedience to God, and a lack of trust in his goodness
(no. 397). A "break in communion with God" (no. 1440). Therefore,
reparation is responding to God's unrequited love.

Now, what a theologian must do is not eliminate the data of
Scripture and Tradition. That is not how Theology is done. What
a theologian must do is understand, as far as possible, the
mystery revealed in them. And in this case, it often happens that
when our people are told from a theological perspective how sin
offends God, they end up loving Him more, marveling at the
greatness of His love. A God insensitive to sin is not the
Christian God. If God is sensitive to sin, it is because He truly
loves us, because He seeks our response. Our God is not
a paternalistic God who indulges all the whims of His
grandchildren. He is the Father who suffers precisely because He
loves. We have spoken about this in our Christology ( Lord and
Christ ).

7. The Eucharist, farewell dinner

Pagola's treatment of the Eucharist is truly disappointing. He
says it was simply a farewell supper. It is a supper that brings
to mind the final banquet of the Kingdom. In it, Jesus meant to
signify that his death would not destroy anyone's death, that his
death would not prevent the coming of the Kingdom. And at the
moment of breaking the bread, what Jesus wants to convey is that
we must see him in the pieces of that bread given up to the very
end. That bread and that wine will remind them of Jesus' total
self-giving unto death and will evoke the final feast of the
Kingdom (367).

It is therefore a remembrance and an evocation. It says nothing
about its sacrificial meaning. How could it, if it doesn't admit
that Christ's death had one? Nor does it say anything about what
St. Paul affirms regarding the Real Presence: that the cup is
a participation in the blood of Christ and that the bread is
a participation in his body (1 Corinthians 10:16), to the point
of stating that whoever eats the bread or drinks the cup of the
Lord unworthily becomes guilty of profaning the body and blood of
the Lord (1 Corinthians 11:27). It also forgets Christ's words in
the Gospel of John, when he affirms that unless we eat the flesh
of the Son and drink his blood, we have no life in us (John
6:53-54).

But Pagola begins by stating that the Lord's Supper was not
a Passover meal. He cannot help but cite in a footnote the
indications in the Gospels that identify the meal with the Jewish
Passover (Mark 14:1, 12, 17-18; Luke 22:15). It is true that
there is a chronological problem, since the Synoptic Gospels
place the meal between the 14th and 15th of Nisan, at sunset
(Mark 14:12); consequently, it was a Jewish Passover meal, and
all the events of the Passion took place between the 14th and
15th. But according to the Gospel of John (John 13:1, 29; 18:28;
9:14), Jesus died on the 14th, since that day, as he notes, was
the day of preparation for Passover, when the lambs were
sacrificed in the temple. Therefore, he died on the afternoon of
Friday the 14th. Consequently, Jesus had to move the Last Supper
forward by 24 hours. We have detailed in our work, *The
Eucharistic Mystery* (Ed. Palabra), all the interpretations that
this earlier date in John's account has given rise to. John's
dating carries significant weight; but in any case, as Jeremiah
rightly says, the decisive factor is that Jesus celebrated his
meal within the context of the Jewish Passover celebration. Thus,
he says that it is mentioned that the Last Supper took place in
Jerusalem, and we know that the Passover feast, since 621 BC, had
ceased to be a domestic celebration and had become a pilgrimage
to Jerusalem. A borrowed room was used (Mark 14:13-15) according
to the Jewish custom of freely granting certain premises to
pilgrims. It took place in the evening, reclining and not sitting
(as was done at the Passover meal, as a sign of liberation). The
washing of the feet is explained by the practice required to be
able to eat the Passover meal. The fact that Jesus broke the
bread during the meal (“while they were eating”: Mark 14:18-22)
is significant, since an ordinary meal always began with the
breaking of the bread itself. The presence of wine was not
customary and was reserved for solemn occasions. The red wine was
specific to the Passover meal. The hymn that was sung (Mark
14:26; Matthew 26:30) was the Hallel .which was recited at the
Passover meal. After supper, Jesus did not return to Bethany as
on previous nights but went to the Garden of Gethsemane (it was
obligatory to spend that night in Jerusalem: Deuteronomy 16:7).
During the meal, Jesus announced his imminent Passion, and we
know that the explanation of the special elements of the meal was
an integral part of the Passover rite. We should also add the
theme of memorial ("do this in remembrance of me"), which
belonged to the atmosphere of the Passover celebration. The
Passover meal was held in remembrance of the liberation from
Egypt. And Jesus commands that his memorial (zikaron) be made.
The Jewish Passover made present the rite of liberation
accomplished by God in the Exodus (Exodus 12:1-14). Now Christ
gives us the Eucharist as a memorial that makes present the
Passover accomplished in him through his death and Resurrection.
And we cannot accept Pagola's assertion that the memorial is not
historical here, because without Jesus's command to reiterate it,
the subsequent development of the Eucharistic liturgy would have
been impossible. Why is it that everywhere, without exception,
Christians of Jewish origin cease to celebrate Passover and
instead celebrate the Eucharist? To deny the Paschal character of
the Eucharist because it doesn't mention bitter herbs, as Pagola
does, is to disregard the fact that this narrative, founded on
Jesus, has a liturgical structure within which elements that have
not acquired sacramental significance no longer fit.

Thus, the Eucharist once again becomes a memorial of Christ's
death and resurrection. Pagola also overlooks the theme of the
new covenant, which refers precisely to the old covenant made by
Moses in the Old Testament (Ex 24:1-8), established with the
blood of animals sprinkled on a central stone representing God
and twelve stones arranged in a circle, recalling the twelve
tribes of Israel.

We have already discussed the significance of the text of the
Servant of Yahweh in Isaiah 53, in which it is said that he bore
the sin of “the many” ( rabim ). “The many” is the same term
Christ uses in the institution of the Eucharist (Mark 14:22-25;
Matthew 26:26-29). It is also the term used in the famous saying
of the ransom: “The Son of Man did not come to be served, but to
serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many” (Matthew
20:28). Thus, we see that Christ assumes the figure and function
of the Servant of Yahweh who gave himself as atonement for the
sins of humanity (Isaiah 53:10). The same prepositions used in
the institution of the Eucharist, üper and peri (for the sake
of), are characteristic of expiatory sacrifices, indicating for
whose sake the atonement is made. The letter also speaks of the
blood offered ( didomenon ). Furthermore, the letter to the
Hebrews presents Christ's sacrifice as the true, unique, and
definitive atoning sacrifice that has superseded the expiatory
sacrifices offered on Yom Kippur , the Day of Atonement.
Therefore, to deny Christ's atoning sacrifice is to deny the
entire letter to the Hebrews.

What Christ did in the Eucharist was to institute the sacrifice
of the new and eternal covenant, which would be sealed with his
blood on the cross, to leave it to his Church as a memorial of
his death and resurrection: “Do this in remembrance of me. For
whenever you eat this bread and drink this cup, you proclaim the
Lord’s death until he comes” (1 Corinthians 11:26). In the
institution of the Eucharist, Christ gives himself to his own in
a real way through his body and blood. It is a sacramental
anticipation of what will occur in a bloody way in the mystery of
his cross and resurrection. But it is understood that whoever
does not believe in the divinity of Jesus Christ cannot grasp the
wonder of what he has done in the Eucharist.

8. The Resurrection

I am obliged to summarize the topic of the Resurrection more than
I would like, which I have discussed in detail in my work Lord
and Christ (Ed. Palabra), but the length set for this work
dictates it.

The first thing that strikes you when reading Pagola, who is so
concerned with historical accuracy, is that he completely changes
the historical order of events related to the Resurrection. The
Gospels present first the discovery of the empty tomb, which
provokes bewilderment and fear in the women; and then they speak
of the apparitions, which confirm their belief in the
Resurrection. Pagola, on the other hand, begins with the
apparitions and then speaks of the empty tomb. Why? Because he
understands that everything boils down to an "experience" of
faith (this is how he interprets the apparitions), and the tomb
is a reality that can essentially be disregarded.

Pagola maintains that the Resurrection is real but not
historical; that is, it did not take place within history,
because it is a reality that transcends it (418). We agree that
this is not a Resurrection like that of Lazarus, who returns to
earthly life and then to death. Christ's Resurrection is
transcendent because with his glorified body he has definitively
conquered death. But he has left traces in history: an empty tomb
and apparitions. That is what the texts say. The verb used to
describe Jesus' appearance is ophthé , an aorist passive that
translates as "he appeared." This verb is used because it is the
one used in the Vulgate translation when speaking of God's
appearances in the Old Testament. But other verbs are also used,
such as faino and faneroo, which mean visible appearance. And
likewise verbs like this one in meso autón : he stood in the
midst of them (Lk 24, 36; Jn 20, 19-26).

But since Pagola refuses to acknowledge that Christ's
Resurrection is simultaneously transcendent and historical, he is
forced to explain that what happened was that the apostles had an
"experience" of faith that Jesus was alive, based on their faith
in God's faithfulness (420). And they attributed this
"experience" to God. Only God could have revealed something so
great and unexpected to them. They were familiar with the
doctrine of the Resurrection of the Body found in Daniel 12:1-2
and perhaps had heard of the seven martyrs tortured by Antiochus
Epiphanes (2 Maccabees 7:9-23), which helped them interpret their
"experience" of Jesus as alive and risen.

Let us pause for a moment to reflect on all this. What
"experience" of faith could the apostles have had after the death
of Jesus, when he died as all those crucified die, cursed by God?
For Scripture says (Galatians 3:13) that whoever dies on a cross
is cursed by God. And Jesus was legitimately tried by the
Sanhedrin and condemned as a blasphemer. They were hiding,
preparing to return to fishing on the Sea of
​​Tiberias. When they tell Thomas that they have seen
him, he replies that unless he puts his hands into the wounds, he
will not believe (John 21:25). Therefore, the New Catechism
states that claiming that faith in the Resurrection arose from
faith itself is entirely untenable (n. 644), since the apostles
would not have returned to faith without their tangible encounter
with Jesus (n. 643).

One small detail: the disciples on the road to Emmaus, as some
theologians say, recognized Jesus only through an "experience" of
faith, but the text says that, in the midst of that "experience,"
Jesus made himself invisible to them ( afantos egeneto ), which
demonstrates that alongside the experience of faith there was
a visible manifestation that now disappears. Therefore, there was
a visible appearance that cannot be confused with the
"experience" of faith. In any case, if one had wanted to speak of
an "experience" of faith, the disciples had a Greek term, horama
(inner vision above all), that they could have used for it. And
yet they don't use it even once. Furthermore, a Resurrection,
even that of the Messiah in the midst of history, was absolutely
unimaginable for the Jews. The Maccabean martyrs awaited the
Resurrection, but for the end of history. Did the disciples on
the road to Emmaus not recognize him at first? Let us not forget
that only Jesus has the power to grant these apparitions. He
could not be seen by whomever he wished, as in the case of
Lazarus, but only by whomever Jesus chose. He alone grants these
apparitions and appears to whomever he wishes, whenever he
wishes, and however he wishes. If I may, we can recall the
apparitions at Lourdes: only Bernadette saw the Virgin, while
those accompanying her did not. We humans do not have the power
to grant Christ the power to appear.

It is ridiculous, on the other hand, to resort to the argument
that Paul doesn't speak of the empty tomb. If he doesn't speak of
it, it's because he didn't have the experience of finding it; but
he mentions it implicitly when he recalls that it was the one who
was buried who rose again (1 Corinthians 15:3-5). Nor can it be
said that Paul's was an "experience." He heard a voice in which
Christ identified himself and told him what he had to do. Indeed,
he says that Christ spoke to him in Hebrew (Acts 26:14). St. Paul
always makes excuses when he speaks of his "visions" and never
does so when he speaks of the encounter with Christ that made him
an apostle. When John and Peter feel compelled not to speak of
Jesus, they respond by saying that they cannot stop speaking of
what they have seen and believed (Acts 4:20), referring above all
to the Resurrection (Acts 4:10).

Speaking of the empty tomb, Pagola says: “We do not know whether
(Jesus) ended up in a common grave like so many of those
executed, or whether Joseph of Arimathea was able to do something
to bury him in a tomb in the surrounding area” (431). But the
discovery of the empty tomb is not the decisive factor. What is
decisive is not its discovery but the revelation made about it:
“Jesus of Nazareth, the crucified one, has been raised by God”
(432). What matters is that Jesus’ disciples experienced him as
alive through faith.

One small detail: if we consider the discovery of the empty tomb
by Peter and John, who rushed to it after Mary Magdalene reported
finding it empty, we read that John arrived first and saw the
burial cloths lying there, and the same thing happened to Peter.
But the Greek text doesn't speak of the cloths lying there, but
rather of the cloths that were keimena , that is, laid out, lying
flat, without the outline of the body, as Father Iglesias
explains in his New Testament . That is why John says of himself
that he "saw and believed" (John 20:8), because he understood
that, since they were still bound but empty, the body had not
been stolen. For the disciples, it was the apparitions that gave
them faith; for John, faith began with the empty tomb, although
he later confirmed his faith through the apparitions.

No one denies, therefore, that the Resurrection of Christ is
transcendent (it was not like that of Lazarus); but the
Resurrection is falsified when it is separated from history. Does
the risen Christ, who is God, not have the power to manifest
himself visibly? Who are we to tell God what he can or cannot do?
The Resurrection cannot be separated from the historical
dimension. Christianity is not an ideology nor an inner
"experience." Christianity is based on history: on seeing and
touching the Word of life, as St. John says (1 John 1:1), the
most transcendent and the most realistic of the four theologians.
But is modern theology returning to Gnosticism?

Conclusion

We will attempt to state clearly and succinctly the conclusion we
have reached regarding Pagola's book: quite simply, this is
neither the faith of the Church nor the faith of Scripture. He
states quite clearly: "At no time did Jesus make any claim to be
God: neither Jesus nor his followers during his lifetime used the
title 'Son of God' to profess his divine nature" (379). For
Pagola, Jesus is not God. He is an itinerant prophet who believed
in the God of the Old Testament and who discovered his face as
a compassionate Father. The Kingdom of God, consequently, is not
the arrival of God's salvation, which coincides with the person
of Christ and brings us divine filiation and the forgiveness of
sins; a Kingdom that obviously must also fight against evil and
injustice. For him, the Kingdom of God is only this human and
social dimension as liberation from suffering and injustice.
Christ's healings (which he never calls miracles) are also not
works that transcend human capacity and that can prove Jesus'
divinity; They don't go beyond healings attributed to the fact
that Jesus stirred in people the emergence of faith, awakening
hidden abilities—a religious healer. The power to forgive sins
belongs not to Christ but to God. Jesus proclaims God's
forgiveness through his closeness to sinners, insofar as they
open themselves to his mercy, but without the condition or
commitment to change their lives. Nor does he see in the Passion
of Christ a mystery of salvation willed by the Father, who sends
his Son to offer his life for the redemption of our sins; it is
simply Jesus's rejection of proclaiming God's merciful goodness.
The Eucharist is merely a farewell meal in which the arrival of
the Kingdom and the death of Christ are remembered, and the final
victory of the Kingdom is evoked. It is a remembrance and
a recollection. Nothing more. And, as we have seen, the
Resurrection has no historical dimension. Everything is reduced
to an "experience" of faith (this is how he interprets the
appearances) through which the disciples came to believe that
Jesus was still alive. The discovery of the empty tomb is not the
decisive factor. “We do not know whether (Jesus) ended up in
a common grave like so many of those executed, or whether Joseph
of Arimathea was able to do something to bury him in a tomb in
the surrounding area” (431). The decisive factor is not that, but
the revelation that is made: “Jesus of Nazareth, the crucified
one, has been raised by God” (431).

The dominant category in this Jesuology (not Christology) is that
of an immanentist "experience" incapable of acknowledging that
the Word, the second person of the Trinity, truly entered history
to divinize us in Christ and liberate us from the slavery of sin
and death from which we could not free ourselves. Christianity,
according to this view, has no other originality than having
revealed to us the face of God as a good and compassionate Father
through an itinerant prophet named Jesus.

José Antonio Sayés
