### José Antonio Pagola: Volver a Jesucristo
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AQnI9VNl9qg

- Good afternoon everyone.
- I’m going to speak about something that I carry very deeply
  within me—something that has been with me for several years
  now.
- I want to share the questions I’ve been asking myself for about
  ten years, ever since I wrote my book on Jesus, and also the
  situation I now find myself in.
- The questions I keep asking are these:
    - Can Christianity today find within itself the spiritual
      strength it needs to bring about a true conversion to
      Jesus—yes or no?
    - Is it possible, at this moment, to mobilise the Church’s
      energies towards a more faithful following of Jesus?
    - Will Pope Francis open up a new horizon?
    - And what can we do from the grassroots—from our parishes
      and Christian communities?
- Because, even if Pope Francis creates a transparent reform and
  the major structures of the Vatican are renewed, we will not
  really feel that here. The Church will survive this crisis
  depending on what we ourselves do.
- The Church is much more than the Vatican. I worry that we focus
  everything there and fail to notice how faith is gradually
  fading away.
- Naturally, I cannot cover all aspects or dimensions of
  a possible renewal of the Church. That’s not my aim.
- My goal is much more modest.
- But in some way, I want to ignite something here. Don’t think
  we are few—this is being recorded, and I know how these things
  can spread. I want to spark something of the fire that Jesus
  wanted to light.
- Jesus said: “I have come to bring fire to the earth.”
- And we must ask ourselves: where is that fire today, when
  Christianity—at least outwardly—seems to be fading?
- I also want to shake our consciences—mine included—so that we
  take more seriously what the Spirit of Jesus is clearly telling
  our small churches today.

### Is another kind of Church possible?
- Is it just a dream?
- Can we begin that change now?
- That is what I want to talk about.
- Starting point: Four key realities
- I will begin by highlighting four major facts that can help us
  reflect on the need—and the possibility—of returning to Jesus.

### 1. The risk of a closed Church
- In recent decades, countless sociological studies, surveys, and
  statistics have been published about the critical and declining
  state of Christian churches—not only the Catholic
  Church—especially in the West.
- To ignore these facts is to close our eyes and face the future
  irresponsibly.
- And yet, something like that has been happening in important
  sectors of the Church—sectors that want to move into the future
  by looking backwards.
- But there is an even more dangerous risk.
- Faced with these negative—and increasingly negative—statistics,
  we may react instinctively in self-defence, without pausing
  calmly, with faith and evangelical clarity, to discern what
  followers of Jesus should actually do today.
- Let me briefly point out three aspects of this “closed Church”:
    - In recent years, it is not difficult to observe attitudes
      of nervousness and fear within the Catholic Church—pastoral
      behaviours driven more by self-preservation than by the
      Spirit of Jesus, whom we profess in the Creed as “Lord and
      giver of life.”
    - We can also see a growing attitude of defensiveness. In
      Spain, for example, the Church has often been on the
      defensive, sometimes even aggressive—far removed from the
      trusting spirit of Jesus, who sent his disciples out “like
      lambs among wolves.”
    - This attitude has led many to see modern society as the
      Church’s main enemy—as if society had deliberately set out
      to destroy Christianity.
- In this way, often unconsciously, denunciation and condemnation
  have become a kind of pastoral programme. Some even think that
  the Church’s most urgent task is to judge and condemn.
- This isn’t just my opinion. The French bishop Claude Dagens
  once said that faith was being turned into a “counterculture”
  and the Church into a “counter-society.”
- From such a position, it becomes almost impossible to proclaim
  the God of Jesus as the best friend of the human person—or to
  look at the world with the compassion with which Jesus looked
  at it.

### 2. The option for restorationism
- At a time of deep socio-cultural change—when major decisions
  are needed, at least at the pastoral and disciplinary level—the
  Church’s leadership has largely chosen to retreat into what is
  often called `restorationism`: restoring the past, even the
  pre–Vatican II past.
- This carries an obvious risk: turning Christianity into
  a religion of the past in a society that looks only towards the
  future.
- It risks making Christianity increasingly irrelevant,
  especially for younger generations.
- Instead of walking alongside the men and women of today—helping
  to build a more humane, healthier, happier world—the Church has
  often withdrawn into rigid preservation of tradition.
- This mindset has spread beyond the hierarchy into many areas of
  Church life: a religious conservatism far removed from the
  creative, prophetic spirit of Jesus of Nazareth.
- Strict observance is enforced, creativity is discouraged,
  orthodoxy is tightly controlled—and everything seems fixed
  forever.
- In such a context, it can seem that the only task is to
  preserve and repeat the past.
- And over the past decades, there has been little encouragement
  of innovation, experimentation, or the search for new paths—so
  alive in the years following the Second Vatican Council.
- It is difficult to recognise in all this the spirit of Jesus,
  who wanted “new wine in new wineskins.”

### 3. Widespread passivity
- Perhaps the most widespread trait among Christians who still
  remain in the Church is passivity.
- Of course, we should not overlook the many committed Christians
  involved in education, social movements, NGOs, and work with
  the marginalised.
- But this should not hide the fact that millions of Catholics
  remain passive.
- What has happened?
- A very serious distortion of the Gospel.
- For centuries, believers have been educated in submission,
  obedience, silence, and passivity.
- Christianity has been organised not as a religion of calling,
  but as a religion of authority.
- Lay people are expected simply to listen and obey.
- As a result, Church structures have not encouraged shared
  responsibility among the People of God.
- Even after Vatican II—despite changes in language and some
  steps forward—lay people are still often not truly involved in
  thinking, planning, or decision-making.
- And this, perhaps, is the greatest obstacle to the
  transformation the Church now needs.
- …and that fact is perhaps the greatest obstacle to promoting
  the transformation the Church needs today: a mass of believers
  passively handed over to a clearly restorationist hierarchy
  will hardly open up new paths for the project of Jesus in the
  midst of this society.
- But there is a fourth point that I must add in this reflection:

### 4. A Church that is seeking new paths.
- In a short time—indeed, the day after tomorrow—it will be one
  year since Pope Francis was elected.
- This man has generated completely unexpected
  expectations—things we could not have imagined just a few
  months ago.
- He has created a new ecclesial climate. He is breaking the
  dynamic in which we have been living in recent years, and
  little by little he is defining his purpose.
- I have followed him as closely as I could, because it seems to
  me that this is the most important event I will live
  through—and many of you as well.
- Yes, the Council is there, but the conciliar spirit froze. And
  I am convinced that conciliar decrees alone will never convert
  the Church. We must look for another path.
- The decrees of a council can change some things over time, but
  they do not easily change the climate.
- The director of the Jesuit journal 'La Civiltà Cattolica' said
  that the Pope is seeking a Church that finds new paths—not
  merely one that looks for them. And those words, I think,
  already summed up his desire.
- I paid close attention to his homily at Pentecost last May.
  I expected him to say something important—the feast marks the
  birth of the Church—but what struck me was the freshness of his
  language, his strength, his determination to free the Church
  from the fears that have paralysed us in recent years.
- He said things like this:
    - “Novelty always frightens us, because we feel safer if
      everything is under control—if we are the ones who
      construct, plan, and organise our lives according to our
      own schemes, certainties, and preferences.”
- And he added:
    - “We are afraid that God may lead us along new paths that
      take us beyond our limited, closed, and often selfish
      horizons.”
- Then he publicly asked a question that clearly reveals his
  direction:
    - “Are we willing to follow the new paths that God’s novelty
      presents to us, or do we lock ourselves into outdated
      structures?”
- Outdated structures—structures that have lost their capacity to
  respond.
- What are we going to do?
- We are not used to this kind of language in these times, and we
  still do not know how far we will be able to go under the
  impulse of Pope Francis.
- But what matters is that something has shifted. An inertia has
  been broken, and no one knows what changes, innovations, or
  conversions may begin to emerge.

### Returning to Jesus Christ
- And now, in this context, I move to what is, in my judgement,
  the central point.
- The decisive shift that Christianity needs today—the essential
  self-correction—is simply this:
    - TO RETURN TO JESUS.
- To centre the Church more truthfully and more faithfully on his
  person, his message, and his project.
- The Pope himself, in one of his homilies at Santa Marta, said:
    - “The Church must bring Jesus. This is the centre of the
      Church: to bring Jesus. And if the Church does not bring
      Jesus, it is a dead Church.”
- He uses a language I myself have not dared to use until now.
- And in his apostolic exhortation—where he expresses the
  direction he wants the Church to take—he says, right at the
  beginning:
    - “We must return to Jesus, who can break the dull patterns
      in which we try to enclose him.”
- This radical conversion to Jesus is the most important thing
  that can happen in the Church in the coming years.
- Of course, many things will have to be done in different areas
  and at all levels. But nothing will be more decisive than
  fostering this return to Jesus.
- The Pope continues by saying that being Christian without Jesus
  is the greatest danger we face.

### What does this “conversion to Jesus” mean?
- Let me offer a brief reflection.
- I dare to speak of conversion—and not just because we are in
  Lent. What I am speaking about is something much deeper.
- This is not about a temporary adjustment of the Church to
  modern times. The Pope is not talking about that kind of
  “updating.” Nor am I speaking about simply recovering the
  spirit of Vatican II.
- I am speaking about returning to Jesus as the source—the only
  source and origin of the Church.
- About allowing the God revealed and embodied in Jesus to be the
  one and only God of the Church:
- the God who is a friend of life, the defender of the poor.
- Only by going back to this source can any necessary renewal
  follow.

### No ready-made answers
- This conversion is not something we should expect from the
  hierarchy.
- Historically, the hierarchy has not led great movements of
  conversion.
- Nor should we expect it from theologians—or even from priests
  and religious. I am speaking of a sustained conversion that
  must unfold over the coming decades.
- We have no ready-made answers. Everything is new.
- The only thing we can pass on to future generations—the few
  that will come—is not a formula, but a spirit: a direction, an
  orientation towards the Gospel and towards Jesus.

### A deeper problem in Christian life
- Today, even among very good Christians, Christianity is often
  not centred on following Jesus.
- It hasn’t been taught that way.
- We prepare people for First Communion, for Confirmation, for
  many things—but not to make a `real personal decision` in
  relation to Jesus.
- The project of the Kingdom of God—the project of building
  a more human world—is largely absent from pastoral programmes.
- Compassion, which is the great legacy of Jesus—“Be
  compassionate as your Father is compassionate”—is not the
  central principle guiding the Church.
- Even when charitable work functions well, the poorest are not
  truly placed at the centre.
- So what we need is not simply a religious reform.
- After twenty centuries of Christianity, the very heart of the
  Church needs conversion and purification.
- And in this unprecedented time of socio-cultural change, the
  Church needs an unprecedented conversion—a new heart capable of
  generating faith in Jesus Christ in a new way.

### A warning
- If, in the coming years, our parishes and communities do not
  create a more human, welcoming, and even enthusiastic climate—a
  return to the Gospel and to Jesus—
- then it is very likely that we will see our faith gradually
  disappear.
- Many parishes will vanish. Especially smaller ones.
- And Christianity, where it survives, may become diluted into
  sectarian and declining forms, increasingly far from the
  movement inspired by Jesus.

### Back to the essential
- Returning to Jesus is not about making small adjustments—like
  changing how Mass is celebrated.
- It is already too late for that.
- What we need is something deeper:
    - a return to what is essential—to the original, foundational
      experience of Jesus.
- To root the Church in him as the only truth from which we can
  live, and from which we can move creatively into the future.
- We cannot just “tidy up” the Church. We cannot keep waiting—for
  reforms, for decrees.
- It is too late for that.
- We are being called to act—to create a new climate of humble,
  realistic, simple searching, focused on living the essential of
  the Gospel.

### A new relationship with Jesus
- This leads to the next step:
    - conversion to Jesus means developing a new `quality of
      relationship` with him.
- A Church made up of Christians who relate to a poorly known
  Jesus—a vaguely understood, abstract, silent Jesus—cannot
  endure.
- A Jesus who does not speak, who does not attract, who does not
  call… Such a Church risks disappearing—
- And one has to ask:
    - why should it continue, and what is the point?
- A Church without Jesus has no place in this world; it would
  simply vanish. Like salt that has lost its taste, it is of no
  use. A Church that is neither salt, nor light, nor leaven in
  society has no reason to exist.
- What we need are communities marked by a new experience of
  Jesus.
- We need Christians who live from Jesus and for his project—who
  belong to the Church because they belong to Jesus, and who help
  make the Church more faithful to him, not simply because they
  were baptised, as the records say.
- We need men and women who introduce Jesus into communities as
  the most valuable reality, the most attractive reality.
- The Pope is also using this language:
    - the only truly attractive thing the Church has today is
      Jesus.
- This means recovering and caring for our essential identity as
  followers of Jesus.
- Concretely, this would mean moving in the coming years towards
  a new level of Christian existence—a new phase of Christianity,
  more inspired and motivated by Jesus, and better organised for
  serving the project of the Kingdom of God.
- That is the horizon from which we should be working today.
- We must work within our Christian communities, but what is
  decisive is this:
    - we must not resign ourselves to living a Christianity
      without conversion.
    - That makes no sense.
- All of us are being called at this moment to collaborate in
  a task that is difficult—but deeply compelling.

### How can we live Christianity today without passion?
- We may do what we can—even if it costs us everything—but we
  must try to move, in the small history of our churches, towards
  a new phase that is more faithful to Jesus.
- Age does not matter.
- Each of us, in our own way, can contribute to making Jesus more
  present and more alive in the Church—to making the Church more
  truly his, and its face more human, more like his.
- Knowing Jesus, seeing as he saw
- If we ignore Jesus—if our people do not truly know him—our
  parishes and communities will end up not knowing themselves.
- They will not know what they are for.
- They will not be able to grasp what is most essential about the
  mission Jesus lived.
- If we do not learn to look at life as Jesus looked at it—to
  look at people, to interpret events with the same compassion—
- then we may think we are privileged people living in the light
  of revelation, but in reality we will not know how to see the
  world as Jesus would see it today.
- And if we do not listen to the suffering of people — especially
  now, in times of crisis, with hunger and hardship — then our
  communities will become deaf communities, unable to hear the
  Gospel, and therefore unable to communicate the Good News of
  Jesus.

### Pope Francis and resistance
- The Pope has arrived—and he is doing this wonderfully.
- But you will soon see the resistance he will face. That will be
  the clearest sign. In fact, he has already had a fairly calm
  first year—more or less.
- We must dare to discern what is true and what is false in our
  celebrations, in our projects, in our priorities.
- We must not be afraid to name our deviations from the Gospel.
- I have gathered many examples from his apostolic exhortation
  The Joy of the Gospel—where he describes distorted forms of
  Christianity that make evangelisation impossible today.
- And the description is striking—you can recognise it
  everywhere.
- But this is not about blaming or attacking one another.
- It is a mistake to think that by condemning each other—as
  bishops, theologians, factions—we will bring about conversion.
- The Church will not be converted to Jesus in that way.
- What we need instead is to take responsibility together—to
  carry the present sin of the Church together.
- It belongs to all of us—not equally, perhaps—but all of us are,
  in some way, complicit through passivity, unconsciousness, or
  inaction.
- So the question remains:
    - Can Christianity today find within itself the strength
      needed to set this process in motion?
- Or is all this just a talk—and nothing more?

### 3. Releasing the power of the Gospel
- I now move to a third point:
    - to release the power of the Gospel.
- Pope Francis has already pointed to the path and the driving
  force. He says: “Every time we return to the sources and
  recover the original freshness of the Gospel, new paths arise,
  creative methods, new forms of expression, more eloquent signs,
  and words filled with renewed meaning for today’s world.”

### Direct contact with the Gospel
- Let me highlight two key points.
- First: we must seek together a direct contact with the Gospel.
- This is simply a matter of evangelical common sense—of learning
  to see clearly what is happening.
- The most obvious and serious symptom of the current religious
  crisis is this:
    - people are drifting away—steadily and increasingly.
- We can analyse the causes, of course—but one thing is clear:
    - The Church is losing its power of attraction. It is losing
      credibility.
- People are not coming. They are leaving.
- And this should force us to ask a crucial question:
    - What are we going to do?
- Do we continue as before—trying, with good will, to serve those
  who still come—even though we are working within a Church that
  is losing its appeal?
- Or do we recover, as quickly as possible, the Gospel as the
  only real force capable of awakening faith in people today?

### What people actually encounter
- When people come to a parish today, they do not encounter the
  Gospel of Jesus directly.
- What they experience is the functioning of an old,
  centuries-old religion, showing clear signs of ageing and
  decline.
- The Gospel is there—but hidden behind layers of language,
  customs, practices, devotions, and formulas that are
  increasingly incomprehensible.
- It is not easy for people today to recognise the power of the
  Gospel within all that.
- And yet, that power comes from the impact Jesus himself had in
  Galilee.

### A critical problem
- Yes, the Gospel is still present in some way, and it gives
  meaning to the lives of many good people. But as it is
  currently lived, it does not generate followers of Jesus.
- It produces adherents to a religion—people who fulfil what is
  expected, more or less—but not disciples who consciously choose
  to live from the Gospel.
- We still initiate Christians NOT into an `experience of Jesus`,
  but into a religion—through doctrinal, moral, and sacramental
  instruction.
- And yet, as Pope Benedict XVI said:
    - “One does not begin to be a Christian through an ethical
      decision or a great idea, but through an encounter with
      a person.”

### Second-hand Christianity
- Many good Christians only know the Gospel second-hand.
- Their understanding of Jesus is pieced together from what they
  have heard from preachers and catechists.
- They may live correctly within the Church—but without any
  `direct, living contact` with the Gospel.
- And that is the core issue.
- The energy of the Gospel is trapped within a religious
  structure in crisis—unable to reach people in a living way.

### A new question
- …So we must ask: has the time not come to place the Gospel
  truly at the centre of our communities?
- Because what we need is to share the Gospel of Jesus in the way
  it was shared in Galilee—where people could talk freely about
  what he said and what he didn’t say…
- we must begin to gather in very small groups:
- believers, less-believing people, even those who do not know
  whether they believe or not—but who are interested in Jesus.
- And we must give the Gospel the opportunity to come into direct
  and immediate contact with the men and women of today—
- so that it can enter into their questions, their fears, their
  hopes, their failures.
- We must awaken—this is important—in ordinary people a desire
  for the Gospel.
- They were the ones who truly understood Jesus—the simple
  people—and Jesus himself gave thanks to the Father because it
  was precisely the simple who understood, not the religious
  leaders.
- We must bring the desire for the Gospel to ordinary people—so
  that they come to know it more deeply, enjoy it, and even
  demand it.
- Because today, almost everything in the Church is decided far
  from the People of God.
- But if the people truly take hold of the Gospel, they will
  carry the hierarchy along with them.
- As Michel de Certeau once said (and he is not the only one):
    - “It is the Christian people who will save Christianity, not
      its leaders—who, in the end, can only follow.”
- And this has happened before.

### The Gospel as the beginning of a new identity
- I am speaking about the Gospel as the story of Jesus—read,
  listened to, and shared.
- Not about the homily.
- I am speaking about creating a new space, beyond the Sunday
  obligation—which no one even knows how long it can survive in
  a world where the rhythm of life has changed so much.
- A group sharing the Gospel is the most natural way to live the
  original Christian experience.
- Because in the Gospel we have the living memory of
  Jesus—preserved by those who followed him, walked with him,
  listened to him, saw how he acted, how he prayed, how he
  related to people—even those considered undesirable.
- The heart of everything is there, in the Gospels.
- And the Gospels are not textbooks.
- They do not offer academic doctrine about Jesus. They are not
  catechisms.
- What one learns first, through direct contact with the Gospel,
  is a way of life:
    - Jesus’ way of being in the world
    - his way of understanding life
    - his way of making life more human
- And the main feature of these groups gathered around Jesus is
  that they offer something new—an experience we ourselves have
  not really had:
    - to be born into faith today, not through instruction or
      training, but through transformation in contact with Jesus.

### A different kind of listening
- In these groups—even if a priest is present—he sits like
  everyone else.
- What is heard is not the catechist, not the preacher.
- What we try to hear is the Word of God embodied in the life of
  Jesus.
- And I am convinced that if we succeed in living this
  experience, it will gradually introduce a new dynamic into
  parishes and communities— a new phase in the life of the
  Church.
- Because it would lead us to live faith not as doctrinal
  agreement expressed in concepts from other times and cultures—
  (doctrine ages; it cannot be constantly renewed fast enough)—
  but as a way of life.
- A way of life that can be lived in any culture, in any age.
- The style of life of Jesus does not become outdated.
- Following Jesus, not repeating formulas
- I am not dismissing doctrine.
- But it must be understood and lived from something more
  fundamental:
    - following Jesus and living his way of life.
- Put simply:
    - we must learn to believe in Jesus Christ by following him,
      more than by repeating formulas.
- We have repeated the Creed for centuries—
    - “God from God, Light from Light…”—
- but where is the question:
    - Who was Jesus?
    - How did he live?
    - How do we follow him?
- That is not in the Creed.
- And this should make us think.

### A shift in understanding Christianity
- After twenty centuries shaped by `Greek metaphysics` and `Roman
  legal thinking`, we are beginning to realise that this is not
  the best expression of Christianity.
- Jesus still has many surprises to offer.
- We have not yet seen the best of what he brings.
- Reading the Gospel as conversion
- We must learn to read the Gospels not as something read before
  a sermon— but as texts of conversion.
- They were written to awaken disciples—to invite people into
  a process of transformation, into identifying with Jesus’
  mission.
- Only from that attitude can the Gospels be understood.
- Otherwise, they are used merely as material for theories and
  doctrines.

### Not just another activity
- Sharing the Gospel like this is not just another parish
  activity.
- It is the matrix from which faith can be renewed.
- The Gospel has a regenerative power that we do not fully grasp.
- Here, faith is lived not out of obligation, but out of
  attraction.
- I once met an atheist who told me: “I go to Mass—just in case.”
- Many people live like that.
- But this is different.
- When people truly encounter the Gospel, it becomes
  a surprise—it transforms Christianity from a duty into an
  exciting path of following Jesus.
- I am convinced that direct contact with Jesus and his Gospel
  can gradually lead our communities into a new phase— a small
  but real step forward.

### A concrete proposal: “Jesus groups”
- And now I come to something practical.
- All of this would mean nothing if we did not take concrete
  steps.
- So I propose what I call “Jesus groups.”
- This is a project already beginning to take shape in various
  places.
- The goal is simple:
    - to live together a process of conversion to
      Jesus—individually and as a group—by going deeper into the
      essentials of the Gospel in a simple way.
- These groups:
    - belong to no movement or institution
    - are not linked to any particular spirituality
    - do not replace existing parish structures
- What defines them is this:
    - they live a process of conversion in direct contact with
      the Gospel, over several years.
- They are based on Jesus’ promise:
    - “Where two or three are gathered in my name, I am there.”

### How they work
- No special training is required
- People do not even need to be practising believers
- They can meet in homes, cafés, or simple spaces
- They are led by lay people—not dependent on clergy
- In some places, people even leave an empty chair in the
  group—as a sign that Jesus is present.

### Who can join
- Anyone who genuinely wants to explore Jesus can join.
- These groups are already forming:
    - among married couples
    - friends
    - religious communities
    - ordinary parish groups

- And in all of this, lay people have a crucial role—
- …because this is something that cannot depend on priests alone—
- lay men and women can also discover here a real mission…
- This can enter the Church—but it can also, and must also, enter
  society.
- It is helpful, of course, to have someone who coordinates
  things—someone who makes sure the group actually meets—but this
  person does not need to be the most knowledgeable.
- People are not coming to listen to them.
- They are coming to listen to the Gospel.
- To support this journey, I am preparing some materials—simple
  aids, not rigid structures—encouraging each group to be
  creative and original.
- What is offered?
    - the Gospel text to be read and shared
    - a simple guide for reading it
    - questions to help the group identify what is most essential
    - reflections rooted in life, not just theory
    - a short commentary to enrich understanding
    - questions aimed at personal conversion
    - and suggestions for prayer—both personal and shared
- All of this, with great freedom and creativity.

### What happens at the end
- And what happens when the process ends?
- The group reflects on its future.
- If it has not worked, it simply dissolves—there is no need for
  yet another group in the Church.
- But if the experience has been good, several possibilities open
  up:
    - they may continue meeting, perhaps monthly
    - they may become a small evangelising team within their
      context
    - or—and this is important—they may commit to starting new
      “Jesus groups” themselves
- Without waiting for priests, permissions, or structures.
- Simply by sharing what they have lived.
- Simple, realistic beginnings
- These are not large groups.
- They are small, simple, human:
- Two couples, a friend, a widower…
- Nothing forced.
- The Gospel spread by attraction—and it must do so again.

### A quiet but powerful hope
- If this were only a dream… But perhaps, quietly—almost
  underground—while Pope Francis is calling for renewal— the
  regenerative force of the Gospel can begin to circulate again.
- The main responsibility lies with lay people.
- I know many who long to live their faith differently—and who
  are perfectly capable of gathering others and guiding such
  groups.
- And alongside other initiatives, this could bring about what
  the Pope himself has spoken of:
    - a new evangelising dynamism that works by attraction.

- And with that, I will finish.
- I have gone on too long—but I wanted to say everything, to
  reach the end. Now, you can share whatever you wish—your
  thoughts, your questions, your dreams.

### Q&A
- Question: Would it not be helpful to have a priest present, at
  least at the beginning, to guide things?
- Answer:
- It depends on the priest.
- There are priests already participating, sitting as equals in
  the group.
- But there is a risk: if a priest begins explaining everything,
  the process changes. It becomes instruction again, instead of
  a journey of conversion.
- This is not a Bible study group. It is a process of conversion.
- Priests can encourage and support—but they are not the centre.
- A key practical insight
- If a group encounters something in the Gospel they do not
  understand:
    - Skip it—and continue.
    - Like with any book.
    - Because even what you do understand is more than enough to
      begin changing your life.
- The Gospels were not written for debate, but for
  transformation.

- On how the groups function
- The questions are designed to engage the person, not just the
  intellect.
- And what is most important is that everything does not turn
  into argument.
- Everyone listens—not to win a discussion, but to listen to
  Jesus.

### Final reflection
- And perhaps the Church of the future will indeed be made up of
  small communities— …what matters is not their size, but the
  `fire` at their centre— and in fact, people are already
  beginning to take steps, even with small difficulties, trying
  to form these groups…

- Someone explains that they have started forming a group, though with some hesitation from their parish priest.
- They asked to use a parish room—and the priest agreed.
- But people keep asking: will there be a priest present?
- And the answer is: it depends—some want one there, others do not.

- In reality, many people still feel they need the presence of a priest—but that situation is changing.
- Because in twenty years, there may be no parish priest available.
- So the real question is: can we function on our own?
- In some places, they already are—there is great boldness in certain regions.
- And this kind of beginning—with some tension, some resistance—is normal.
- That is how things are born.

### On spreading the idea
- If people are forming these groups, it may help them to watch
  or listen to talks like this—to understand the vision more
  clearly.
- The idea will spread naturally.

### Do these groups lead to action?
- Someone asks whether these groups will eventually lead to
  concrete commitments—engagement with real issues like social
  crises or peace processes.
- The answer: YES.
- But that emerges from the process itself.
- The group gradually discovers its path.

- Another question: Should others create similar materials?
- The answer: Yes—there are already many approaches in different
  countries: in France (more intellectual discussion groups) in
  Belgium in Italy in Quebec But most are focused on formation
  rather than conversion. The hope is that more processes like
  this will emerge—ways of returning to the source. With Pope
  Francis, this may well happen.

### Is prior faith required?
- Another question: do people need to already have faith?
- No.
- This is for anyone who wants to get to know Jesus.
- People come with whatever faith they have—or none.
- This is not about debate or instruction.
- It is about listening, sharing, and allowing the Gospel to speak.

### A key insight
- When Jesus spoke, there were all kinds of people present.
- The same must be true here.
- The success of this does not depend on perfect organisation—
- it depends on giving the Gospel the chance to be heard
  directly.

### On young people and experience
- There are already some experiences with younger people and
  mixed groups—students, former pupils, lay collaborators.
- It is still something emerging—something not yet fully born.

### A final anecdote
- He recounts a conversation with a bishop who asked:
- “Who is going to manage all this?”
- And he replied:
- “Jesus.”
- The bishop laughed and said:
- “Yes… Jesus—and human mediations.”
- And the answer came:
- “He will know.”
- Of course, human structures matter—but they are not the most
  important thing.
- If even a few people truly encounter the Gospel and come into
  harmony with Jesus— then we will see what happens.

### A realistic note on difficulty
- The real difficulties are not intellectual questions about God.
- They arise when the Gospel touches real life:
    - money
    - comfort
    - relationships
    - the difficulty of living coherently
- That is where true conversion becomes challenging.

### Final exchanges
- There are questions about publicity.
- The answer:
    - There is no intention to hide this.
    - Books and materials will be published, talks shared—this
      will spread.
    - But not through marketing strategies. Rather, through the
      natural movement of people who find something meaningful.

### Closing thought
- There is no fear of where this might go.
- We will simply see what happens.
