- The Unexpected Jesus.
- Today's Gospel: Mk 9:14-29.
- This weekend I finished a book that I had long wanted to read:
    - "Jesus: An Historical Approximation," and was written by
      a Spanish priest named Jose Pagola.
- It’s a wonderful retelling of the story of Jesus, from a man
  who has spent, according to the author’s bio, thirty years
  studying the historical Jesus.
- There were a lot of great insights and surprises. For example,
  he notes that when Jesus cautions the Pharisees about straining
  out a gnat and swallowing a camel, he’s using a little
  wordplay. “Gnat” is `qalma`; “camel” `galma`. I really enjoy
  those kinds of insights.
- Frankly, I’m not embarrassed to say that some of them I was
  embarrassed that I didn’t know, or hadn’t thought of, or never
  even considered. Sometimes I read books on Jesus and I think,
  “How could I have missed that all these years?”
- One of Pagola’s insights was about the three disciples who are
  named today, his innermost circle—Peter, James and John.
  Pagola’s book reminds us that they can be counted as among his
  dearest friends not only because they are with him at certain
  important moments, like the Transfiguration, and the Garden of
  Gethsemane, and not only because they are among the first
  disciples he calls; but also, because he gives them
  `nicknames`.
- A few years ago, Daniel J. Harrington, SJ, the New Testament
  scholar, told me that he felt that “`Cephas`,” or “Petrus,” the
  name that Jesus to Simon is not simply a theological statement,
  as in “You’re the rock on which I will build my church,” but
  a clever nickname: Rocky, given to him because of his
  “angularity of character,” as Fr. Harrington said. He’s
  a rocky, tough, hard-headed guy and Jesus uses that
  characteristic as a way of underlining his role in the
  community.
- Also, “Boanerges,” “Sons of Thunder,” could be a nickname Jesus
  gave James and John. Maybe because they asked Jesus if they
  could call down God’s wrath on a town; or maybe because they
  asked to sit at his right hand.
- Whatever the case, we get a real sense of the intimacy Jesus
  had with these three. The three he’s known the longest, Peter
  and the brothers James and John, who he called by the Sea of
  Galilee, are the ones who get nicknames. This is the way it is
  in our day as well.
- So they know him. But like the other disciples, they still
  seem, at times, unable to understand him.
- Peter often gets things wrong about Jesus, as when he suggests
  that Jesus shouldn’t suffer. James and John argue over who will
  be greatest in heaven, certainly getting Jesus wrong. And if
  they don’t get him, the other disciples, at times, don’t get
  him even more.
- When they expect him to be tender, he is harsh. When they
  expect him to be harsh, he is tender. When they expect him to
  do one thing, he does another.
- Today the other apostles, presumably the nine, are arguing
  (either among themselves or with the scribes or with the
  crowds) about why they can’t heal the epileptic boy.
- At the time, epilepsy was seen as connected to the moon and its
  cycles; the Latin “lunatic” preserves that etymology. In the
  end, they don’t understand that this kind of demon must come
  out with prayer, as Jesus says--so presumably they’ve not been
  praying as they did the healing. 
- But they also don’t understand the pastoral need here. They’re
  arguing right in front of the desperate father and the poor
  boy. You can imagine, for a contemporary example, two pompous
  doctors arguing loudly over a child in front of the parent in
  an emergency room. It’s just terrible bedside manner.
- So Jesus shows them what to do. He speaks to the father, asks
  questions about the boy, listens to the father's answer, makes
  himself physically close to the boy, and then acts confidently.
- It’s hard to know what Jesus did that they didn’t do, other
  than not praying (and fasting), but it’s clear that they missed
  something. Something so important that he calls them
  a “faithless generation.”
- What’s the point? Well, one might be that no matter how much we
  think we know Jesus, no matter how close we are to him, even if
  we feel as close as Peter and James and John and the other
  apostles and disciples, we can always learn something.
- You read a book, you hear a homily, you go on retreat, you
  speak to a friend, you visit a spiritual director, or you just
  have an insight looking at a painting or a film or a play about
  Jesus. And you understand Jesus better. You get him better.
- It’s easy to feel stupid for not knowing something that you
  think you should have. That’s how I felt at times when I read
  that Pagola book. Every few chapters I thought, “I never
  thought of that!” The disciples probably said that to
  themselves all the time.
- But the Risen One is always ready to be with us, and ready to
  teach us something new. So we should never lose our curiosity
  about Jesus. We should never stop learning about him. And we
  should always be able to, like the disciples do hear, ask him
  questions.
- For he’s always ready to teach us.
