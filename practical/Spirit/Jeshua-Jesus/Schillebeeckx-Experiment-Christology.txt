### Why this book was written
    - Schillebeeckx is saying that the New Testament talks about
      `salvation` using ancient Jewish and Greek ideas that no
      longer make sense to us in a literal way.
    - Those ideas were shaped by their culture, just as our hopes
      and expectations today are shaped by ours.
    - What matters for Christian faith is not holding onto those
      ancient models themselves, but trusting in the concrete
      historical person Jesus of Nazareth as the source of
      salvation.
    - The early Christians used the language and images available
      to them to express what they experienced in Jesus, but
      those images are not timeless norms. If we treat those
      cultural frameworks as _absolute_, we end up replacing
      faith in Jesus with loyalty to a past religious culture
      rather than to Jesus himself.
    - Our pattern of expectation regarding salvation-for-mankind
      in no way resembles the ancient varieties, centred as they
      were on a "messiah-son of David" or a "messiah-son of man";
      and just as foreign to us is the combination of those two
      originally independent traditions.
    - That in itself is a key reason why I'm writing this book:
        - what does salvation in Jesus, coming to us from God,
          mean to us now?
    - Schillebeeckx is saying that ancient Christian ideas of
      salvation (like a coming messianic king or heavenly judge)
      no longer match how modern people actually experience hope
      and wellbeing.
    - Today, we already see real forms of healing and “wholeness”
      coming from many places—social movements, psychology,
      education, therapy, and human solidarity—not only from
      explicit religious faith.
    - This makes the old claim that “all real salvation comes
      from Jesus alone” sound implausible if it is taken in
      a narrow or exclusive way.
    - At the same time, modern culture has overtrusted science,
      technology, and economics as sources of wellbeing, and this
      has produced new forms of alienation, revealing that our
      modern idea of wellbeing is also deeply flawed.
    - The result is a double critique:
        - both ancient biblical models and modern secular models
          are inadequate on their own, and this opens space again
          for religious experience—including faith in Jesus—as
          one genuine, non-reducible source of human healing and
          meaning, alongside others.

### Salvation?
    - me:
        > "That we must look to Jesus Christ alone for every
        > facet of salvation, as a representative Christian
        > tradition has often asserted, is contradicted anyway by
        > a great many facts of modern experience..."
        - Does that mean we *should* look to Jesus alone for
          every facet of salvation or we *should not neccesarily
          look* to Jesus alone.. ?

    - Answer:
        - It *sounds* like it could be read either way. But
          Schillebeeckx’s meaning is actually quite clear once
          you slow it down.

    - Short answer first:
        - He is saying we should NOT NECESSARILY look to Jesus
          alone for every facet of salvation, if “salvation” is
          understood in a broad, concrete, human sense (healing,
          wellbeing, liberation, wholeness).
        - He is explicitly rejecting the traditional claim that
          *all* salvation, in *every* sense, comes only and
          directly from Jesus.

    - Let me explain:
        - First, what he has just argued in the paragraph before:
        - Schillebeeckx says that modern experience shows
          something undeniable:
            - many real forms of healing and wellbeing clearly
              come from non-religious sources.
            - Science, medicine, psychology, social reform,
              education, and therapy really do free people from
              suffering and alienation.
            - They work — historically, empirically, visibly.
        - These forms of healing deal with:
            - physical illness
            - psychological and psychosomatic suffering
            - social oppression and exclusion
            - loss of identity and agency

    - And they do this successfully, through human expertise and
      intervention.
    - So the old religious idea of “salvation” has shrunk in
      practice. It has “lost territory” to other effective forces
      that genuinely help people.

    - The deeper question he then raises:
        - Schillebeeckx does *not* say that this makes Jesus
          irrelevant. Instead, he asks whether there are deeper
          forms of alienation that science and technology cannot
          fully address.
        - He names these deeper levels:
            - human finitude (we are mortal, limited, vulnerable)
            - our uneasy relationship with nature (which never
              fully becomes safe or controllable)
            - guilt, moral failure, and sin
            - the limits of self-redemption (we cannot fully save
              ourselves)

At this depth, human techniques start to run out of power.

This is where Jesus becomes theologically significant — not as a competitor to medicine or psychology, but as someone who speaks to ultimate freedom, meaning, reconciliation, and relationship to God.

Third, now the key sentence you asked about:

“That we must look to Jesus Christ alone for every facet of salvation … is contradicted anyway by a great many facts of modern experience.”

This sentence is not saying:
“Despite appearances, we really should still look only to Jesus.”

It is saying the opposite:
The claim that *everything* counts as salvation *only* if it comes from Jesus is factually false.

Why?
Because modern experience clearly shows:
• people are healed
• people are liberated
• people become more whole
without explicit reference to Jesus or Christianity.

That is why he says this traditional assertion has become implausible and confusing for many Christians.

Fourth, what Schillebeeckx is *actually* trying to do:

He is trying to redefine salvation, not deny it.

His position is roughly this:

- Salvation is broader than religion
- Jesus is not the sole cause of every kind of human wellbeing
- But Jesus may still be decisive at the deepest level of human existence
- Especially where questions of ultimate meaning, guilt, finitude, and freedom arise

So Jesus is not the *exclusive supplier* of all healing,
but neither is he reduced to just one helpful figure among others.

That tension — refusing both exclusivism and reductionism — is exactly why he is writing the book.
