# Summary

### 1. Karl Barth:
    - Barth emphasized Jesus' divine mission and unique
      relationship with the Father, but avoided speculating on
      the specifics of Jesus’ self-consciousness as God.

### 2. Dietrich Bonhoeffer:
    - Bonhoeffer focused on Jesus' obedience to the Father and
      his role as the revelation of God, without delving into the
      psychological aspects of Jesus' divine self-awareness.

### 3. Hans Urs von Balthasar:
    - Balthasar believed Jesus was aware of his divine mission,
      particularly in his passion, but viewed the details of his
      self-consciousness as a mystery.

### 4. Jürgen Moltmann:
    - Moltmann stressed Jesus’ relational knowledge of his divine
      mission through his suffering and hope, but did not explore
      his inner self-awareness as God.

### 5. Henri de Lubac:
    - De Lubac upheld the traditional Catholic view that Jesus
      was fully aware of his divine mission, but saw the
      specifics of his divine self-consciousness as ultimately
      mysterious.

### 6. Reinhold Niebuhr:
    - Niebuhr emphasized the ethical implications of Jesus' life,
      suggesting Jesus knew his divine mission, but refrained
      from speculating on his inner awareness as divine.

### 7. John Paul II:
    - John Paul II affirmed Jesus' full divinity and humanity,
      holding that Jesus had a divine awareness through the
      hypostatic union, though his human mind may not have fully
      grasped it.

### 8. Stanley Hauerwas:
    - Hauerwas focused on Jesus' ethical actions and relationship
      with the Father, without delving into the metaphysical
      details of Jesus’ self-consciousness as divine.

### 9. James H. Cone:
    - Cone emphasized Jesus' identification with the oppressed
      and his divine mission, but did not explore the specifics
      of Jesus’ self-awareness as God.

### 10. Sarah Coakley:
    - Coakley viewed Jesus’ divine self-consciousness as kenotic
      (self-emptying) and relational, but approached the issue as
      a mystery grounded in faith and contemplation.




### 1. Karl Barth
    - View on Jesus' self-consciousness: Barth, in his Church
      Dogmatics, emphasized that Jesus is the "God-man," fully
      divine and fully human. However, Barth was cautious about
      speculating too much on the inner consciousness of Jesus.
      For Barth, what is important is that Jesus acts in full
      obedience to the Father, demonstrating the divine will. He
      suggested that Jesus’ self-awareness as the Son of God was
      part of his unique relationship with the Father, but Barth
      did not delve into a psychological understanding of Jesus'
      awareness. He would likely assert that while Jesus was
      fully divine, how that divinity was understood in his human
      mind is ultimately a mystery beyond our grasp.

### 2. Dietrich Bonhoeffer
    - View on Jesus' self-consciousness: Bonhoeffer focused more
      on Jesus' obedience and his call to discipleship than on
      the metaphysics of Jesus' self-awareness. In Christ the
      Center, he emphasizes that Jesus is the revelation of God
      and the center of all human existence. Bonhoeffer does not
      provide a detailed answer to the question of Jesus’
      self-awareness as divine, but he strongly affirms both
      Jesus' full divinity and humanity. His Christology tends to
      focus on Jesus' mission and relational obedience to the
      Father, rather than psychological self-awareness.

### 3. Hans Urs von Balthasar
    - View on Jesus' self-consciousness: Balthasar, in works like
      Mysterium Paschale and Theo-Drama, deals deeply with
      Christ's role in salvation. He takes a mystical and
      dramatic view of Jesus' life, death, and resurrection.
      Balthasar suggested that Jesus had an awareness of his
      divine mission, particularly in his passion, and saw the
      cross as the culmination of his self-giving love as God.
      However, Balthasar maintained a sense of mystery about how
      Jesus' divine and human natures interacted within his
      self-consciousness, suggesting that while Jesus was aware
      of his divine mission, his human mind may not have fully
      comprehended it in a way we can easily articulate.

### 4. Jürgen Moltmann
    - View on Jesus' self-consciousness: Moltmann, in The
      Crucified God, emphasizes Jesus' experience of abandonment
      on the cross as a moment of profound identification with
      human suffering. He views the cross as the central
      revelation of God's nature. Moltmann’s focus is on the
      relational dynamic between the Father and the Son,
      especially in the context of suffering. He does not
      explicitly discuss Jesus’ self-consciousness as divine but
      highlights the relational and eschatological dimensions of
      Jesus' life. Moltmann might suggest that Jesus knew his
      divine mission as part of his relationship with the Father,
      but his understanding of himself as divine in his human
      consciousness is a divine mystery.

### 5. Henri de Lubac
    - View on Jesus' self-consciousness: As a major figure in
      ressourcement theology, de Lubac was cautious about
      speculative theology. He emphasized the mystery of the
      incarnation and upheld traditional Catholic teaching about
      Jesus' two natures. De Lubac likely held that Jesus, in his
      human nature, was fully conscious of his divine mission,
      but how this self-awareness manifested is something that
      cannot be fully known from a human perspective. He
      emphasized Christ's role in bringing humanity into
      communion with God but would likely affirm the traditional
      Catholic view that Jesus' human mind may not have fully
      grasped his divine identity in a way that could be easily
      understood.

### 6. Reinhold Niebuhr
    - View on Jesus' self-consciousness: Niebuhr, known for his
      emphasis on Christian realism, did not dwell extensively on
      the inner consciousness of Jesus. He was more focused on
      the ethical implications of Christ's life and teachings,
      especially in relation to human sinfulness. Niebuhr would
      likely hold that Jesus, as the incarnate Son of God, had
      a unique awareness of his divine mission. However, he might
      refrain from making specific claims about Jesus’
      self-consciousness as God, focusing instead on Jesus'
      ethical actions and relationship with humanity as evidence
      of his divine nature.

### 7. John Paul II
    - View on Jesus' self-consciousness: John Paul II,
      particularly in his Theology of the Body and Redemptor
      Hominis, emphasized the mystery of the incarnation and the
      unity of Christ's divine and human natures. As a Roman
      Catholic theologian, John Paul II would hold to the
      traditional teaching that Jesus is fully divine and fully
      human, and while he may have had human limitations in his
      earthly mind, he always possessed divine knowledge through
      the hypostatic union. John Paul II would likely argue that
      Jesus had an awareness of his divine mission, but the full
      scope of his divine self-consciousness in his human mind is
      a mystery left to faith.

### 8. Stanley Hauerwas
    - View on Jesus' self-consciousness: Hauerwas, in works like
      The Peaceable Kingdom and Resident Aliens, does not focus
      on the metaphysics of Jesus' self-consciousness. His work
      emphasizes Jesus' ethical teachings and the role of the
      Church as a counter-cultural community. Hauerwas would
      likely affirm that Jesus knew his mission and his
      relationship to the Father, but his focus would be on how
      Jesus lived out his divine calling through his actions,
      rather than on his inner psychological awareness.

### 9. James H. Cone
    - View on Jesus' self-consciousness: Cone, as the founder of
      Black Liberation Theology, focused on Jesus as the
      liberator of the oppressed. He emphasized Jesus'
      identification with the suffering and marginalized. While
      Cone firmly believed in Jesus' divinity, he did not focus
      on Jesus’ self-consciousness as divine. His theological
      emphasis was more on what Jesus did—how he lived and died
      for the oppressed—than on speculative questions about
      Jesus’ awareness of his divinity.
