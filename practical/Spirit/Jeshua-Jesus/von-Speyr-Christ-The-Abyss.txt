
# Adrienne von Speyr — Christ's Prayer, Gethsemane, the Cross
& Holy Saturday

## 1. The basic question

Von Speyr's mystical theology presents Christ not simply as the
divine Son executing a known programme of salvation, but as the
**incarnate Son genuinely living a human existence** — including
a genuinely human will, genuine suffering, genuine anguish, and
the experience of abandonment.

This gives her account of the Passion a strikingly different
character from presentations in which:

> Jesus knows exactly what is going to happen, knows He will rise
> on the third day, understands the divine plan completely, and
> therefore simply carries it out despite the pain.

For von Speyr, the mystery is much deeper:

> **The eternal Son really enters into the human condition from
> within.**

---

# 2. Gethsemane — the human will of Christ

The crucial movement is:

    "Not my will, but yours be done."

Jesus genuinely desires that the cup might pass from Him:

    "Father ... if it be possible, let this cup pass from me."

The request is not theatrical.

Christ's human will genuinely recoils from death.

Yet He gives that very human will back to the Father.

### Von Speyr's central insight

The surrender is not:

> "I know exactly what will happen, so I can safely agree to it."

It is closer to:

> **"I genuinely desire another way; nevertheless, I place even
> that desire into Your hands."**

Thus:

    human desire ↓ "take this cup from me" ↓ surrender of that
    desire ↓ "not my will, but Yours" ↓ complete availability to
    the Father

This is why Gethsemane is not merely Christ "following orders".

It is the **radical surrender of His human will**.

---

# 3. Knowing vs. experiencing

A crucial qualification:

Von Speyr should NOT simply be reduced to:

> "Jesus did not know He would rise."

Her Christology retains the mystery of the incarnate Son's divine
knowledge and His profound consciousness of the Father and His
mission.

The more subtle claim is:

> **Divine knowledge does not cancel genuine human experience.**

Christ can possess profound knowledge of the Father and still
enter into:

- anguish
- darkness
- abandonment
- the horror of death
- surrender
- silence.

Thus:

> **Jesus' knowledge does not function as a psychological
> safety-net protecting Him from the full existential reality of
> suffering and death.**

Knowledge does not cancel suffering.

Knowledge does not cancel surrender.

---

# 4. Why this changes the meaning of "Thy will be done"

For von Speyr, Jesus does not merely teach:

    "Thy will be done on earth as it is in heaven."

He **lives** that prayer.

Gethsemane is the prayer made existentially real.

The Our Father becomes:

> not merely a formula Jesus gives to His disciples, but
> something Jesus Himself embodies.

The deepest meaning of:

    Thy will be done

is therefore revealed in:

    Not my will, but Yours.

This is not passive resignation.

It is **filial surrender**.

---

# 5. The contrast with a "pre-programmed" Passion

A flattened picture:

    Jesus receives the divine plan ↓ knows the whole sequence
    ↓ knows Easter is guaranteed ↓ deliberately carries out the
    programme ↓ suffers, but knows everything will be all right

Von Speyr's picture:

    The eternal Son becomes man ↓ assumes a genuine human will
    ↓ genuinely recoils from death ↓ asks that the cup pass
    ↓ does not impose His desired outcome ↓ surrenders His human
    will to the Father ↓ allows Himself to be handed over
    ↓ enters suffering, abandonment and death ↓ becomes utterly
    receptive

The difference is enormous.

The Passion becomes not the execution of a known programme but an
**act of radical filial trust**.

---

# 6. From Gethsemane to the Cross

There is a profound progression:

### Gethsemane

    "Father..."

    "Take this cup from me."

    "Not my will, but Yours."

Movement:

    desire → anguish → surrender → obedience

### Cross

    "My God, my God, why have You forsaken me?"

Now the movement is:

    abandonment → darkness → continued prayer

The form of address itself becomes significant.

In Gethsemane:

    Father

On the Cross:

    My God

Von Speyr's interpretation emphasises that the **experience of
the Father's presence disappears**.

This does NOT mean:

- the Father ceases to be Father;
- the Son ceases to be the Son;
- the Trinity is actually divided.

Rather:

> **Christ's human experience enters into the absolute darkness
> of God-forsakenness.**

---

# 7. The extraordinary fact: He still prays

This may be one of von Speyr's deepest insights.

The ordinary intuition is:

    I experience God → therefore I can pray.

Christ on the Cross reveals the opposite:

    I no longer experience God's presence → and yet I continue to
    address God.

Prayer therefore reaches its absolute limit.

The cry:

    "My God, my God, why have You forsaken me?"

is simultaneously:

- genuine anguish;
- genuine abandonment;
- an address to God;
- the beginning of Psalm 22;
- continued fidelity.

Even when God seems absent:

> **"My" remains.**

Christ still belongs to the Father even when the Father is no
longer experienced as present.

---

# 8. Holy Saturday — the descent into the abyss

The movement does not stop at the Cross.

Von Speyr's Holy Saturday theology takes seriously:

    "He descended into hell."

This is not merely:

    death → waiting → Sunday

but:

    Cross ↓ death ↓ descent into the realm of the dead ↓ the
    extremity of abandonment ↓ silence ↓ Resurrection

The crucial claim:

> **Christ actually enters death.**

He does not merely stand outside the abyss and rescue people from
it.

> **He goes into the abyss.**

---

# 9. Holy Saturday as the "underside" of the Cross

Good Friday:

    Christ gives everything.

Holy Saturday:

    Christ has given everything.

On the Cross there is still:

- speech
- prayer
- conscious action
- surrender.

On Holy Saturday:

> **silence**

Christ is dead.

His obedience becomes an:

> **"obedience of a corpse."**

This is obedience reduced to pure receptivity.

There is nothing left to do.

Nothing left to control.

Nothing left to accomplish.

The Son has handed Himself over completely.

---

# 10. The terrifying depth of the descent

Von Speyr/Balthasar understand Christ's descent as an entry into
the condition of those who are God-forsaken.

Not because Christ is sinful.

Not because the Father rejects the Son.

Not because the Trinity is broken.

Rather:

> **The sinless Son enters, from within, into the condition
> produced by sin: separation, abandonment, and the loss of God's
> experienced presence.**

This is why "abyss" is such an apt word.

The movement of the Incarnation becomes:

    eternity ↓ humanity ↓ suffering ↓ Gethsemane ↓ abandonment
    ↓ death ↓ the realm of the dead ↓ the abyss

There is no point at which Christ says:

> "This is as far as I go."

He goes all the way down.

---

# 11. "Loss of glory"

Von Speyr's language becomes extraordinarily radical here.

Christ enters into the experience of the:

> **loss of glory**

Again, this is not ontological separation from the Father.

The eternal Son remains the Son.

The Father remains Father.

The Trinity remains Trinity.

But in His human condition Christ enters into the experience of
what it means for the creature to be:

> **God-forsaken.**

Thus:

    Ontologically: Father and Son remain united.

    Experientially: Christ enters the deepest darkness of
    abandonment.

The two are held together without being reduced to one another.

---

# 12. Holy Saturday and the absence of "psychological safety"

This connects directly with Gethsemane.

The usual psychological picture might be:

> "Friday is terrible, but Jesus knows Sunday is coming."

Von Speyr's Holy Saturday pushes against this.

The mystery is:

> **Christ surrenders the outcome itself.**

He does not cling psychologically to:

    "I know exactly what happens next."

He remains surrendered to the Father.

There is no grasping backwards.

There is no grasping forwards.

There is simply:

> **obedience in the present.**

Hence the profound paradox:

> **The Son's obedience becomes absolute precisely when there is
> nothing left for Him to do.**

---

# 13. "Neither looking back nor looking ahead"

Holy Saturday can therefore be understood as:

    no looking backwards no looking forwards no control no action
    no felt consolation no visible triumph

Only:

    surrender

The Son has entrusted Himself completely to the Father.

And then:

    silence.

This makes Holy Saturday perhaps the most radical expression of
Christ's obedience.

---

# 14. Hell — what is the abyss?

For von Speyr, hell is not fundamentally about imagery of fire.

At its deepest level it is:

> **the loss of God.**

The horror is not merely:

    pain

but:

    "I have lost God."

And worse:

    "I have lost God forever."

Hell is therefore the absolute negation of communion.

The abyss is the place where the creature's "No" to God reaches
its ultimate extremity.

---

# 15. And yet Christ enters there

This is the astonishing Christian paradox:

> **The Son enters the place where God seems absent.**

Or:

> **Love enters the place where love has been rejected.**

Christ does not save from a safe distance.

He does not merely understand abandonment.

He enters it.

He does not merely understand death.

He dies.

He does not merely speak about the lost.

He descends to the place of the lost.

Thus:

> **There is nowhere human beings can go where Christ has not
> already gone.**

---

# 16. The deepest paradox

The Christian mystery becomes:

    The Son remains eternally united to the Father AND the
    incarnate Son enters genuine human God-forsakenness.

Theologically:

    no rupture in the Trinity

but experientially:

    real abandonment

This is precisely why the theology is so difficult.

It refuses two easy solutions:

### Too weak:

> "Jesus only felt a little abandoned, while secretly knowing
> everything was fine."

### Too strong:

> "The Father literally ceased to be united to the Son."

Von Speyr instead holds the paradox:

> **The eternal unity remains; the human experience of
> abandonment is nevertheless real.**

---

# 17. Why Easter becomes so much more radical

If Holy Saturday is merely:

    body in tomb
    + soul waiting
    + Sunday arrives

then Resurrection is simply the next stage.

But if Holy Saturday is:

> **the Son entering the absolute depth of death and
> God-forsakenness**

then Resurrection becomes something vastly greater.

The movement is:

    divine life ↓ enters death ↓ enters abandonment ↓ enters the
    abyss ↓ remains surrendered ↓ Resurrection

The abyss is real.

But:

> **the abyss is not deeper than God.**

Death is real.

But:

> **death does not have the final word.**

Abandonment is real.

But:

> **divine love reaches further down than abandonment.**

---

# 18. The heart of the whole progression

The whole Passion can therefore be contemplated as a progressive
surrender:

    Gethsemane "Not my will, but Yours."

            ↓

    Cross "My God, my God, why have You forsaken me?"

            ↓

    Death complete surrender

            ↓

    Holy Saturday silence absence passivity "obedience of
    a corpse"

            ↓

    Descent Christ enters the abyss of God-forsakenness

            ↓

    Resurrection the Father's life reaches into death and raises
    the Son

The key is that **Christ goes all the way down**.

---

# 19. The theological significance

Von Speyr's Christ is therefore not less divine because He is
portrayed as vulnerable.

Quite the opposite.

Her understanding of the Incarnation is:

> **God does not merely come close to human suffering. God enters
> it.**

And not merely suffering:

> **God enters death.**

And not merely death:

> **God enters the experience of abandonment.**

And not merely abandonment:

> **God enters the abyss.**

This is why her Holy Saturday theology can feel
"earth-shattering".

The claim is not merely:

> Christ knows what suffering feels like.

It is:

> **Christ has entered the furthest place to which human
> existence can descend.**

---

# 20. The great paradox of Christian hope

The deepest hope is therefore not:

> "Things will probably turn out all right."

It is:

> **Christ has already gone into the place where there seemed to
> be no hope at all.**

He has entered:

    anguish abandonment death silence the realm of the dead the
    abyss

And therefore Easter is not God's avoidance of the abyss.

It is:

> **God's victory from within the abyss.**

The darkness is real.

The silence is real.

The death is real.

The abandonment is real.

But they are not ultimate.

---

# 21. One sentence to retain

If the entire von Speyrian vision had to be reduced to one
thought:

> **Christ does not save humanity by standing safely outside the
> abyss and reaching down to us; He saves us by entering the
> abyss Himself — all the way to death, all the way to
> God-forsakenness — and discovering there, in absolute surrender
> to the Father, that even the abyss cannot separate the Son from
> divine love.**

Or more simply:

> **He goes all the way down.**

And Easter means:

> **the Father's love goes all the way down with Him.**
