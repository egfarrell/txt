# Me:
    - I'm not questioning Jeshua as the Messiah, Son of God,
      second person in the Trinity but I'm wondering:
        - Was Jeshua more earthy, mixed, muddled, HUMAN than we
          sometimes give credit for?
        - Did any theologians examine this?

# Analysis
    - Yes. This has been taken seriously by a number of modern
      theologians. They usually approach it carefully, because
      classical doctrine (especially after Chalcedon) draws hard
      boundaries around “sinlessness.”
    - But many have probed exactly the human depth,
      vulnerability, and developmental reality that your question
      is getting at — including the baptism.

    - First, a key distinction many theologians make:
        - sinlessness does not necessarily mean psychological
          completeness, moral omniscience, or finished
          self-understanding at every moment.
        - Hebrews already opens this door: Jesus “learned
          obedience through what he suffered.”
        - Learning implies process.

### Karl Rahner
    - Rahner strongly emphasized Jesus’ genuine human
      consciousness. He argued that Jesus’ human self-awareness
      unfolded historically, within time, culture, and
      experience. Rahner did not deny sinlessness, but he
      rejected the idea that Jesus walked around with constant,
      explicit divine self-knowledge. The baptism, for Rahner,
      can be understood as a decisive moment of vocational
      clarification — not repentance for sin, but radical
      openness to God’s will emerging within a human life.

### Edward Schillebeeckx
    - Schillebeeckx went further in stressing Jesus’ full human
      solidarity. He described Jesus as a person of profound
      `God-trust` rather than metaphysical self-certainty.
    - The baptism functions as a turning point: Jesus publicly
      aligns himself with Israel’s repentance and suffering.
      Schillebeeckx avoids saying Jesus was sinful, but he does
      suggest Jesus entered into ambiguity, risk, and genuine
      existential decision.

### James Dunn
    - Dunn explored early Christology and argued that Jesus
      likely experienced his mission as something he discerned,
      not something fully possessed from childhood.
    - Dunn takes the baptism seriously as a moment of
      commissioning and empowerment by the Spirit.
    - This does not require sin, but it does imply that Jesus’
      human life involved growth, discernment, and responsiveness
      — not preprogrammed certainty.

### Hans Küng
    - He explicitly challenged overly abstract accounts of
      sinlessness. He argued Jesus’ moral authority does not come
      from exemption from struggle, but from radical faithfulness
      within struggle.
    - Küng was willing to say that Jesus shared fully in human
      moral tension and temptation — real temptation, not
      play-acting. He remained careful not to assert actual sin,
      but he clearly rejected the “untouchable superhero” Jesus.

### Rowan Williams
    - Williams often speaks of Jesus’ “risk” — that the
      Incarnation means real exposure to misunderstanding, fear,
      and apparent failure.
    - For Williams, Jesus’ baptism is an _act of solidarity_ that
      risks being misread, even by Jesus’ contemporaries. That
      risk is part of what makes his obedience real.

- What almost none of these theologians say
    - Very few mainstream theologians explicitly say “Jesus
      sinned.” That would collide directly with creedal
      Christianity. But many of them quietly dismantle the
      thinner assumptions that often sneak in under the word
      “sinlessness,” such as:
        - Jesus always knew exactly who he was in a clear,
          conceptual way
        - Jesus never experienced moral uncertainty or emotional
          conflict
        - Jesus’ obedience required no discernment or struggle

- In that light, the baptism can be read not as Jesus repenting
  of sin, but as Jesus entering fully into a human posture of
  conversion, openness, and surrender — something a sinless
  person could still do if they are genuinely human.

- Your instinct about Jesus being “earthy, mixed, muddled, HUMAN”
  is actually close to where a lot of serious 20th-century
  Christology landed:
    - safeguarding divinity not by shrinking humanity, but by
      letting it be real like ours — growth, fear, prayer...


Theologians on the edge

- Some figures who really do press right up against the
  edge,sometimes stepping over it in the eyes of church
  authorities, precisely on the question of Jesus’ humanity,
  development, struggle, and the meaning of “sinlessness.”
- None of them are casual or sensational; they’re careful,
  serious thinkers who deliberately risk trouble.

### Piet Schoonenberg
    - Schoonenberg argued that traditional language about Jesus’
      sinlessness often smuggles in an inhuman moral perfection.
      He suggested rethinking sin not as isolated moral failure
      but as alienation from God. On that definition, Jesus
      remains faithful — but Schoonenberg allows for genuine
      limitation, conflict, and historical conditioning in Jesus’
      moral life. This raised alarms because it loosens the tight
      equation between sinlessness and flawlessness.

### José Antonio Pagola
    - $MY_NOTES/practical/Spirit/Jeshua-Jesus/Pagola-Yeshua-Historical-Approximation.txt
    - Pagola presents Jesus as a Spirit-driven Galilean prophet
      whose identity unfolds from experience rather than
      metaphysical self-knowledge.
    - The baptism is central: Jesus discovers his vocation in the
      Jordan. Pagola is careful with language, but critics argue
      that his Jesus looks dangerously close to a human being who
      “becomes” Son rather than simply expressing an eternal
      identity.

### • Roger Haight
    - Haight explicitly reconceived Christology “from below.” He
      argued that Jesus’ unity with God is relational and
      historical, not a constant exercise of divine attributes.
      Rome formally censured him. Haight never claims Jesus
      sinned, but he insists that Jesus’ faith, obedience, and
      trust were genuinely human acts, carried out without
      guarantees. That makes Jesus’ life morally risky in a way
      classical formulas tend to flatten.

### • Eberhard Jüngel
    - Jüngel pushed Lutheran theology to radical conclusions. He
      spoke of God’s self-identification with the godless and the
      abandoned.
    - In Jüngel’s framework, Jesus enters fully into humanity’s
      distance from God — including God-forsakenness. While he
      does not say Jesus sins, his theology comes uncomfortably
      close to saying Jesus inhabits the condition of sinners
      from the inside, not merely as a representative.

### • Dorothee Sölle
    - Sölle rejected metaphysical Christology almost entirely.
      Her Jesus is radically human, politically engaged,
      vulnerable, and exposed to failure. For her, insisting too
      strongly on sinlessness risks denying the Incarnation
      itself. Many consider her well beyond orthodoxy, but she
      articulates very clearly the intuition you voiced: a Jesus
      who is too “pure” becomes unreal.

### • John Hick
    - Hick went the furthest. He explicitly denied classical
      doctrines of incarnation and sinlessness, interpreting
      Jesus as a uniquely God-conscious human being rather than
      ontologically divine.
    - Hick is outside orthodox Christianity, but his work is
      important because it shows where the internal logic of
      emphasizing Jesus’ humanity can lead if the brakes are
      fully released.

- A key pattern you might notice
- What separates “edge-of-orthodoxy” thinkers from outright
  rejection is usually this line:
    - Jesus may share fully in human limitation, development,
      ignorance, temptation, fear, and ambiguity — but not in
      alienation from God.

- Once a theologian allows even a hint of that alienation, they
  cross from reformulation into rupture. Most of the thinkers
  above hover precisely at that boundary, trying to preserve real
  humanity without collapsing the confession that “God was in
  Christ.”

- Why the baptism keeps coming back
    - The Jordan scene is so dangerous precisely because it looks
      like conversion. These theologians take that danger
      seriously rather than explaining it away.
    - They ask whether repentance can mean radical openness
      rather than moral guilt — and whether a sinless Jesus could
      still need transformation, vocation, and surrender.

- Your instinct here is not marginal. It’s one of the deepest
  fault lines in modern Christology:
    - whether we protect Jesus’ divinity by insulating him from
      human messiness, or by trusting that God can be fully
      present in a life that genuinely grows, risks, and decides.

- If you want to go one step further, the next question many of
  these theologians ask is whether Chalcedon answers this tension
  — or quietly freezes it.
