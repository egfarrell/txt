- This French priest who took the road less travelled — married
  twice, ordained at 65 — has become the tireless advocate of
  Jerzy Popieluszko, a martyr of Polish communism who may soon be
  canonized thanks to a miracle in France.

- With his little grey mustache, his jovial manner and the
  repartee of a former salesman chatting up clients, Bernard
  Brien sets you at ease. Seeing him slice the Sunday roast like
  any suburban dad, it is hard to imagine the circuitous path
  that led this apparently ordinary man to the priesthood. If we
  bring it up, he brushes it aside with a smile, like a child who
  just pulled a fast one, before changing the subject to last
  night’s football game.

- And yet, Bernard Brien — or Father Bernard, as we see by the
  cross pinned on his shirt — is no ordinary priest. First,
  because he was ordained late in life, at 65 years old, an age
  when his friends and former colleagues were getting ready to
  enjoy retirement. Also because before this unexpected calling
  brought him to Nogent-sur-Marne, this man "Brittany born and
  bred" was married twice, fathered two sons, blessed with four
  grandchildren, and divorced twice.

- And, to top it all off, shortly after being named chaplain for
  the hospitals of Creteil, near Paris, he found himself at the
  origin, so to speak, of the miracle that could advance the
  cause of the Blessed Jerzy Popieluszko, famous martyr of Polish
  communism and subject of a remarkable biography by Father
  Bernard. You see, it was Father Bernard's idea to call on the
  intercession of the former chaplain of Solidarity at the
  bedside of a patient suffering from a rare form of leukemia.
  That was on Sept. 14, 2012, the priest's birthday, and that of
  Father Popieluszko, born the same day. The dying patient
  recovered and the doctors, stupefied, concluded it was "an
  unexplained recovery."

- Truth be told, it is not the first time we have heard of this
  miracle; Father Bernard had already accepted to share it after
  several months of cautious silence. He is more reticent,
  however, to talk publicly about his past life. It generated
  fierce resistance from the church: refusal from a first bishop,
  rejection by certain colleagues and even a letter asking Bishop
  Michel Santier of Creteil, whom he had known in Vendee (Western
  France), not to ordain him.

- "This story of a divorced, remarried man was not acceptable,"
  he figures, happily surprised that this miracle had the effect
  of "validating" his ordination as well as the "courage" of
  Bishop Santier.

- "He took a risk with me. At the beginning, he warned me: 'this
  is going to be complicated,' but several months later — and
  after the canonists he consulted gave the green light — he told
  me: 'I accept you as you are,'" recalls the priest.

- Father Brien said he was sometimes tempted to give up, "but
  something inside me said not to let go."

- Today, the 68-year-old Father Bernard has found his place. The
  second of nine children, his itinerary is still a source of
  bewilderment to his family.

- "They knew me as a successful businessman, when I used to drive
  a Porsche," he says. Even today, when he is invited to speak in
  parishes, the question still "spins" in his head: "Why did the
  Lord choose me, when he knew my tumultuous background?"

- Some people in his entourage saw a sign for divorced and
  remarried people, well before the end of the Synod on the
  Family where this issue generated lively debate. He refuses to
  draw hasty conclusions. "I am not in favor of distributing the
  Eucharist to everyone, but providing an accompaniment and
  authorizing people to take part in sacraments again, why not,
  on a case by case basis. … This is what Pope Francis seems to
  be saying, if we read between the lines."

- The question remains, why him? The only valid response he can
  come up with is inspired by the pope, to whom he feels very
  close: “It is all about mercy. I think about Jesus with the
  adulterous woman, the Samaritan, Zachariah … He was very
  merciful with me too. Even if I am one hell of a square peg,”
  he sums up with his trademark vernacular.

- Paradoxically, it is these simple words that make him so
  convincing when asked about his conversion. Returning from
  a business trip in 2004, Bernard Brien comes home to an empty
  house in Les Sables-d'Olonne. His second wife had cleared out
  while he was gone.

- "I was devastated. Everything we had built together was all so
  meaningless. I took a sheet of paper and started to take stock
  of my life, going right back to my childhood: my baptism,
  catechism. … I remembered that I was happy then, and that I was
  angry with God for having waited so long to send me a sign. All
  those lost years. But in fact it was me who was blind to his
  presence."

- A few days later, he stops in a chapel where he stays "hooked"
  for one hour in front of the Blessed Sacrament, filled with
  a new kind of peace. One year later, in the same chapel, he
  hears the call to become a priest, convinced now that "we
  cannot find happiness by climbing the social ladder but by
  climbing down to serve others."

- Father Bernard draws his strength from his prodigal son story.
  His two marriage experiences and the pain he suffered infuse
  his priestly mission. At the hospital, his humanity and his
  sense of contact draw him close to others. He is sought out by
  patients and medical staff alike. He who suffered from the
  "Pharisees" who considered the church as customs duty
  instinctively understands those who are not practicing
  Catholics and are hurt by this moralizing: "I was like you for
  40 years," he answers, before offering them a chance to live
  their faith more fully.

- "I always have my calling card with me. Sometimes a person will
  say 'no' one week, then call back the next for the Sacrament of
  the Sick,” he says. One Friday, he is called to the palliative
  care unit. The husband would like his dying wife to receive the
  sacrament, but she obstinately refuses. “She was still lucid,
  we talked about it. She had rejected many things to do with her
  illness. … After about 10 minutes, she accepted. Her husband
  and I each held one of her hands during the prayer. Shortly
  after I left, she let go and died,” he says, his voice thick
  with emotion.

- This talkative priest, grounded in common sense, loves to
  answer questions with this kind of anecdote. Miracles? For him
  they are found in everyday encounters, moments when this man
  who “acts on instinct” tries to let himself be guided by the
  Holy Spirit. Since the miracle of Father Popieluszko, for whom
  he has become a tireless advocate, carrying a relic at all
  times, Father Brien is submerged by prayer requests. Letters,
  emails, even people knocking at his door. This straight-talking
  “young” priest shoots from the hip, often outraged by the
  church’s administrative foot-dragging or its clericalism,
  bemoaning that it does not listen enough to its followers’
  hardships, that we “do not pray enough for vocations,” that
  there is “not enough conviviality.”

- His journey back to his faith has not changed the man: his
  feisty character remains intact. "Sometimes when I think back
  on my day, I realize that I did some crappy things." Prayer is
  now the rudder that steers this boat enthusiast's life. He is
  convinced that "it isn't the miracles that have dried up, but
  our faith. Very often, we are still blind."
