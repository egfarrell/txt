### Hans Urs von Balthasar
    - While Balthasar did not write extensively about Joan, he
      invoked her in thematic contexts. In Theo-Drama, Volume II:
        - "She (Joan) stands as a dramatic figure... seized by
          the divine call, acting against all worldly logic...
          Her mission is not explained by sociology, but by
          obedience to divine initiative."
    - He viewed her life as theo-dramatic, meaning her role
      unfolded in the interplay of divine freedom and human
      response—a living drama of grace and vocation.

### Pope John Paul II
    - In an address to French pilgrims on May 10, 1982
      (celebrating her 550th anniversary):
    - "Joan, the saint of patriotism and freedom of conscience,
      reminds us that holiness is possible in political and
      military engagement, when conscience is obedient to God."
    - Also said:
        - "She shows how the Spirit breathes where it wills, even
          in the most unlikely figures: a peasant girl becomes
          the standard-bearer of divine justice."

### Pope Benedict XV
    - In the 1920 canonization homily:
        - "Joan of Arc, led by heavenly voices, fulfilled
          a divine mission. She placed her trust not in sword or
          might, but in the justice of her cause and the will of
          God."
    - He emphasized her faith, humility, and courage as exemplary
      virtues for all Christians:
        - "Let all learn from this humble virgin that God exalts
          the lowly and confounds the proud."

### George H. Tavard (1922–2007):
    - A modern Catholic theologian, Tavard explored Joan’s
      spirituality in his book The Spiritual Way of St. Jeanne
      d’Arc. He described her life as a "perfect example of the
      conjunction of contemplation and action," highlighting the
      unity of her spiritual visions and her practical efforts to
      liberate France.
    - Tavard emphasized her deep faith and her commitment to
      _uniting heaven and earth_ through her mission.

### Simone Weil (1909–1943):
    - While primarily a philosopher, Weil’s reflections on Joan
      of Arc in 1943, during her time in London, carry
      theological weight due to her deep Christian mysticism.
      Weil saw Joan as a model of Christian patriotism, loving
      her country not as an idol but with compassion for its
      people’s suffering. She contrasted Joan’s faith-driven
      patriotism with the failures of the modern French
      nation-state during World War II, noting that Joan’s heart
      belonged first to God, avoiding idolatrous nationalism.

### Modern Theological Reflections:
    - Some contemporary theologians and scholars, such as those cited
      in modern analyses, have debated whether Joan’s visions were
      divine or possibly the result of neurological conditions like
      epilepsy or schizophrenia. However, Catholic theologians
      generally affirm her sanctity, focusing on her unwavering
      faith, humility, and martyrdom. For example, posts on platforms
      like Reddit’s r/CatholicPhilosophy highlight her canonization
      as evidence of her holiness, emphasizing her personal piety
      over her military actions.

    - The Catholic Church’s official stance, as reflected in sources
      like Catholic Online and Franciscan Media, underscores Joan’s
      extreme piety, her commitment to God’s will, and her role as
      a model of faith and courage. Her canonization was based on her
      holiness, not her military victories
