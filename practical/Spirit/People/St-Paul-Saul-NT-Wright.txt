
- An Evening with NT Wright: Paul
    - https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6Dv2vewlFyI

- Why write a biography of Paul?
    - Among most significant public intellectuals in history.
    - Wrongly quarantined in “religion departments”; he belongs
      equally in politics, culture, economics, community
      formation, and public life.
    - Paul not merely a “religious figure” but
      a world-interpreting and world-remaking thinker:
        - Articulates a new vision of reality.
        - Embodies it by founding, shaping, correcting, and
          sustaining communities that “experiment” with a new way
          of being human.
    - Paul stands at a major historical transition-point:
    - Holds together Jewish world (scripture, covenant,
      monotheism),
    - Greek world (philosophy, moral discourse),
    - Roman world (empire, roads, power).
    - Paul’s “new synthesis” comes from the gospel: 
        - Jesus the crucified-and-risen Messiah is Lord of the
          world.

- Paul and Israel’s story
    - Paul insists: the Jesus-event is the fulfillment of
      Israel’s vocation, though in a shocking/unexpected form.
    - Echoes the Emmaus pattern: scripture only makes full sense
      after Messiah’s suffering and vindication.
    - The gospel generates the `energy` for Paul’s work and
      willingness to suffer.

- Paul and philosophy
    - Because Israel’s God is Lord of all, Paul believes every
      worldview must be rethought: “take every thought captive”
      to Messiah.
    - He can “outflank” Stoics/Epicureans/Platonists:
    - Sometimes sounds like them, but his larger framework
      reasserts itself: Jesus as key to creation’s meaning.

- Paul and Rome / “empire” / power
    - Paul announces “good news” (euangelion) that Jesus is
      kyrios (Lord) and even sōtēr (Savior)—terms the Caesars
      liked.
    - Not a simple rival empire; rather, power is inverted:
    - The new power is suffering love (echoing Jesus in Mark 10).
        - Mark 10:42–43: rulers “lord it over” others, but “it is
          not so among you”
        - Mark 10:44–45: “whoever wishes to be first among you
          must be slave of all; for the Son of Man came not to be
          served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom
          for many.”
    - Paul understands his own suffering as participation in that
      cruciform power.

- Why biography (not only letters)?
    - Reading letters without life-context = reading them “in
      a vacuum.”
    - Wright wants readers to “live into” the moments (e.g.,
      Galatians) with narrative pressure and continuity.
    - Biography shows:
    - Continuities of personality and thought,
    - Development, slippage, re-application of scripture,
    - Emotional intensity and darker episodes.

- “What’s new” in Wright’s approach
    - The “quiet decade” after Damascus (unknown ~10 years after
      early flurry: Arabia/Damascus/Jerusalem → sent to Tarsus).
    - Wright infers its weight from later material.
    - Reads Romans 9:1–5 as deeply personal—“my kinfolk” not
      abstract “the Jews”:
        - Possibly family, close relations; grief with
          faces/voices/tears.
    - Spain: Wright becomes more open to Paul reaching Spain:
        - Cites 1 Clement (“limits of east and west”) as likely
          reliable.
    - Paul’s prayer life as central explanatory key:
        - Prayer “bubbles up” in letters; “pray constantly”
          implies his own practice.
    - Paul’s Jesus-and-Spirit-shaped prayer is rooted in Jewish
      tradition; seeing this adds “3D” depth.
    - Justification re-situated:
        - Not abandoned, but located inside the larger portrait
          of Paul’s vocation, worship, and community-making (not
          a detached slogan).

- The striking “dark period” (2 Corinthians 1)
    - Wright highlights Paul’s severe collapse: “so crushed…
      despaired of life itself.”
    - Reads it as something like serious depression, not a minor
      wobble.
    - Not resolved by pious clichés; rather, it forced deeper
      reliance on “the God who raises the dead.”
    - Wright imagines prison as spiritual winter:
        - Like a plant driven to send roots deeper into
          scripture, into Jesus’ death, and into hope.
    - Possible literary fruit: 
        - if prison letters were from Ephesus (Philippians,
          Colossians, Ephesians, Philemon):
    - Their towering passages (Eph 1; Phil 2; Col 1) may be
      poetry before theology:
        - Not “theology then poem,” but darkness giving birth to
          poetic confession which the church later recognizes as
          theology.

- “Religion” as a misleading category
    - “Religion” in Paul’s day ≠ modern “religion.”
    - Paul’s movement is a whole-life community experiment rooted
      in worship that spills into every sphere.
    - Paul insists he remains loyal to the one God of
      Israel—contested then and controversial now.
    - Wright values Jewish dialogue; notes Rabbi Lord Sacks’
      endorsement to avoid caricaturing Judaism.

- Paul’s aim (Wright rejects a common framing)
    - Wright rejects: “Paul told people about Jesus so they go to
      heaven when they die.”
    - That worldview is actually closer to Plutarch/Middle
      Platonism (souls returning to heavenly home) than to Paul.
    - Paul’s hope: renewal of heaven-and-earth and bodily
      resurrection within God’s integrated new creation.

- What Paul actually built: communities
    - Paul founded/maintained communities described as:
    - Worship-based
    - Educational
    - Egalitarian
    - Philanthropic
    - Fictive-kinship (living as family)
    - “Church” language can mislead; these traits define their
      social novelty.
    - Education includes practical literacy:
    - Churches helped people learn to read—especially
      scripture—for maturity.
    - Egalitarian table fellowship (Jew/Gentile; slave/free;
      male/female):
    - Not “better manners,” but sign of powers defeated.
    - Peter’s withdrawal at Antioch = “denying the truth of the
      gospel.”
    - Philanthropy:
    - Caring for needy within the community and beyond; a key
      reason Christianity spread.
    - Public posture:
    - “Rejoice with those who rejoice; weep with those who weep”
      → good neighbors, socially attractive.
    - Wright’s contemporary anecdote (Gateshead): church as “a
      great big second family” = a living sign of Pauline
      ecclesiology.

- Renewed humanity and renewed thinking
    - Goal: renewed humans, renewed minds (Rom 12), renewed in
      knowledge in creator’s image (Col 3).
    - Paul trains not merely “what to think,” but how to think:
    - “Work out your salvation” = apply gospel tools in concrete
      situations, not “lookup-and-put-back.”

- Portrait of Paul’s personality
    - Sheer energy: work, travel, tentmaking; constant
      availability to people.
    - Blunt courage: confronts Peter, magistrates, mobs,
      governors; strategic and fearless.
    - Leadership pattern akin to founders of successful
      organizations: vision, urgency, persuasion, confrontation.
    - Yet vulnerable, open-hearted, emotionally transparent;
      loved deeply by communities.

- Unity and holiness: the hard pastoral task
    - Wright’s maxim: `unity` is easy without `holiness`;
      holiness is easy without unity.
    - Paul’s pastoral genius: hold both together amid pagan
      pressures and internal conscience conflicts (e.g., meat
      offered to idols).

- Q&A highlights
    - Suffering vs triumphalism/“health and wealth”
    - Paul’s joy/life-abundance is real, but often spoken from
      prison or with fresh wounds.
    - New creation has begun; old creation “strikes
      back”—costliness is normal for those on the “leading edge.”
    - Wright notes pastoral resonance: people today recognize
      themselves in Paul’s darkness.
    - Sexual morality as countercultural witness
    - Early Christianity rejected “open season” sexual culture;
      demanded discipline of men as well as women.
    - Attracted many women (protection, dignity, value).
    - Galen: Christians are “mad” for two reasons—bodily
      resurrection and not sleeping around.
    - Underlying logic: goodness of creation and the body; bodies
      are for redemption, not trivial use.
    - Damascus road: not “conversion” from Judaism to
      Christianity
    - Paul experienced a real resurrection appearance; heaven and
      earth overlap in biblical worldview.
    - Wright speculates Paul may have been praying Ezekiel
      1 throne-chariot imagery; sees Jesus on the throne.
    - Paul remains a loyal worshiper of Israel’s God; the shift
      is recognizing God acted in Jesus, and ceasing violent
      Torah-enforcement.
    - Key claim: the Law was good but temporary; fulfilled in
      God’s act in Christ (Gal 3; Rom 8).
    - Justification by “faith/loyalty”
    - “Pistis” can mean faith, `trust`, faithfulness, `loyalty`.
    - “Obedience of faith” echoes imperial language of “obedient
      loyalty”—now owed to Jesus.
    - Wright affirms Reformational “no merit by works” as
      urgently needed in 16th-century debates, but warns against
      making late-medieval questions the controlling frame.
    - For Paul, “works” excluded are often works of the Law
      (boundary markers: circumcision, Sabbath, food laws,
      temple).
    - True faith issues in love; otherwise something is wrong at
      the root.
    - How to live in a hostile culture
    - Philippians 4 as program: disciplined attention to what is
      true/noble/lovely; enjoy goodness wherever found (art,
      beauty, creation).
    - Model what you’ve “seen in me”: embodied apprenticeship,
      not abstract slogans.
    - Paul’s singular strength:
        - Wright ultimately locates it in prayer: deep Jewish
          prayer re-centered on Jesus, driving everything else.
    - Why churches focus more on Paul than Jesus
    - Western distortion: gospel reduced to “leaving earth for
      heaven,” making Jesus’ kingdom teaching seem like snippets.
    - Proper frame: Jesus launched God’s kingdom; Paul implements
      what Jesus achieved.
    - “Thorn in the flesh”
        - Wright: we don’t know; rejects confident speculation
          (temptation, illness, guilt memory, eyesight all
          proposed).
    - How this differs from *Paul and the Faithfulness of God*
        - - Same evidence, different questions: this book aims to
          get “inside Paul’s skin,” tracking development and
          personality.
        - Begins (in the larger book) with Phinehas/zeal/violent
          “righteousness” background to illuminate Paul’s
          pre-Christian zeal (the transcript cuts off mid-point).

- “Reckoned as righteousness” as a controlling Pauline scriptural
  lens
    - Wright suggests Paul likely carried the Genesis/Abraham
      phrase (“reckoned to him as righteousness”) early on as
      a key text.
    - After “the great thing happens” (implied: Paul’s encounter
      with the risen Jesus), Paul rereads Israel’s Scriptures and
      discovers a different mode of “covenantal zeal”—faithful
      covenant membership/identity is rethought around Messiah
      and Spirit, not prior boundary-markers.

- Timeless vs culturally specific: the question needs reframing
    - Wright pushes back on the common split: *some Bible parts
      timeless; others merely cultural*.
    - Instead: all Scripture is culturally conditioned (e.g., NT
      written in first-century Greek; Paul’s categories sit
      within Second Temple Judaism, 4 Ezra, Dead Sea Scrolls,
      etc.).
    - Therefore doctrines like justification are true, but not
      “timeless” in the abstract sense; they mean what they mean
      within their historical world.
    - He warns the search for “timeless truths” can sound overly
      Platonic (truth as disembodied ideas).
    - God’s truth is incarnate truth: meant to become real in
      actual human lives, as it became real in first-century
      lives.

- How Scripture applies: translate the *real issue*, not the
  surface detail
    - Example: 1 Corinthians 8–10 (food offered to idols).
    - Most modern Christians don’t face that exact scenario.
    - Yet the underlying problem—how to live as faithful
      monotheists in a polytheistic/idolatry-saturated culture—is
      pressing today.
    - So we translate principle and pastoral logic into
      contemporary equivalents, rather than dismissing the
      passage as irrelevant.
    - Example: circumcision in Galatians
    - Not typically a live pastoral issue in many congregations.
    - But it could be again in certain contexts (e.g., mixed
      Jewish/Gentile settings), so the text remains potentially
      direct, not merely “ancient.”

- Women’s head coverings (1 Corinthians 11): identity, not
  fashion
    - Wright argues Paul’s aim seems clear: women leading in
      worship should look like women, not like “fake men.”
    - In that society, head covering signaled female identity and
      propriety; modern Western analogies are not
      straightforward.
    - He notes he grew up where women wore hats—not the same
      social function as Paul’s coverings.
    - Contemporary difficulty: Western culture is presently
      confused about male/female meanings and roles, so we should
      be cautious about claiming we “get it right” and Paul
      didn’t.

- Against the claim that Paul is simply sexist
    - Wright’s main counter: read Romans 16 (often neglected in
      Romans teaching that focuses on chs. 1–8).
    - Romans 16 begins with Phoebe, called a deacon at the church
      in Cenchreae.
    - Paul entrusts the letter to Phoebe herself, not to
      a husband.
    - Wright suggests (as plausible custom, though not provable)
      Phoebe may have been expected to read and perhaps explain
      Romans to the Roman house churches.
    - Striking implication: the first “Bible exposition” of
      Romans may have been delivered by a woman.
    - Romans 16 also includes other prominent women leaders,
      including Junia (“an apostle,” as Wright states here).
    - Overall claim: Paul expects and affirms women in
      significant ministry/leadership, even if some passages
      raise hard questions.

- Tension texts: 1 Timothy 2 and translation/setting
    - Wright acknowledges passages about women being subject to
      husbands, and specifically 1 Timothy 2, while noting debate
      about Pauline authorship.
    - He highlights that the key verbs in 1 Timothy 2 are
      notoriously difficult to translate.
    - He mentions looking up the verb (rendered oddly in
      transcript) and finding a wide semantic range; it cannot be
      treated as transparently obvious.
    - “Usurping authority” is mentioned as one hypothesis (not
      asserted as certain).
    - Contextual hypothesis: 1 Timothy addresses Ephesus, where
      the Artemis cult was prominent.
    - Possible scenario: a female-dominated religious culture
      could tempt Christians into an opposite error—assuming
      women must run everything and men must “make room.”
    - Wright stresses: cannot prove it, but it shows the text is
      contextually complex and not reducible to a simplistic,
      universal ban.
    - He observes that some Christians have treated 1 Timothy
      2 as “clear as day” to override 1 Corinthians 11 and Romans
      16; Wright would rather read the harder text in light of
      the clearer, broader Pauline practice.

- Paul’s pastoral concern: holiness and unity held together
    - Wright recalls Paul’s dual concern: holiness and unity—and
      the perennial pastoral challenge is to hold both without
      sacrificing either.

- Modern denominational fragmentation: Paul would be ashamed
    - Asked what Paul would say about today’s fractured churches:
    - Wright: Paul would “hang his head,” urging a return to
      square one.
    - The worst feature is not merely disunity, but our
      complacency about it.
    - He acknowledges real progress (e.g., ecumenical openness
      that was “unthinkable” a century ago).
    - Yet much remains; token ecumenism can become mere
      conscience-soothing.

- Reformation languages and unintended division
    - Wright offers a historical diagnosis:
    - Reformers rightly insisted on worship and Scripture in the
      vernacular.
    - But vernacular worship naturally produced
      national/linguistic churches (German, Dutch, French,
      English).
    - As theological differences emerged, groups labeled others
      “heretics,” sometimes entangled with linguistic/cultural
      divides.
    - He insists theological differences matter—deeply—but
      persistent disunity has spiritual and political
      consequences.

- Cosmic politics: unity as witness against the “powers”
  (Ephesians 3)
    - Wright cites Ephesians 3: through the church, the
      many-colored wisdom of God is made known to principalities
      and powers.
    - Vision: a united, diverse church embodies God’s new
      creation in a way empires cannot.
    - He contrasts this with Caesar’s dream of a unified “family”
      achieved by Roman uniformity and coercion.
    - When the church becomes genuinely united in its glorious
      diversity, Caesar learns God has called time on oppressive
      empire.

- Paul’s Roman citizenship: not a contradiction but part of God’s
  providence
    - Paul’s Roman citizenship:
    - Gives practical leverage (legal protections; appeals;
      mobility).
    - Wright says Paul even relishes it, and that this doesn’t
      negate Paul’s counter-imperial gospel; it shows the
      complexity of Paul’s life and strategy.
    - He connects Roman infrastructure to providence (“when the
      time had fully come…”):
    - roads, postal systems, magistrates—tools Paul can use for
      mission.
    - Strategic partnership: Paul chooses Silas/Sylvanus, also
      a Roman citizen, on the second journey—suggesting
      deliberate planning.

- Philippi and legal “trump card”
    - In Philippi, after being beaten/imprisoned, magistrates
      want them to leave quietly.
    - Paul asserts citizenship: beaten without trial; imprisoned
      without charge—illegal for Roman citizens.
    - He demands (and receives) a public apology, likely because
      officials fear repercussions (Wright references Cicero’s
      *In Verrem* as background to Roman sensitivities).
    - Interesting practical detail: citizens carried a diploma
      (passport-like proof), possibly worn on a string; Wright
      wonders how Paul kept/proved it amid arrest and beating.

- Jerusalem and appeal to Caesar: irony and Isaiah fulfilled
    - In Jerusalem Paul again invokes citizenship to avoid
      illegal torture.
    - His appeal to Caesar is described as bold (“chutzpah”) and
      “heavily ironic.”
    - Wright sees a deeper scriptural frame: Isaiah 52:13–15
      (“kings shall shut their mouths because of him”).
    - Paul, as servant-messenger, expects rulers/nations to be
      shocked by the servant’s gospel—whether they respond with
      faith, rejection, or even execution.

- Overall “striking” through-line
    - Wright presents Paul as:
    - a Scripture-saturated Jew re-reading covenant and
      righteousness in Messiah-light,
    - a pastor balancing holiness with unity,
    - a strategist who uses imperial privileges against imperial
      injustice,
    - and a theologian whose “incarnate truth” demands
      translation into lived faithfulness rather than extraction
      of abstract timeless propositions.
