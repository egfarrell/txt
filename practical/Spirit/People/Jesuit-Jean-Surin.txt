### A 17th century exorcist learns to live again - Ruth Smith - Medium
The healing of a troubled Enneagram Type One
ruthsmith453.medium.com
Ruth Smith

- Father Jean-Joseph Surin was the principal exorcist in the
  famous case of the demon-possessed nuns of Loudun. Weakened by
  his bruising battle with the demons, Surin returned to the
  Jesuit College of the Madeleine at Bordeaux in 1637. There, he
  sank into a pit of depression and physical suffering that
  lasted for almost twenty years. Overwhelmed by the conviction
  that he was himself demon-infested and damned, he lost the
  ability to write, to move without extreme pain and even to
  breathe freely and deeply. His recovery was slow and uneven,
  but marked by important lightbulb moments that still resonate
  in a secular 21st century world.

### Limiting beliefs
    - In some ways, you could say that it was the French Jesuit
      priest’s beliefs that caused his suffering. For a devout
      Catholic of his time, anxiety about the possibility of
      eternal damnation was a given. And that fate was no easy
      one to avoid. Since the Fall of Adam and Eve, human nature
      (and to some extent, everything natural) was felt to be in
      opposition to God. But Surin had an additional
      vulnerability. In the course of ministering to Jeanne des
      Anges, the possessed Mother Superior, the exorcist had
      offered to take demonic entities into himself, not a course
      of action likely to make for inner peace.
    - In addition to a belief in hell and the possibility of
      demon possession, Surin was prone to a particularly
      self-punishing form of perfectionism. It would seem from
      his writings that he would conform to an Enneagram Type One
      personality. For those not familiar with this system, Type
      Ones are personalities with a strong sense of certainty
      about right and wrong, and a deep desire to identify with
      rightness. For Surin, this meant conforming to (his
      conception of) God’s laws. “There is nothing,” he wrote,
      “as important as observing the good order that God had in
      the world” (Into the Dark Night and Back: The Mystical
      Writings of Jean-Joseph Surin, p206. All future quotations
      will be from this text). By his own admission, Surin was
      never one to shirk what he saw as his duty. As a Type One,
      he had to contend with a highly developed superego: the
      voice in the head which holds us to account. One-type
      character traits, like those of all the nine Enneagram
      types, are valuable assets but taken to unhealthy extremes,
      they can become pathological. Surin had always practised
      severe forms of self-denial. Now, truly believing that
      demons had possessed his soul, Surin became convinced that
      he was the object of God’s anger.
    - Enneagram theory suggests that there are nine personality
      types in all. Types Two, Three and Four are ‘heart’ types,
      individuals whose principal orientation to the world is
      through feeling. Types Five, Six and Seven (the ‘head’
      types) favour a mind approach. Eights, Nines and Ones, like
      Surin, are ‘body’ or ‘gut’ types, whose engagement with
      life is predominantly instinctive. It is interesting that
      the priest’s inner struggles expressed themselves in bodily
      limitations.
    - “For, during the entire time when my spirit was as I have
      described it, my body was also tied up and could not move
      hands or feet without extreme pain” (p210).
    - Physical movement was so painful that, for years, Surin
      would experience growing dread every Thursday and Friday,
      in anticipation of the weekly change of shirt that would
      take him sometimes “the entire night of Saturday to Sunday”
      (p225). Anxiety caused a tightness in his chest which
      prevented him from breathing deeply from his diaphragm. And
      it was not only his breathing that was tight and
      constricted. During the years between 1637 and 1655, Surin
      spent almost all his time in the narrow space between his
      bed and the wall, from where he could reach everything he
      needed and ease himself onto his bed at night.
    - The very narrowness of Surin’s focus on his spiritual state
      exacerbated the problem. “I believe,” he wrote later, “that
      the cause of the excessive suffering in my spirit was that
      my attention was totally there, but without diverting me
      elsewhere” (p204). Yet, outside the scope of his tunnel
      vision, there were other areas of Surin’s life, to which he
      had limited conscious access. Separate compartments of
      experience in which he appears to have operated perfectly
      well, even creatively.
    - “There is another wonder: during the entire time of my
      greatest suffering and despair, I composed all the songs
      about divine love that, having all been collected, made an
      entire book, and are giving great consolation to good
      souls” (p205).

### The road back to health
    - Recovery began for Father Surin when a fellow Jesuit,
      Father Bastide, agreed to take charge of him at Saintes,
      and the change afforded him greater rest and freedom. He
      later wrote:
    - “I needed good broth, good treatment and diversions. That
      was indeed the true medicine I needed at the time. True
      spirituality involved gathering the forces of my brain,
      which was overwhelmed; and my soul, being completely
      destroyed, needed to relax, and sleep and divert itself”
      (p201).
    - Enneagram theory suggests that Type Ones can benefit from
      opening out into the natural territory of Type Seven, one
      of the ‘head’ triad. Sevens are characteristically
      fun-loving, with a strong appetite for pleasure, a love of
      freedom and spontaneity and a tendency to flit from one
      thing to another. For Surin, as for many people caught up
      in the tight vice of anxiety, diversion — filling the mind
      with thoughts of other things — would have been an
      important tool. Bastide also seems to have employed good
      therapeutic skills. Most of Surin’s fellow priests in the
      College at Bordeaux had taken every opportunity to confront
      what they saw as his fantasies, believing that his
      inability to move was purely psycho-somatic. Bastide
      accepted the exorcist’s own narrative of his malady, that
      it was a spiritual one caused by the demons which had taken
      up residence in him. At the same time, Bastide repeatedly
      said that he thought the illness would pass and Surin would
      again enjoy peace. In other words he gave hope.
    - Surin’s suffering, however, did not let go of him quickly
      or easily. Healing took a long time and progress was
      interspersed with times of cloud. During one very dark
      time, he resolved on suicide and was only prevented by
      being unable to lift his leg high enough to jump from the
      window onto the paved courtyard below (p212). Relief from
      Surin’s mental straitjacket and his physical constriction
      came gradually, as he began to open up to wider vistas. He
      likened his recovery to someone who has been in a dark and
      frightful chamber, having a window opened. From there the
      prisoner can see, if not yet venture into, “a lovely
      landscape” (p211).
    - The power of compassion
    - Perhaps the most significant key to recovery came on 12th
      October 1655, when he was back in the college at Bordeaux.
      The priest who acted as Surin’s confessor came into his
      bedchamber to hear his confession. At this point, Surin was
      still convinced that he was the object of God’s eternal
      wrath, yet he felt a great desire to serve God. In
      a tortuous act of twisted logic, he had come to believe
      that his own urge to do good was contrary to God’s order.
      After all, God’s order decreed that men destined for hell
      should be capable only of wrongdoing. When he confessed his
      ‘sin’ of wanting to serve God, the confessor was
      understandably nonplussed. His reply to Surin can be seen
      as a much needed gift from the domain of heart, the third
      centre of human functioning according to the Enneagram, and
      the natural territory of Types Two, Three and Four. The
      confessor told Surin that he felt “great compassion” for
      him.
    - “I am not,” he said, “a man of revelation [the mind], nor
      one who trusts his instincts very much [the gut].
      Nonetheless, I must tell you that I have often had an
      impression that does not come from my imagination, nor from
      my own senses, which is: before you die, Our Lord will do
      you the grace to see that you are mistaken, and that you
      will finally do as other men do; and I hope you will die in
      peace” (p207).
    - Perhaps because of the compassion behind it, this response
      had a life-changing effect on Surin. Alone in his
      bedchamber after he had confessed, he was able to hear God
      speak words of “tenderness and love,” not in his mind where
      the persecuting thoughts originated, but in his heart.
      A channel of loving communication between the priest and
      his God had been re-opened (p207–8).

### Surrender
    - On the Friday after Pentecost in 1656, Surin was in his
      room, feeling content and at peace, when the old intrusive
      thought came once again forcefully into his mind: “But yet,
      you are damned” (p218). He felt himself about to sink back
      into despair, when instead it came to him to submit
      completely to what had occurred. To Surin this was
      surrendering to the divine will. Throwing himself on the
      bed face down, he was engulfed by a wave of peace. Rather
      than giving credence to the hateful thought and fighting
      it, he accepted the fact of its existence and looked beyond
      it to a greater reality, that of the grace of God. He later
      wrote that “despair never again was able to dominate in my
      interior” (p218).

### Looking outward
    - The ability to walk freely did not fully return until 1660,
      when Surin was staying with a neighbour in the country. The
      release came when, one day, he struggled to the garden door
      with his friend. For the first time in many years, he felt
      able to make the effort to negotiate steps — the five or
      six steps down to the threshold of the door. The way he
      described the key moment is interesting.
    - ‘With my heart quite joyful, I began to see how very
      beautiful the door was, and to pay a bit more attention to
      the objects that were there’ (p223).
    - What came first — the joyful heart or the greater attention
      to objects outside of himself — is impossible to know.
      Perhaps they fed into each other. But it was after he began
      to really notice and appreciate the objects around him that
      he found himself able to walk down the steps to the door.
      That was enough for one day but the following day, he
      ventured into the garden itself and on the third day, he
      went all the way to the well and into the woods. “After
      that,” he writes, “I always walked” (p224). As Huxley
      points out in The Devils of Loudun, healing came as Surin
      looked beyond the doctrine of fallen nature and allowed
      himself to see beauty in the natural world (p304–5).

- Working with the Enneagram as a tool for psycho-spiritual
  growth entails two apparently contradictory movements. In
  recognizing that I conform to a particular personality type,
  I learn to accept ‘what is.’ As a ‘head’ type (I identify as
  a Type Five) of course I also have access to emotions and to
  gut instinct, but my default position will be to come at life
  primarily through thinking about it. There are strengths about
  my personality style which I enjoy but there are also
  weaknesses and limitations. At the same time, I am also
  something deeper than my personality, something essentially
  unlimited. From this deeper place I can begin to access the
  ‘foreign’ territory of other personality types. Like Father
  Surin, I can move out of constriction into a broader, freer
  place.
