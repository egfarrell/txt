
- Bornkamm was quite explicit about *how little* can be said
  — but also about what still remains solid once the tradition is
  critically sifted. What follows is a fair summary of the
  “critically minimal historical picture” of Jesus that Günther
  Bornkamm thought could be responsibly recovered.

### 1. Jesus was a Galilean preacher of the kingdom of God

At minimum, Bornkamm held that Jesus:

* appeared publicly in Galilee
* proclaimed the nearness of the kingdom of God
* did so in oral, striking, non-systematic forms (sayings, parables, aphorisms)

What matters historically is *not* a detailed apocalyptic schema, but that:

* Jesus announced God’s decisive action as imminent and demanding response now
* the proclamation was inseparable from Jesus himself as its bearer

Bornkamm thought this basic preaching activity is multiply attested and historically unavoidable.


### 2. Jesus spoke and acted with an extraordinary, unmediated authority

This is perhaps the most central historical result for Bornkamm.

Across diverse traditions we find Jesus:

* speaking without appeal to prior authorities
* pronouncing forgiveness
* redefining obedience to Torah
* making radical demands on individuals

Bornkamm argued that even if individual sayings are shaped by the church, the *pattern* is historically secure:

* Jesus behaves as one who stands directly in God’s place

This is not yet later Christology, but it is enough to explain:

* fascination
* offense
* escalation of conflict


### 3. Jesus’ activity provoked both devotion and opposition

Historically recoverable is not just what Jesus said, but the effects he had.

Bornkamm emphasized that:

* people left homes and livelihoods to follow him
* others accused him of law-breaking, deception, or blasphemy

This polarization cannot be explained unless:

* Jesus made claims that disrupted normal religious boundaries
* his authority was experienced as real and threatening

The reactions themselves are part of the historical data.


### 4. Jesus was remembered as a healer and exorcist

Bornkamm did *not* attempt to verify miracles in a modern sense.

But he argued that it is historically secure that:

* Jesus was widely known as a worker of mighty deeds
* healing and exorcism belonged to his public role

Reason:

* these traditions are early, widespread, and embarrassing for later rationalizing theology
* even hostile sources presuppose them (attributing them to demonic power)

Thus, whatever one thinks “really happened,” Jesus was experienced as a bearer of divine power.


### 5. Jesus stood in tension with established religious norms

Bornkamm was careful here, but insisted that Jesus:

* relativized ritual purity
* associated freely with outsiders
* placed obedience to God’s present claim above legal casuistry

This does *not* mean Jesus rejected Judaism.
It *does* mean his practice created real friction within it.

The tradition did not invent this tension; it struggled to explain it.


### 6. Jesus went to Jerusalem and was executed by Roman authority

For Bornkamm, this is the hardest historical core.

Minimal facts:

* Jesus’ mission led him to Jerusalem
* he was executed by crucifixion
* Rome carried out the sentence

Bornkamm stressed:

* crucifixion presupposes a perceived political or public danger
* Jesus’ activity could not be reduced to harmless moral teaching

The death of Jesus demands explanation — and that explanation lies in his public authority and disruptive presence.


### 7. Jesus did not found a church or formulate doctrine

Equally important are the *negatives*.

Bornkamm argued we cannot securely claim that Jesus:

* intended a new religious institution
* taught a worked-out Christology
* anticipated the church’s later theological formulations

Yet:

* his impact was so strong that a movement *had* to form after his death
* Easter faith arose as response, not invention


### Condensed summary (Bornkamm’s minimal Jesus)

Historically recoverable is a figure who:

* proclaimed God’s imminent reign
* confronted people with an immediate decision
* acted with an authority experienced as divine
* disrupted established religious expectations
* inspired devotion, provoked conflict
* and was executed as a public threat

Bornkamm’s key claim is that this much is enough:

* enough to explain the rise of Easter faith
* enough to ground theology historically
* enough to show continuity without collapsing history into dogma
