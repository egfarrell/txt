### Florence Nightingale at Home
    - In 1835, William Nightingale wrote to his daughter setting
      out a strict regime for keeping fit:
    - "Exercise for 10 minutes every day before breakfast. Before
      you dress do the exercise of the arms 20 times.
    - In the course of the day 20 minutes exercise must be done
      and if not well done 10 minutes more.
    - Run down to the gate before breakfast by the road … Every
      day you must be an hour out of doors before dinner unless
      you have permission to do otherwise."

### Florence Nightingale (Mark Bostridge)
    - In Teresa of Avila, Florence discovered a reformer of
      passionate temperament who suffered from frail health for
      much of her life and struggled against a strong desire to
      be admired by others (!)
    - The books loaned by Clare included... Spiritual
      Conferences, an extraordinary compendium of mystical
      theology and hagiography across nineteen centuries by
      Frederick William Faber, a founder of the London Oratory
      (who insisted on addressing the Virigin Mary as 'Mamma')
      ...
    - But it was towards those mystics who had worked actively in
      the world that Florence found herself drawn by instinct and
      experience, and for whom she expressed the greatest
      admiration.
    - It should come as no surprise that she retained a deep
      suspicion of mysticism as it was commonly understood:
        - a withdrawal from the outside world into a life of
          prayer and contemplation, punctuated by moments of
          ecstasy and self-mortification. ...
    - Florence's 'true doctrine of mystics', by contrast, would
      place the accent not on devotion, but on 'work and
      suffering for the love of G-d'.
    - We are called upon not merely to think about ideals, 'but
      to do and suffer for them'. A mystical state, so defined,
      is a permanent condition, and not a sudden 'call' or spark
      of insight.
    - Florence commented that she would have been much happier
      personally ... if she had spent her life '_thinking_ only
      of the Ideal ... instead of struggling daily, hourly, with
      the selfishness, indifference, wilful resistance, which are
      all that surround me now ...'
    - It was the difference, she thought, between swimming
      against a strong current, with the waves closing over your
      head, and standing on the bank looking at the blue sky.
        - (I think the World still needs true Carmelites and
          Carthusians too!)
    - Evelyn Underhill, in 'Practical Mysticism', saw Florence as
      'one of the greatest and most balanced contemplatives of
      the 19th century' and also as a figure acting 'under mystic
      compulsion' like Joan of Arc.
    - Florence attemps to resolve the central problem that was
      pertinent to her own life; how to find the proper
      relationship of action and union with G-d.
    - A spiritual ideal must be embodied in everyday life. 'If we
      keep the Spiritual Ideal for Sundays or prayers, it is like
      people who go to hear Bach's music at Westminster and think
      their enjoyment devotional feeling'.
    - Florence insists there will be no heaven, unless we make it
      for ourselves.
    - Like Teresa of Avila and John of the Cross, we must ask G-d
      to give His will to us. Prayer is not for asking G-d what
      we want - because since G-d is immutable, we are unable to
      change His mind (? not according to Process Theology), but
      for asking Him what He wants us to do. We must surrender
      our will completely to G-d's:
        > 'Uniting oneself with His work by observing what His
        > _universal laws_ are, as the only means of carrying out
        > His work'.
    - We shouldn't waste time wondering whether our intentions
      are pure, and in accordance with G-d's will, but in each
      action 'stake ones existence in carrying out that action'.
      'There will be challenges and obstacles, but these are only
      to be expected, and we ought to be ready:
        > to go down "into hell" for G-d's service
    - Christ on the cross is the highest expression of G-d, not
      'in the vulgar meaning' of atonement, but in the way that
      G-d, as fellow-sufferer, hangs on the cross every day 'in
      every one of us'.
    - Florence believed that the passive and the active life
      could be united, though she admitted such a union had never
      been achieved, party because the active life was so much
      a product of modern science.
    - Florence asks: should we look upon the Bengal sanitary
      commissioners as G-d's true missionaries in India??
        - (truer than St. Francis Xavier, a Jesuit missionary,
          who had preached and converted in India in the 16th
          century)
    - It might seem a shocking idea, and one that would
      undoubtedly repel some religious people, as well as
      providing Lytton Strachey with the basis for his snide
      remark that Florence Nightingale thought that G-d was
      a sanitary engineer.
    - But in cleaning out a foul drain are they not carrying out
      G-d's work; and are we not as bound by G-d's laws to clean
      a drain, and save the population from typhus, as we are by
      the commandment 'not to steal'?
    - According to Florence, religion should infuse the
      intellectual work of the day, even its _scientific
      discoveries_, and 'the practical intellectual work of all
      of us'.
    - In advancing these ideas, she was simply advocating to the
      outside world what had long been manifestly true for
      herself: that religious faith should exist, not on the
      periphery of an individual's existence, as an excuse for
      shirking responsibilities, but at its very centre, guiding
      and informing our actions in daily life.
    - On six occasions '_The Voice_' is recorded as speaking to
      her and at various other points in the diary the reader
      suddenly becomes aware that a dialogue with G-d is taking
      place.
        - Much of this dialogue is concerned with Florence's work
          for India, then occupying a large amount of her time.
    - Other parts of it are filled with a surprising degree of
      self-reproach and self-doubt ('O G-d in Thee have I never
      trusted, therefore I am rightly confounded').
    - Though on one occasion, she turns to questioning G-d
      Himself ('O G-d, are you sure you are doing all you can for
      the Bosnians?')
    - However, the predominant note in Florence's relationship
      with G-d is the same here as it had been thirty years
      earlier. Is she willing to give up her own name and
      reputation for G-d? She begs Him to
        > 'Take O take from me ever the wish to impress, cause of
        > my unrest'
    - When she receives bad news about the provision of nurses at
      the Royal Herbert Hospital, she realises that she felt
      disappointment in her own name, and not in G-d's.
    - At the age of 57, she still feels divided between a love of
      self and a love of G-d.
    - Even her work can become 'an idol, a Moloch', preventing
      her from confronting her pride.
        - 'Moloch': Canaanite god of sacrafice, mentioned in
          Leviticus
        - 'Moloch' has been figuratively used in reference to
          a person or a thing which demands or requires a very
          costly sacrifice.
