### Socrates’s daimonion
- Socrates’s daimonion (literally “little divine thing” or “inner
  spirit”) was his name for a mysterious inner voice or sign that
  would sometimes intervene in his life. He described it in
  Plato’s dialogues—especially the Apology—as something that
  never told him what to do, but rather warned him when he was
  about to take a wrong step. It was, in his words, “a voice that
  comes to me and always forbids me to do what I am about to do,
  but never commands me to act.”
- It wasn’t a “demon” in the later Christian sense of an evil
  spirit; the Greek daimon was morally neutral—more like
  a personal spiritual guide or intermediary between gods and
  humans. Socrates claimed that this voice had accompanied him
  from childhood, sometimes halting him mid-sentence or
  persuading him not to take certain political or personal
  actions. It was never hysterical or emotional—more like a quiet
  check on his reason.
- Philosophically, the daimonion is fascinating because it shows
  how Socrates experienced conscience and intuition as an
  externalized presence. He trusted his rational argumentation in
  debate, but he also trusted this subtle moral signal that
  seemed to operate outside his conscious logic. It’s as though
  his intellect and his inner “signal detector” worked together:
  reason showed him what was plausible; the daimonion told him
  what was right.
- Later thinkers have interpreted it in various ways:
    - Psychologists call it a poetic description of conscience or
      subconscious intuition.
    - Religious interpreters see it as divine guidance.
    - Existentialists sometimes read it as an expression of
      radical self-integrity—a refusal to betray one’s inner
      compass.
- Socrates’s daimonion and Ignatius’s “discernment of spirits”
  actually belong to the same family of human experience: both
  are attempts to describe that subtle, invisible dialogue
  between the mind’s logic and its mysterious inner promptings.
- Each man, in his own idiom, was studying the same question: how
  do you tell whether the voice inside you is wisdom—or vanity in
  disguise?
