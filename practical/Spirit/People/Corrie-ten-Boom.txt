### Corrie Ten Boom
    - Corrie ten Boom was a Dutch Christian watchmaker and writer
      who, along with her family, helped hide Jews from the Nazis
      during World War II, saving many lives.
    - She and her family were eventually caught and sent to
      concentration camps, where her father and sister died, but
      Corrie survived. After the war, she wrote about her
      experiences in her book, *The Hiding Place*, and became
      a speaker on forgiveness and reconciliation.

2. THEOLOGICAL REACTIONS TO HER “TRAMP FOR THE LORD” LIFE

Your focus on Tramp for the Lord is perceptive, because it shows a different Corrie than The Hiding Place, and theologians actually read that later wandering phase quite differently.

I’ll break this into how scholars/pastors tend to interpret it, and then what makes it theologically unusual.

A. How theologians tend to read her postwar wandering

Most theologians don’t analyze Tramp for the Lord as literature so much as vocation theology in narrative form. A few recurring interpretations:

    Radical obedience over institutional vocation
    Commentators often frame her postwar life as a kind of lay, charismatic calling that sits outside normal ecclesial structures. She had no parish, no academic post, no denominational office — just invitations, trains, and trust.

This makes her attractive to evangelical theologians who emphasize “calling” as obedience to God’s leading rather than office-holding. Finn and Nichols both implicitly treat her wandering as an extension of wartime faithfulness: same obedience, different battlefield.

    A lived eschatology
    Several evangelical interpreters see her itinerancy as driven by a strong sense that the world stood on the edge of judgment, persecution, or renewal. She believed Christians needed to be spiritually prepared for suffering, not comfortable.

Her warnings against complacency, and her insistence that Western Christians were unprepared for persecution, give her life a distinctly eschatological urgency — not speculative, but practical: travel now, speak now, forgive now.

    “Kenotic” witness rather than strategy
    Pastoral theologians sometimes describe her life as kenotic (self-emptying) rather than strategic. She didn’t build institutions, endow seminaries, or found movements. She spent money as fast as it came, trusted provision, and moved on.

This is often contrasted with more managerial postwar evangelicalism (Billy Graham–style organization). Corrie looks almost anachronistic by comparison.

B. Why her tramp-like life feels theologically strange

You’re right to sense something unusual here. Several reasons theologians find her postwar life hard to categorize:

    It resembles pre-modern Christian itinerancy
    Her lifestyle looks less like modern Protestant ministry and more like medieval mendicant wandering — except without vows, orders, or rule. That’s awkward for Protestant theology, which doesn’t quite know where to put that kind of sanctity.

Some scholars quietly note that her life resembles saints’ vitae more than modern autobiography.

    Her faith resists psychologization
    After WWII, many theologians emphasize trauma, processing, memory, and healing. Corrie almost never does this. She moves, speaks, forgives, and goes on.

This has led to both admiration and discomfort. Critics worry about repression; admirers see an almost frightening clarity of will shaped by obedience rather than introspection.

    She treats dependence as a theological virtue
    In Tramp for the Lord, dependence is not a problem to solve but a mode of faith. Missed trains, lost money, exhaustion — all are narrated as occasions for trust, not as disruptions.

Theologically, this aligns her with a very “bare” doctrine of providence: God governs not just outcomes but logistics. That’s a strong claim, and many modern theologians would soften it.

C. How Tramp for the Lord reframes The Hiding Place

Readers who only know The Hiding Place often expect Tramp for the Lord to be “what happened after healing.”

Instead, theologians often note that it presents no resolution, only continuation. The same faith that hid Jews now hops trains and crosses borders. There is no narrative arc toward stability.

That’s why many see her wandering not as trauma aftermath, but as vocation fully embraced — almost an enacted parable: a Christian life without securities, justified only by obedience.

D. A quiet critique that appears in scholarship

Some academic theologians (especially those concerned with Holocaust memory) worry that her itinerant preaching universalized suffering too quickly — turning concrete Jewish suffering into general Christian exhortation.

This critique isn’t about her sincerity, but about the theological risks of a roaming witness whose message simplifies in order to travel.

Still, even critics usually concede that her life embodied exactly what she preached — which makes her hard to dismiss.

    What “hagiography” means in this context

Classical hagiography (vitae of saints) is not mainly about psychological depth or historical completeness. Its purpose is theological formation.

Typical features:
– the saint’s life is ordered around obedience, not development
– interior struggle is minimized or stylized
– providence is concrete and frequent
– travel and instability signify mission, not homelessness
– miracles are ordinary disruptions, not spectacles
– the saint becomes a living argument for a theological claim

When you read Tramp for the Lord through this lens, it fits remarkably well.

In Tramp for the Lord, Corrie ten Boom almost disappears as a modern psychological subject.

There is:
– very little introspection
– almost no trauma narration
– no sustained self-analysis
– no attempt to justify or explain her calling

she goes, she speaks, she forgives, she trusts, she leaves.

This is exactly how saints function in medieval vitae: the point is not who they “are inside,” but what obedience looks like when fully enacted.

Her interior life is legible only through action.

In hagiography, this is the miracle economy:
not suspension of natural law, but God’s visible involvement in logistics.

Modern theology tends to resist this clarity.

Ambiguity belongs to the reader, not the exemplar.

Discomfort:

These are exactly the tensions scholars raise when saints’ lives are read as history rather than theological narrative.

Those absences are what allow the saintly function to emerge.

The book forms readers by example, not argument.
That is hagiography’s core task.
