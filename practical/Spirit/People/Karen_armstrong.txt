Karen Armstrong Interview

IV
    Today I talk about belief with someone who knows more
    about it than most of us and for several reasons. At the
    age of 17 while she was still at school, Karen Armstrong
    decided she wanted to become a nun.  In 1962 she entered
    the Society of the Holy Child  Jeshua. Seven years later
    she left the order with much sadness and thirteen years
    after that, wrote about the entire experience in
    a memoir called, Through the Narrow Gate.  That isn’t
    her only insight into religious belief; since then she’s
    become an academic researching and writing about the
    world’s religions - she’s written and published some 14
    books including, A History of God, Islam – A Brief
    History and the Battle for God, fundamentalism in
    Judaism and Christianity and Islam.  Not surprisingly,
    she’s in considerable demand as a lecturer, especially
    in America, to explain the rise of militant
    fundamentalism and perhaps help them answer the question
    asked so painfully after September 11th.  Why is America
    so hated?  She also teaches Christianity at London’s Leo
    Baeck College.  Karen Armstrong - ex nun I suppose is
    a tag that will always live with you.  Do you mind that?

KA
    I get a bit weary of it sometimes but then in a sense
    I would say it’s the ex that I object to, rather than
    the nun part of it, because in some strange turn-around,
    I’ve remained quite nun-like in many ways. I’ve never
    married, I live alone and I spend my days writing,
    thinking, talking about God and spirituality, so
    I sometimes think that when I decided to enter a convent
    all those years ago as a young and very ignorant
    teenager, I was having a stab perhaps, at the kind of
    life I’ve got now but which wasn’t available to me at
    that time.

IV
    Let’s examine how you got there, then, because, 17 is
    a very early age to make decisions.  Your father was
    a converted Roman Catholic….

KA
    Yes but, my parents weren’t religious people,
    particularly. We went along to mass on Sunday mornings
    - often went as early as possible to get it all over and
      out of the way  - and then rushed home and enjoyed the
      rest of the day. We weren’t a devout family and my
      parents, poor things -  I’m very sorry about this now
      - were horrified when I decided that I wanted to be
      a nun.

IV
    Was it an act of rebellion?

KA
    A bit, in a way.  I think  these things are very, very
    complex, but there was part of me that felt ill at ease
    in the kind of late fifties, very early sixties society,
    that was very family based and I lived in Birmingham,
    which was a very materialistic part of the country,
    where money was king and I, in my idealistic teenage
    way, I didn’t like that.  I wanted to find God, I had
    very vague ideas what this meant but I thought I would
    immediately become wise, serene, Buddha-like, and
    perhaps, lose all this adolescent confusion that so
    plagues a 17 year old. It didn’t happen.

IV
    When you wrote your book you said something rather
    remarkable, you had strong religious beliefs as a child
    but little faith in God.  Can you enlarge on the
    distinction?

KA
    Well, yes, in the Western world since the 18th century,
    we’ve tended to equate faith with believing things, with
    accepting certain ideas, doctrines or propositions about
    God, heaven, the afterlife and the rest of it.  But
    that’s a very peculiar development in the history of
    religion, other religious traditions - I’m thinking of
    Judaism, Islam, Buddhism -  don’t place any much
    credence on belief. Basically you can believe what you
    want, it’s not important, what is important is doing
    things, living in a certain way - uniformity of practice
    very often, rather than uniformity of belief. But we in
    the West have gone on for believing things and when
    I was in my convent, I had strong beliefs about the
    existence of God, the nature of Jeshua Christ and
    redemption but I cannot say that that gave me any great
    faith that life had ultimate meaning and value. The word
    faith is a sense that despite all the depressing
    evidence to the contrary, life does have some ultimate
    meaning.

IV
    And you never found that, even in the convent?

KA
    I found that the beliefs that I had, were actually
    militating against this faith, because the God I thought
    I believed in, was a very harsh task master, a cosmic
    big brother, endlessly looking down, marking up my every
    fault, never satisfied with what I did. I was never able
    to please him and indeed, never able to get in contact
    with him. I expected to have all kinds of wonderful
    spiritual experiences but the heavens remained
    obstinately closed  for all kinds of reasons that I can
    understand now but couldn’t at the time and frankly,
    when I eventually lost my belief in this God, life
    became a whole lot better.

IV
    Let’s talk about your own spiritual life then, your own
    spirituality, because in your late teens you’re going to
    be very susceptible and highly charged with
    sensitivities about religion.  So, what did your inner
    life consist of, was prayer very meaningful, did you
    have visions?.

KA
    I was absolutely hopeless at prayer, I could not
    meditate at all. Every morning we’d go into the chapel
    at six o’ clock and meditate until seven and I could not
    keep my mind on this for more than two minutes at
    a time.  Now I have no problem with concentration
    normally. I can sit over my books at my desk for hours
    and not notice the time going by but it was the wrong
    kind of prayer for me. Plus it was a bad time to become
    a nun - it was just before the changes wrought by the
    second Vatican Council and I was one of the last people
    to go through a system that was very flawed,  that was
    endlessly driving into us a sense of our faults and
    failings and I think, we were so inured and embedded in
    egotistic anxiety about our own performance, we couldn’t
    possibly have had a religious experience.

IV
    Did you find love there?

KA
    No, this was one of the reasons why I came out. Because
    I entered at this very difficult time for the religious
    orders, it was a very cold environment, we weren’t
    allowed for example, to have friends. We were supposed,
    in those days,  to give all our love to God, so
    particular friendship, in most religious communities was
    frowned upon. We were never allowed to speak in two, for
    example, always had to wait ‘til a third person came
    along and indeed, most of our days were passed in
    silence. So it combined religious devotion with the
    English stiff upper lip and reserve and so it was
    a fairly lethal mixture and it was this cold-heartedness
    that eventually I rebelled against and I said that this
    had nothing whatever to do with the gospels, where you
    have a very hands-on, engaged Jeshua going out, loving
    people and very much involved with them, and where love
    and concern for the neighbour and affection for the
    neighbour is at the heart of piety, and I’ve come to
    believe that compassion, love, feeling with people, is
    at the heart of the religious life.

IV
    The Mother Superior said to you at one point, that brain
    work distracted the mind, so that even thinking was
    considered as getting in the way of God.

KA
    Yes and this I think was a hangover from the Victorian
    period, where women were not supposed to be great
    brains, in fact, women weren’t supposed to have brains
    much at all. It was thought until relatively recently
    that our brains were so fragile that they would crumble
    under the impact of real strenuous thinking, so most of
    the time I spent sewing, scrubbing floors and even
    a disastrous foray into cooking, which was a great
    penance for the whole community, because I’m not a cook,
    and the idea was that it would free you from distracting
    circumstances and so that your mind would be free to
    soar to God but my mind was just, sort of completely
    bound up with my bad needlework, which I couldn’t do,
    couldn’t master.

IV
    Did it make you feel guilty?

KA
    Just despairing and hopeless, and I think what I felt
    most guilty about was that I was continually in tears
    and I’m not a particularly weepy person,  but I wept
    throughout this seven years like a broken water spout.
    We were either weeping or in paroxysms of giggles,
    I think were obviously stretched, absolutely taut.

IV
    When you came out, you made this bold decision and you
    left.  Do you think you were damaged?

KA
    Yes, I do think I was damaged, it took me about six
    years to recover from this, and I’m still not sure that
    I’ve altogether outgrown some of those things today,
    though I’ve learnt to compensate for them and use them
    in a different way but, for six years I existed, I think
    in a state of grief. I was suicidal, I was anorexic, and
    just utterly, utterly depressed.  I had no idea how to
    relate to people and it wasn’t that I wanted to die so
    much as that I simply did not know how to live.  What
    had happened, was a kind training we had had in those
    day, -  it was a form of conditioning, mild form of
    brainwashing, we’d be told to do something one day and
    the next we would be absolutely excoriated for doing it.

IV
    Was this to keep you obedient?

KA
    Obedient and not reliant on yourself, so that you would
    submit your will and judgement wholly to the will and
    judgement of your superior.  Now it was very hard for me
    after I left the religious to think at all. I mean, even
    when I was a student at Oxford, I found it almost
    impossible to relate directly and spontaneously to
    a poem I was reading in English literature . Unless
    I had found some critic, it didn’t matter how miserable
    the critic or writer this might have been, who said what
    I was struggling to articulate, I didn’t feel I could
    have the idea.

IV
    What about your family? Your family had regretted your
    becoming a nun; were they a source of comfort to you
    once you came out?

KA
    Well I found it very difficult to relate to my family,
    because we’d hardly seen each other for all those seven
    years. They’d been allowed to visit me, say, once every
    few months, for a starchy session in the convent
    parlour;   was never allowed to eat with them, I had to
    go off to the refectory, were never allowed to talk
    about what happens inside the enclosure.

IV
    Could you hug them?

KA
    Oh yes but with restraint in that nun-like way that we,
    kiss with a sort of banging of cheeks on either side and
    it was very chilly and they could see, I think, that
    I was terribly damaged, that I was not the sort of,
    bright confident little girl that had gone into the
    convent, I was this, thin, scraggy, haunted looking
    woman and I think they thought I’d  been lost forever.

IV
    You came out and you missed the sixties the 1960s, it
    wasn’t just the rock ‘n roll but a great many things of
    significance were happening in the sixties which were
    changing the social conscience and the way of life in
    our community.  Did you find that very disorienting?

KA
    Yes, it was like having been Rip van Winkel, or someone
    who’d gone to sleep for a hundred year and come out and
    found an entirely changed society, because we’d not been
    kept abreast of any of these developments. I’d never
    heard of the Beatles, for example, I’d never heard of
    Vietnam - and I left in 1969. So coming out and seeing
    youth protesting, demonstrating, when we’d not been able
    to voice a single objection in the convent. And the
    sexual revolution was underway - instead of the tight,
    polite little clothes we used to wear in the late 1950s,
    you know, people had long scraggy hair and skirts right
    up to their thighs, this was just bewildering for me.

IV
    You were only in your mid twenties at that time, did you
    kick over the traces and try and catch up?

KA
    Not immediately. It was impossible for me as I said to
    make any friends, to have any contact with people, just
    even to get through the day was difficult.  Eventually,
    about six or seven years afterwards, I had a delayed
    adolescence,  and I had a string of what we called
    boyfriends in those days and was very feminist and
    rebellious and I think, I needed to do that to get it
    all out of my system, went to lots of parties. That
    didn’t work very well either but it was important,
    I think to do it.

IV
    But something kept you focused.  Did you declare
    yourself to be an atheist at this time?

KA
    I never went that far, I just, because that, to call
    yourself an atheist demands a degree of conviction and
    defiance and passion, I just fell away from religion, in
    an exhausted and wearied way.  I felt sickened by it
    all, I tried to open myself to God and God had, wanted
    nothing to do with it. I realised I’d never believed
    much in this cosmic deity anyway and the whole idea of
    religion for about thirteen years was utterly abhorrent.

IV
    And what was your moral principle then on what, what
    guided your behaviour, grounded you?

KA
    I think that a struggle, because most of these years
    were a struggle, a struggle to recover from the convent,
    also a struggle with undiagnosed epilepsy, which was
    distorting my perceptions, because I have a form of
    epilepsy that affected moods very badly and until that
    was diagnosed, I thought I was going off my head, so
    life was such a struggle, I was really clinging at
    straws and working towards health. I think kindness was
    one of the things that I began to value very, very much,
    -that the importance of treating other people kindly and
    with respect, which is not a great religious virtue I’m
    sorry to say but also not necessarily a virtue in our
    society, where we tend to be rather dismissive - I began
    to feel that kindness was of crucial importance and in
    a sense it was that that eventually, by  a long and
    circuitous route brought me back to religion.

IV
    And then you wrote the book, Through a Narrow Gate,
    which was the account, a very touching and heartbreaking
    account of your experiences as a nun.  Did that, as it
    were, complete the story, was that closure?

KA
    Yes, it was very, it was redemptive I think, because the
    first draft of that book was extremely angry and it was
    pointed out to me, that if it had been that bad in there
    I wouldn’t have stayed as long as I did. So I had to dig
    down and remember things that had got buried over the
    years under the anger and resentment and distress and
    I began to realise how much of it that I had valued,
    that there had been one or two nuns in there, who’d been
    quite wonderful and who have remained luminaries for me,
    and so that it redeemed that time and laid it to rest,
    it was a closure.

IV
    And there was something of a turning point then, because
    having written that book, you went to Jerusalem to make
    a television programme, and you began to see religion in
    a completely different perspective, in a perspective
    that has been the grounding under which you’ve worked
    ever since.

KA
    Yes,  I was invited to make a documentary series on St.
    Paul and I was working with an Israeli company in
    Jerusalem,  and when you’re living in Jerusalem, you
    cannot escape from other faiths,  and I had realised how
    parochial and limited my religious upbringing had been.
    I’d never thought as Judaism as anything but a kind of
    prelude to Christianity and I knew nothing about Islam
    at all, or Greek Orthodoxy. But surrounded by these
    different traditions, all jostling together in this
    small town, [, you become more aware of the hostility
    but also, the very real links that bind them together
    and so, in my subsequent work I have tried to develop
    what I once called, triple vision, so that you see the
    three families as one tradition that went in three
    different ways but, none of them has the monopoly of
    truth in my view.

IV
    Would you say that you discovered your true vocation
    now, having, the nun having failed and that was to be an
    academic devoted to scholarship and to write what you
    discovered?

KA
    Yes, I think and my scholarship is I think a form of
    prayer.  While I’m studying and immersed in my books,
    I get moments of transcendence, or awe, or delight, just
    mini seconds.  Now my colleague, Lionel Blue tells me
    that this is a very Jewish way of praying, that this is
    what Jews do when they study Torah, or Talmud. They
    don’t speak to God in the way that Christians often do
    but in the process of studying they will get glimmers of
    the divine, St Benedict had much the same idea, he used
    to make his monks study in the course of which he said,
    they would get mini seconds of  prayer, so that
    I stumbled on this form of spirituality. But I would
    also say that what I’m pleading for is understanding.
    We’re living in an age of huge religious violence and
    intolerance and bad religion. Now the challenge of the
    twenty first century is that all the religions draw on
    the compassion that is at their root and learn that we
    are all members of the same family.

IV
    Let’s stay with you and your own life Karen, because
    this is a rather interesting journey that you’ve taken,
    unique certainly.  What do you think religion is, what
    do you make of religion?

KA
    I see religion as an art form. Ever since we fell out of
    the trees and became recognisably human, we created
    religion and works of art to give us some sense that
    life has ultimate meaning and value to make us wonder,
    to hold us in an attitude of awe, because we’re meaning
    seeking creatures. We can’t exist, we fall into despair
    very easily and art is one of the things that
    transfigures reality for us, that makes us look more
    clearly and I think religion
    - you can see it as a moral aesthetic. By living in
      a certain way you transform yourself and I think
      beliefs are neither here nor there.

IV
    But if religion has been created by man to give meaning
    to the world, is there any absolute meaning to the world
    and life, or is it just a masquerade?

KA
    There is meaning  and it’s not just a masquerade, any
    more than art is masquerade but you can never articulate
    what that meaning is, anymore than you can say exactly
    what a Beethoven quartet means. You know that it affects
    you, you know that it touches you deeply, lifts you
    beyond yourself momentarily and gives you a sense that
    everything is at least for this moment all right, if
    there’s a world in which this can happen, it is all
    right.  That is what the best of religion should be,
    it’s that struggle to articulate meaning and to be
    compassionate to others.

IV
    And is this a human instinct, is this something built
    into our biological structure that we shall invent and
    believe in God?

KA
    Well, not all religions do believe much in gods, in the
    Buddhist tradition for example, gods are neither here
    nor there and in Hindu tradition, you could say the
    ritual is more important than the gods, in mythology,
    but, you’re right, our human mind is so constituted that
    we do have ideas and seek experiences that go beyond our
    grasp.  We look for ecstasy,  and if we don’t find it in
    a church or synagogue or a mosque, we’ll look for it in
    rock music, in theatre, music, drugs, sex, skiing even,
    sport, because we feel then, we are living most fully
    and religion was for most people until the
    popularisation of art, one of the chief means whereby
    they got hold of this ecstasy that we need to make our
    lives meaningful.

IV
    Then what has happened then to the humanist/atheist
    tradition that has swept the West in which religion is
    seen to have been discredited, as it were, disliked by
    the scientists who say it can’t be proved and therefore
    there is no evidence?  Are we doing it in the wrong
    vocabulary here?

KA
    Yes, we are. When you try and prove religious ideas and
    religious people themselves try and do this, it’s
    a great mistake I think, you get bad religion and bad
    science. You can’t prove the existence of God,  and
    everybody always entirely understood this and nobody
    until the modern period when we started to think in
    wholly literalistic, scientific ways, ever thought that
    scripture was designed as literal truth. But I’d like to
    make proviso; - this humanism, this liberalism, this
    distrust of religion is not the case in America. America
    is the second most religious country in the world after
    India and it’s not all fundamentalist by any means. The
    Americans - I spend a great deal of time there - half of
    them at least, are engaged on a very exciting quest and
    a rebellion against some of the traditions that they
    feel are too limiting and a desire to think and worship
    in a more pluralistic way, and a hunger for religious
    affairs. When I talk in America, people will have tears
    in their eyes, whereas here, people will at best say,
    well that’s frightfully interesting,  and see it as
    a sort of odd little quirk as though I were
    investigating the morays of an ancient Polynesian tribe
    but in America, they don’t think that religion is
    discredited; they’re becoming more religious all the
    time.

IV
    Have you lectured in an Islamic country?

KA
    I have and my books are published in Arabic.

IV
    Because these books while being scholarly, seem to
    answer some other need in you which is to crusade,
    - probably not too strong a word, although it’s word you
      hate, I know, because you feel it’s done so much
      damage throughout history - but nonetheless, you feel
      perhaps some mission to explain and that has focused
      your moral choices and your behaviour.

KA
    I have felt for most of my live an outsider. You know
    being a nun,  being a woman in a very male dominated
    church, being unmarried in a society dominated by
    coupledom, growing up catholic in a country where
    Catholicism is profoundly un-British and odd,  so I do
    feel an outsider and I’ve minded that very much.  When
    I see other people as outsiders, both on the global
    sphere or in our own country, I feel a great empathy and
    I recognise the pain that lies behind a lot of this
    frantic self assertion and I think they’re heading into
    very dangerous territories if we acerbate that pain by
    insensitive and ignorant denigrations of traditions that
    are incredibly rich and complex and can’t be just
    dismissed out of hand as they often are.

IV
    Well you’ve written much about the three religions in
    the book Christianity, Judaism and Islam.  More recently
    you’ve written about fundamentalism in all of those
    religions and you say that all religions at some point
    in the twentieth century have had a fundamentalist
    strand. That must give you great cause for concern.

KA
    No, it’s just one of those developments, because there
    are many, many types of religious expression but in
    these twentieth century, a militant form of piety grew
    up that was a rebellion against secular society. The
    first fundamentalist movement sprang up in the United
    States at the time of world war one. It reached the
    Islamic world not until a degree of modernisation had
    taken place in the late 1960s, everywhere western style
    government has developed, a religious counter-culture
    has grown up alongside it, wanting to drag God, the
    sacred religion back to centre stage. But also every
    single one of them has been a response to what is
    perceived to be an attack by secularists.Every single
    fundamentalist movement that I’ve studied in Judaism,
    Christianity and Islam is convinced that modern secular
    society wants to wipe them out. We must realise that
    fundamentalism has developed in a symbiotic relationship
    with an modernity an secularism that has been
    experienced as aggressive and invasive.

IV
    But nonetheless, it is the motive of many
    fundamentalists movements to re-conquer the secular
    world for their own faith.

KA
    I think, again, this is a bit of an overstatement. The
    vast majority of fundamentalists are simply trying to
    live what they regard as the religious life in a world
    that is increasingly hostile to religion and
    marginalises religion. The ultra orthodox Jews in New
    York for example, are not campaigning against the world,
    they’re simply creating a sort of sacred enclave where
    they can be Jewish as they see Judaism should be and
    a lot of the Islamic movements are like this too, a lot
    of the Christian fundamentalists in the United States
    are simply doing it; it’s a fringe percentage - a tiny
    proportion  - that take part in acts of terror and
    violence but certainly, they are trying to reclaim first
    of all their own patch for God and all fundamentalists
    begin with an attack, including Osama Bin Laden on your
    own society, only in a secondary phase, if at all do
    they take on a foreign foe.

IV
    Karen, do you believe that the way you live as it were,
    moving easily between these different concepts, is a way
    we might all move if we’re going to understand each
    other? I think it is extremely enjoyable to share the
    sacred sense that you have in a temple or a synagogue
    and I come from a Christian tradition?  Is that a matter
    of belief for you, that is how we must behave?

KA
    Yes, we can no longer be religious as we were in the
    days when we didn’t know about other traditions.  One of
    the great gifts of the twentieth century was that for
    the first time in history, we had a real knowledge of
    other people’s faith, not just their beliefs or their
    practices, but the faith that lay at the root of this,
    and now none of us really can look at our own faith in
    the same way again. And I would agree with one of my
    great mentors, a Canadian scholar, who was himself
    a Christian minister said that it’s now blasphemous to
    say that no one  tradition has the monopoly of truth,
    because we know so much more now.

IV
    And you in your inner life -  when you look forward to
    what you expect the world to offer you, when you look at
    your life ahead, do you expect to come upon some new
    meaning or do you feel that they are, all these meanings
    are now laid out before you and with the help of your
    scholarship you have found a meaning?

KA
    I’m finding a meaning. Once you say, that’s it, now I’ve
    got it and I can pickle it in aspic and make it an
    orthodoxy, then you’ve lost it, because you’re dragging
    it down to something very limited.  It’s a an ongoing
    process and the meaning of it is the process itself.
    One is continually going into the depths and into the
    depths of yourself, discovering a strength, a presence
    perhaps, in the heart of yourself but it’s like
    a relationship.

IV
    And does it make you happy?

KA
    Yes it does. I can’t wait to get to my desk in the
    morning, and somehow, after all the vicissitudes of my
    life,  I’ve suddenly found the way in and I think we all
    have to do that, we all somehow have to follow our bliss
    as it were, follow the thing that fills you, not anybody
    else but you with completion and delight.

IV
    I shall remember that, follow the bliss. Karen
    Armstrong, thank you.

KA
    Thank you.
