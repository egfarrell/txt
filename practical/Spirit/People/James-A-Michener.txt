    - Chapter 5: Psalm of the Hoopoe Bird.

- This chapter focuses on the character Jabaal (nick-named
  “Hoopoe”) the engineer/architect genius.

### Setting and Context
    - It takes place in the region of the fictional tell (mound)
      called Makor (in the hills of what will become northern
      Israel), around ca. 1040-970 BCE (roughly the last years of
      the reign of King David).
    - The town of Makor is of some strategic importance: it lies
      near a reliable spring/well (“the Source” in a symbolic
      sense) and holds value in terms of defence, water supply,
      and regional trade/power.
    - The religious-cultural background: the people of Makor are
      nominally Hebrews (or people interacting with Hebrew
      identity) but also still influenced by older fertility
      cults, Baal worship, and the ongoing shift toward
      monotheism (Yahweh).

### Main Characters
    - Jabaal “Hoopoe” — an engineer/architect of Makor. He’s
      described (in commentary) as short, a bit comical in
      appearance (muscular, big bottom that jiggled when he
      walked) but mentally sharp, driven, always “peering into
      holes” (hence the hoopoe-bird nickname). He dreams of
      securing Makor’s water supply and building something grand.
    - Meshab the Moabite — Hoopoe’s friend/slave/assistant. He
      helps with the engineering work and plays a key role in the
      tunnel project.
    - General Amram (or the governor of Makor) — Represents the
      political/military leadership of Makor, sceptical of
      Hoopoe’s radical plans.
    - Kerith — Hoopoe’s wife, of a Hebrew priestly line, devoted
      to the worship of Yahweh; romantic and ideological tensions
      exist between her and Hoopoe due to religious differences.
    - King David (external figure) — though not the main
      character here, his presence and policies affect the
      political-religious context of Makor and Hoopoe’s life.
      Hoopoe hopes for royal recognition; the story shows he
      doesn’t receive it.

### Plot Summary (Detailed)

    - Initial Situation & Hoopoe’s Vision Hoopoe is increasingly
      aware of Makor’s vulnerability: the town’s well/spring is
      outside or at least exposed, making it a weak point during
      siege or drought. He proposes a new water-system: a hidden
      tunnel (or a water-gallery) that links the well to the
      interior of the town, so that in time of siege the
      inhabitants could still draw water without the enemy
      knowing (or cutting off access). This is a bold, almost
      futuristic engineering notion in his era. BookRags summary:
      “Hoopoe wants to build a new water system but the governor
      of Makor is opposed to the idea.” BookRags Hoopoe’s
      personality emerges: he is imaginative, willing to question
      conventional wisdom, driven by a mixture of technical
      passion and urgency (for defence and survival). This is
      where the “visionary/mad-scientist” tone comes in: abnormal
      ambition, obsession with tunnels, holes, water, securing
      the town against threat.

    - Political Resistance & Religious Undertones
        - The town leadership (General Amram/governor) resists.
          Their arguments: cost, risk, that the existing well is
          adequate, perhaps suspicion of Hoopoe’s motives (he is
          “different”), and the fact that Hoopoe is tied to older
          traditions (he still worships Baal) whereas the town is
          shifting toward acknowledging Yahweh and aligning with
          King David’s policies.
        - Hoopoe’s wife, Kerith, is vocally in the Yahweh camp,
          and this ideological divide heightens tension: Hoopoe
          is portrayed as somewhat of a hold-out to older ways
          (fertility cults, Baal) while Kerith pushes toward the
          new monotheism. This religious conflict runs parallel
          to the engineering challenge: Hoopoe’s project is
          secular/technical but it gets entangled with faith and
          identity.

    - Survey, Planning & Construction
        - Hoopoe and Meshab venture to the waterfall/stream/well
          area outside Makor and conduct surveys/studies. They
          map out the rock, the slope, the natural spring, the
          geology of the hillside. Hoopoe’s mind leaps ahead: he
          visualises a tunnel system, vaulted galleries, hidden
          outlets. From the summary: “He discusses his plan with
          Meshab the Moabite and they go to study the waterfall
          and they plan the design of the water system.”
        - They begin excavation: cutting into rock, constructing
          the tunnel. Hoopoe’s genius shows: he designs features
          to allow water to flow by gravity, to hide the
          entrance, perhaps to divert enemies or invaders.
          However, the work is arduous; Meshab is indispensable
          in correcting errors Hoopoe makes (his brilliance is
          accompanied by human fallibility). Commentary: “Meshab
          … corrected a big mistake that Hoopoe had made in his
          calculation.”
        - Hoopoe faces obstacles: physical (rock hard to dig,
          cost, manpower, time), political (lack of support,
          possibly sabotage), and relational (his wife drifts
          away toward Kerith’s world of worship at Jerusalem, or
          at least ambition toward Jerusalem). Hoopoe, the
          engineer, is socially marginalised: he is “the nerd” in
          society, his genius not fully recognised.

    - Completion & Unrecognised Triumph
        - Eventually Hoopoe’s tunnel system is completed (or at
          least operative). The narrative describes how the
          hidden tunnel gives Makor an edge: during drought or
          siege the town can draw water, thereby enhancing its
          security and survival. Hoopoe thus accomplishes a major
          feat — saving the people of Makor for generations
          perhaps.
        - Despite the success, recognition is lacking: King David
          visits Makor to inspect things; Hoopoe hopes for royal
          commendation, but instead the king is distracted by
          a singer (a visiting musician) and takes him back to
          Jerusalem, leaving Hoopoe in limbo. Hoopoe’s wife
          Kerith leaves him (to Jerusalem) to worship Yahweh and
          follow a different path. General Amram continues to
          disdain Hoopoe’s achievement. The result: Hoopoe dies
          unappreciated, his social standing low in his final
          years. From commentary: “many unfortunate things
          happened to Hoopoe because of religion… Although Jabaal
          dedicated his life to saving the inhabitants of his
          town… his life was unsuccessful.”
        - The futility of personal genius in the face of
          political/religious forces is a theme. Hoopoe’s
          engineering marvel is overshadowed by the shift in
          worship, the priorities of secular/political
          leadership, and the rising monotheistic identity.

    Symbolic & Thematic Resonance
        - Hoopoe’s story functions as a symbol: engineering and
          human ingenuity applied to survival (water = life) but
          constrained by human structures of power and belief.
        - The tunnel is metaphorical: the underground link
          between past cults (fertility/Baal) and emerging
          worship (Yahweh); the private hidden work of the
          engineer reflects the hidden depths of faith, the
          subterranean forces of history.
        - Hoopoe himself embodies the tension between tradition
          and innovation: he is tied to old religion but working
          toward a future. He is visionary but also socially
          isolated. The narrative shows how genius doesn’t
          guarantee triumph in the larger sweep of history.
        - The chapter thus contributes to the larger novel’s
          theme of how human civilisation builds upon layers of
          faith, technology, and culture — and how these layers
          often hide as much as they reveal.

### Why the Engineer Is “Mad/Scientist”-ish
    - You asked if Hoopoe seems visionary and slightly “mad”
      — yes, you are on the right track. Here’s why that reading
      works and how to articulate it:
    - He pursues a radical plan (a secret tunnel) in an era when
      such engineering would be ahead of its time; this gives him
      the aura of someone pushing beyond accepted boundaries.
    - His emphasis on subterranean holes, hidden galleries,
      “always peering into holes” (like the hoopoe bird searching
      for insects) gives him a quirky, obsessive character trait
      — a hallmark of the “mad scientist” archetype: fixation,
      isolation, deep technical knowledge, lack of social
      recognition.
    - His personal life suffers because of the project (wife
      leaves, under-appreciation, conflict with authority). Often
      in literature the “engineer” or “inventor” figure
      sacrifices social or moral harmony for technical ambition
      — that is present here.
    - The interplay of belief and science: Hoopoe is rooted in
      fertility cult/Baal traditions yet his project is
      technological and secular. That disjunction adds to his
      “outsider” status — he doesn’t fully belong to the rising
      Yahweh-monotheist movement, and yet he builds for the
      community’s survival. That tension gives dramatic edge to
      his “weirdness” in the novel.
    - The story suggests his feat will perhaps have long-lasting
      value (for Makor’s safety) but he remains anonymous. That
      mismatch between world-changing work and personal oblivion
      is a classic “mad scientist” pathos.

### End-Of-Chapter Coda & Its Place in the Novel
    - This chapter is level “V” or chapter “5” in the historical
      sequence of The Source (though counting may differ in
      editions). On the Wikipedia chapter list: “Psalm of the
      Hoopoe Bird – Takes place 1040–970 BCE, during the last
      years of King David.”
    - Later in the novel (in the frame story in the 1960s dig)
      the archaeologists will discover the tunnel or remnants of
      the water system built by Hoopoe beneath Makor, confirming
      the significance of this layer and linking ancient
      engineering with modern excavation. In this way, Hoopoe’s
      work is not just a story but a physical layer under Makor’s
      ruin — tying the novel’s theme of archaeology and time.
    - The chapter shadows the shift from tribal/settled society
      into the Israelite kingdom, foregrounding technology
      (engineering) and faith (monotheism) as twin engines of
      civilisation.
