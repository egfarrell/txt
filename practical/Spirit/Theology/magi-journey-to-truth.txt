
- The author, Evelyn Waugh, wrote a novel in 1950 called Helena.
  Helena was the mother of the first Christian Roman emperor,
  Constantine. In the novel Helena goes to Bethlehem for the
  feast of the Epiphany and she encounters the re-enactment of
  the coming of the Magi. In response, she prays a prayer that
  comes straight from her heart. It is a prayer for her son, who
  at the time was still unbaptized. Her prayer includes the
  lines, “You are my especial patrons and patrons of all
  late-comers, of all who have had a tedious journey to make to
  the truth, of all who are confused with knowledge and
  speculation, of all _who through politeness make themselves
  partners in guilt, of all who stand in danger by reason of
  their talents_. (?) Dear cousins, pray for me, and for my poor
  overloaded son. May he, too, before the end find kneeling-space
  in the straw’

- Helena’s prayer acknowledges the magi as symbols of all those
  who struggle towards the truth by strange routes. They were
  outsiders to the world of Mary, Joseph and their child.
  According to the gospel reading, they came from the east, which
  at that time would have been identified with the area
  corresponding to modern day Iran, ancient Persia. ‘Magi’ is
  a Persian word meaning wise man, priest, an expert in
  astrology, an interpreter of dreams. According to the gospel
  reading, these somewhat exotic characters saw an unusual star
  rising in the sky. This natural phenomenon attracted their
  attention. It spoke to their inquiring minds and their restless
  hearts. They sensed it would lead them to the birthplace of
  a great ruler. As they observed and followed this star, they
  were led westwards to Jerusalem.

- They were not yet in Bethlehem, which is ten kilometers south
  of Jerusalem. The wonders of nature alone could not bring them
  to Bethlehem. They needed the help of the Jewish Scriptures.
  The chief priests and scribes, having consulted the Scriptures,
  made known to them, through Herod, that the infant king of the
  Jews would be born in Bethlehem. It was there that these
  strangers shared their gifts of gold, frankincense and myrrh
  with this humble Jewish couple. It is said of them that
  ‘falling to their knees, they did him homage’. For the
  evangelist, writing fifty years after the death and
  resurrection of Jesus, these magi symbolize the pagans who had
  been responding in faith to the preaching of the gospel. The
  magi’s worship of the child Jesus at the beginning of Matthew’s
  gospel anticipates the final words of the risen Lord to his
  disciples at the end of Matthew’s gospel, ‘Go, make disciples
  of all nations’. The visit of these pagans to Jesus reflects
  the words of Paul in our second reading, ‘pagans now share the
  same inheritance… they are parts of the same body… the same
  promise has been made to them, in Christ Jesus, through the
  gospel’.

- In the words of Helena’s prayer, these magi ‘had a tedious
  journey to make to the truth’, to the one who would declare, ‘I
  am the truth’. We are all on a journey towards the Lord, and
  sometimes our journey can be experienced as ‘tedious’. We
  struggle to find the Lord in the confusion and pressures of
  life. In the language of Helena’s prayer, we can sometimes feel
  like ‘late-comers’ on the journey of faith, falling behind
  others who seem to walk with a surer and firmer step. Yet, what
  matters is not where we are on the journey but our willingness
  to remain on the journey. The Lord is always drawing us to
  himself, and what the Lord asks of us is that we keep holding
  ourselves open to the drawing power of his love. We are to keep
  seeking him, trusting in his promise, ‘seek and you will find’.

- In this regard, we too can look to the magi as our patrons.
  They were a long way from Bethlehem when they set out; it
  wasn’t a straightforward journey. The poet T.S. Eliot captures
  that beautifully in the opening lines of his poem, The Journey
  of the Magi, ‘A cold coming we had of it, Just the worst time
  of the year For a journey, and such a long journey: The ways
  deep and the weather sharp, The very dead of winter’. Yet, they
  remained faithful to the journey and that is what the Lord asks
  of us all, to keep journeying on in faith. The Lord will find
  ways to make the journey shorter. The journey is not all down
  to us. The Lord keeps drawing us towards himself, sometimes
  through the signs of nature, like the stars, sometimes through
  the Scriptures, and sometimes through others, including through
  those who seem strange and exotic to us. Eventually, at the end
  of our earthly pilgrimage, the Lord will bring us to Bethlehem,
  a Hebrew word meaning, ‘House of Bread’. At the banquet of
  eternal life the Lord will fully satisfy our deepest hunger and
  thirst. This banquet is anticipated at every Eucharist where
  the Lord feeds us with the bread of his word and the bread of
  his body. As we journey towards Bethlehem, like the magi we
  have our own gifts to offer the Lord, and the greatest gift we
  can offer him is the gift of our love, expressed in our love of
  all those whom the Lord loves.
