Yes. What you are recalling is real, but it helps to be precise about what exists, what was intended, and what was never completed.

Bonhoeffer did not leave a finished book. What survives are prison letters, theological fragments, outlines, and marginal notes written mainly between 1943 and 1945 while he was imprisoned by the Nazis. These materials were later edited and published posthumously under the title Letters and Papers from Prison. Within these writings he repeatedly refers to a project he hoped to write, sometimes explicitly calling it a “book,” centered on what he famously called a “religionless” or “non-religious” interpretation of Christianity. That book was never written, but the conceptual core of it is visible in these notes.

I’ll break this down into context, the central ideas, what Bonhoeffer meant by “religionless,” what he was reacting against, and how scholars understand the unfinished project.

Historical and biographical context

Bonhoeffer was arrested in April 1943 for involvement in resistance activities against Hitler, including connections to plots within the Abwehr. He spent time first in Tegel military prison in Berlin, later in Gestapo custody, and was executed in April 1945 at Flossenbürg concentration camp.

During imprisonment he was largely cut off from academic life. His theological writing took the form of letters to friends and family (especially his friend and confidant Eberhard Bethge), short essays, outlines, and personal reflections. These were not polished manuscripts; many are exploratory, tentative, and deliberately experimental.

Importantly, this phase comes after his earlier major works: Sanctorum Communio, Act and Being, The Cost of Discipleship, and Ethics. The prison writings are not a repudiation of those works, but a radical rethinking prompted by historical catastrophe and his sense that Western culture had fundamentally changed.

The problem Bonhoeffer thought he was facing

Bonhoeffer believed that modern humanity had reached what he called “the coming of age of the world.” By this he meant that science, technology, political autonomy, and critical reason had rendered traditional religious explanations increasingly implausible and unnecessary in everyday life.

Key claims here:

• People no longer need God as a hypothesis to explain the world.
• Appeals to God as a solution to ignorance, fear, or moral weakness no longer convince.
• Traditional religious language has become unintelligible or dishonest in public life.

He thought Christianity had too often responded defensively, trying to preserve religion rather than tell the truth about God.

This leads to his famous criticism of God as a “stopgap” or Deus ex machina: a God invoked only at the limits of human knowledge, power, or endurance. Bonhoeffer believed this conception was both theologically false and pastorally corrupting.

What he meant by “religion”

This is crucial. Bonhoeffer does not use “religion” as a synonym for Christianity.

When he criticizes religion, he means a historically conditioned complex that includes:

• Metaphysical speculation about a supernatural realm
• Individualistic concern for salvation or the afterlife
• Ritual and piety detached from concrete life
• God used to fill explanatory or psychological gaps
• A sacred–profane division that removes God from ordinary life

He thought much of modern Christianity had become trapped in this framework, which he believed was collapsing.

So “religionless Christianity” does not mean atheism, moralism, or secular humanism. It means Christianity freed from those religious accretions.

Core ideas of the religionless Christianity project

    God revealed in weakness, not power

Bonhoeffer’s prison theology is decisively Christological. He insists that God is known not in domination or transcendence, but in the crucified Christ.

His most famous line here is that “only the suffering God can help.”

This is not sentimental. He means that God refuses to compete with human autonomy or intervene as a supernatural force. Instead, God is present in self-giving, vulnerability, and solidarity with human suffering.

    This-worldliness

Bonhoeffer argues strongly against what he sees as Christian otherworldliness.

Faith is not about escape from the world, inner piety, or preparation for death. It is about full participation in life, responsibility, action, and suffering in the world as it is.

He explicitly says Christians should live “fully in the world,” sharing in its joys and burdens without religious privilege.

    Christ existing as community

This theme continues his earlier work. Christ is not primarily an object of belief or a metaphysical entity, but a living reality present in the community that lives for others.

In prison he radicalizes this: the church is church only when it exists for others, especially the marginalized, oppressed, and suffering.

Religionless Christianity therefore implies a non-religious church: one that does not assert power, demand cultural dominance, or retreat into private spirituality.

    Non-religious interpretation of biblical concepts

Bonhoeffer believed core Christian concepts like God, sin, faith, repentance, and salvation had to be reinterpreted without reliance on outdated religious frameworks.

Examples of what he hints at:

• Sin understood less as individual moral failure and more as refusal of responsibility for others
• Faith understood not as belief in doctrines but as participation in the life of Christ
• God understood not as a supernatural being “out there” but as encountered in ethical responsibility and suffering love

He does not fully work these out. Many notes explicitly say things like “this must be developed further.”

    Silence, secrecy, and the “arcane discipline”

Bonhoeffer was deeply aware of the danger of cheapening these ideas. He worried that talk of religionless Christianity could be misunderstood as liberal reductionism.

He therefore suggests something like an “arcane discipline,” a restrained and careful communication of faith, avoiding religious slogans and avoiding misuse in mass culture.

This is not elitism, but caution: he believed the church must earn the right to speak by action.

The unfinished book

In several letters from 1944, Bonhoeffer mentions that he is sketching an outline for a book on a non-religious interpretation of Christianity. Some notes titled “Outline for a Book” survive.

What is striking is how preliminary they are. They consist of headings, questions, short paragraphs, and reminders rather than arguments.

There is no evidence that he had a completed manuscript hidden away, nor that he had fully resolved the tensions in the ideas. He was very explicit that he was thinking “experimentally” and that much remained unclear even to him.

Scholarly consensus is that:

• The project was real and serious.
• It was not yet mature.
• Bonhoeffer knew it was dangerous and easily misunderstood.
• His execution cut the work short before systematic development.

Later reception and misunderstandings

After the war, these prison writings had enormous influence, especially in the 1960s.

Some theologians interpreted Bonhoeffer as endorsing secularization theology, the “death of God,” or a purely ethical Christianity. Most contemporary scholars think this goes too far.

Bonhoeffer never abandoned Christ, the cross, prayer, Scripture, or the church. He abandoned a certain cultural form of religion.

If you want a single sentence summary of what he was reaching for, it might be something like this:

A Christianity centered on the crucified Christ, lived fully in the world, without religious privilege, metaphysical escape, or moral superiority.

    How Bonhoeffer’s prison theology differs from liberal theology

At first glance, “religionless Christianity” sounds liberal: less dogma, less metaphysics, more ethics, more engagement with the modern world. That superficial similarity caused a lot of confusion after the war. But at a deep level Bonhoeffer is reacting against liberal theology at least as much as against traditional religion.

Here are the main fault lines.

First: starting point

Classical liberal theology (think Schleiermacher, Ritschl, early 20th-century Protestantism) starts with human religious experience: feeling, consciousness, moral intuition, cultural development. God-talk is shaped to fit that experience.

Bonhoeffer starts with Christ, specifically the crucified Christ, and refuses to let human experience define what God can be. The “coming of age” of the world does not authorize us to redesign Christianity; it forces us to ask what Christ means when religion collapses.

So the direction of explanation is opposite:
liberal theology: human experience → reinterpret God
Bonhoeffer: God in Christ → reinterpret human life

Second: view of God

Liberal theology often “thins out” God to preserve plausibility: God as moral ideal, depth of meaning, symbol of values, or religious self-understanding.

Bonhoeffer is ruthless against this. For him, a God reduced to human values is no God at all. His prison writings insist that God is real, active, and demanding precisely where religion fails. God is not less than real because he does not intervene; he is real in a different, cruciform way.

This is why Bonhoeffer says God allows himself to be “pushed out of the world onto the cross.” That is not liberal accommodation; it is a very hard, paradoxical theology of revelation.

Third: sin and judgment

Liberal theology tends to soften sin into ignorance, immaturity, or social dysfunction.

Bonhoeffer never does this. In fact, prison sharpens his sense of guilt, responsibility, and judgment. His Ethics manuscript insists that human beings are implicated in evil not merely by intent but by participation in historical structures.

Religionless Christianity is not morally relaxed. It is morally more severe, because it removes religious excuses.

Fourth: grace and cost

Bonhoeffer’s lifelong opposition to “cheap grace” does not disappear in prison. If anything, it becomes harsher.

Liberal Christianity often emphasizes acceptance without transformation. Bonhoeffer insists that following Christ entails suffering, loss of security, and responsibility without guarantees.

So while liberal theology tries to make Christianity easier to believe, Bonhoeffer is trying to make it harder to misuse.

In short: Bonhoeffer is not liberalizing Christianity; he is de-religionizing it while intensifying its Christological and ethical demands.

    Comparison with later secular or post-Christian thought

Bonhoeffer is often claimed as a precursor by later secular theologies, but the fit is partial and sometimes strained.

Let’s distinguish a few trajectories.

a) “Death of God” theology (1960s)

Think of Altizer, Hamilton, van Buren.

They took Bonhoeffer’s language about God being pushed out of the world and interpreted it literally or metaphysically: God has died, or God no longer exists as a transcendent being, and Christianity becomes purely ethical or symbolic.

Most scholars agree this goes beyond Bonhoeffer. For him, God’s weakness is not God’s absence. The cross is not the disappearance of God but God’s chosen mode of presence.

Bonhoeffer would likely have said that “death of God” theology collapses back into liberalism by making human autonomy the final authority.

b) Secular humanism

Bonhoeffer shares some surface concerns with secular humanism: responsibility, maturity, rejection of supernatural stopgaps.

But the grounding is totally different.

For secular humanism, responsibility arises from human reason, solidarity, or evolutionary ethics. For Bonhoeffer, responsibility arises from being addressed and claimed by Christ, even when God is silent.

Crucially, Bonhoeffer does not think we can justify responsibility to ourselves. Responsibility is obedience without metaphysical reassurance.

That difference matters a lot.

c) Post-Christian or post-theistic thought

Later thinkers influenced by Nietzsche, Heidegger, or postmodernism often argue that Christianity is one narrative among others, no longer normatively binding.

Bonhoeffer does not do this. He never relativizes Christ.

In fact, one of the tensions in his prison theology is that Christ remains absolute even as religious language collapses. He does not solve that tension; he lives inside it.

You could say Bonhoeffer anticipates post-Christian conditions but refuses post-Christian conclusions.

d) Radical political theology

Some later political theologians resonate strongly with Bonhoeffer’s emphasis on suffering, solidarity, and responsibility.

Here the affinity is real. But again, Bonhoeffer’s Christology prevents a full identification. The church exists for others not because ethics demands it, but because Christ takes that form.

So Bonhoeffer is best seen not as a founder of secular theology, but as a theologian of faith after the end of cultural Christianity.

    Is the project coherent, or self-contradictory?

This is the hardest and most debated question.

There are three main scholarly positions.

Position 1: The project is coherent but unfinished

Many Bonhoeffer scholars argue that the apparent contradictions dissolve when read in light of his earlier work.

On this view:
• Religionless Christianity continues his themes of Christ as concrete reality
• His rejection of religion targets cultural forms, not theological substance
• The tensions are real but deliberate, not confused

According to this reading, Bonhoeffer was moving toward a radically Christocentric theology that could survive secular modernity, but he was killed before he could systematize it.

Position 2: The project is fertile but unstable

Others think Bonhoeffer intentionally destabilized theology without resolving the consequences.

They point out tensions such as:
• God is hidden yet commanding
• Christianity is non-religious yet centered on worship
• Christ is absolute yet not metaphysically described

From this angle, religionless Christianity is not a doctrine but a provocation. Its value lies in forcing theology to abandon false securities, not in offering a new system.

Bonhoeffer himself sometimes sounds like this, especially when he speaks of “experimenting” or “not yet seeing clearly.”

Position 3: The project contains unresolved contradictions

A minority but serious view holds that Bonhoeffer never solved, and perhaps could not solve, the tension between non-religious interpretation and Christian confession.

Critics argue:
• If God is not a being “out there,” it becomes unclear what distinguishes God from ethical ideals
• If religious language is discarded, it is unclear how proclamation works
• If Christ is present only in community and action, transcendence risks disappearing

On this reading, Bonhoeffer points toward something powerful but leaves it conceptually unstable.

Most contemporary scholars occupy a middle position: Bonhoeffer is neither incoherent nor complete. His prison writings are fragmentary by nature, but they are not careless. The tensions are part of a theology shaped under extreme historical pressure, where neat resolutions would themselves be dishonest.

A final thought

One way to read Bonhoeffer fruitfully is not to ask, “Did he solve the problem of religion in modernity?” but rather:

What kind of faith remains truthful when religion no longer protects it?

Bonhoeffer did not answer that question fully. But he re-posed it in a way that still unsettles both religious complacency and secular self-confidence.

God does not intervene to stop history’s horrors. God refuses to be a “deus ex machina.” God allows himself to be pushed out of the world and onto the cross.

This is not a statement about God’s inner being in a speculative sense. Bonhoeffer is very cautious about talking about God “in himself.”

This is why Bonhoeffer’s focus remains ethical and ecclesial:
• God is found in responsibility
• Christ exists as community
• The church is for others
• Faith is participation, not metaphysical insight

The cross tells us where God meets us, not how divine attributes are restructured.

Bonhoeffer’s restraint here is deliberate. He distrusts speculative theology, especially after seeing how easily it can be co-opted by ideology.

Moltmann therefore reconfigures classical attributes:
• Omnipotence becomes vulnerable love
• Immutability is rethought as faithfulness in suffering
• Divine impassibility is explicitly rejected

This is bold, systematic, and metaphysical in a way Bonhoeffer never allows himself to be.

The cry of dereliction is for Moltmann a window into the trinitarian life of God itself.

Bonhoeffer never makes that move.

• Metaphysical God-talk has lost public credibility
• The church has catastrophically failed
• Religious language has been weaponized

So he deliberately limits himself to what can be said from within discipleship, suffering, and obedience.

Moltmann, writing after the war, in an academic context, is trying to reconstruct doctrine in light of the cross. He believes theology must risk speculative reformulation to avoid moral bankruptcy.

Now the long answer.

What Bonhoeffer was trying to protect

By the time of the prison letters, Bonhoeffer is guarding several things very fiercely.

    God must not become an explanatory principle

God must not explain suffering, history, evil, or meaning. God is not the reason things happen as they do. Any theology that turns God into a metaphysical account of reality is, for Bonhoeffer, already corrupt.

    God must not compete with human responsibility

If God is conceived as an agent who intervenes, rescues, or balances the moral scales, then humans are relieved of responsibility. Bonhoeffer wants a God whose presence intensifies responsibility rather than replacing it.

    God must not be religiously consumable

Bonhoeffer fears a God who comforts, reassures, or legitimates the church while leaving structures of injustice untouched. The cross must remain a scandal, not a resource.

    God must remain hidden in weakness

The cross is not a spectacle of divine truth but the point where God renounces recognizability. God is present precisely where God cannot be used.

These are all negative safeguards rather than positive doctrines. Bonhoeffer is more concerned with preventing misuse than with constructing a system.

What Moltmann is trying to do

Moltmann shares these concerns, but he thinks Bonhoeffer’s restraint leaves theology exposed in another way.

Moltmann worries that if divine suffering is not affirmed as real in God, then suffering remains external to God and therefore ultimately unresolved or unredeemed. Worse, God risks becoming a silent observer who merely accompanies suffering without being affected by it.

So Moltmann’s project is constructive:

• To say that God truly suffers is to say suffering matters eternally.
• To place the cross inside God’s own life is to prevent God from becoming a distant moral witness.
• To reform doctrine is to prevent classical attributes (impassibility, immutability) from insulating God from history.

In this sense, Moltmann believes he is protecting the cross from domestication by metaphysics that would render it merely pedagogical or symbolic.

Where Moltmann succeeds (by Bonhoeffer’s standards)

There are real points of convergence.

    Moltmann blocks triumphant theism

By insisting that suffering belongs to God, Moltmann makes it very difficult to imagine God as the guarantor of success, empire, or moral order. This aligns strongly with Bonhoeffer’s anti-religious impulse.

    Moltmann intensifies the scandal of the cross

The cry of dereliction is not absorbed into a harmonious divine plan. It ruptures God’s own life. That preserves the cross as an event that resists easy reconciliation.

    Moltmann underwrites solidarity with sufferers

If God suffers, then suffering is not something to be explained away or bypassed. This gives Moltmann’s theology real ethical and political bite, often in ways Bonhoeffer would have recognized.

Where Moltmann risks undoing Bonhoeffer’s safeguards

This is where the critique sharpens.

    Weakness becomes a new divine property

For Bonhoeffer, weakness is a mode of God’s presence, not a definable attribute.

In Moltmann, suffering risks becoming something God “has” in the way God once “had” omnipotence. Even if redefined, it can be systematized, taught, and conceptually mastered.

Bonhoeffer would worry that this turns the cross into knowledge rather than judgment.

    Metaphysics re-enters through the back door

Moltmann insists that he is reforming metaphysics, not restoring it. But Bonhoeffer’s suspicion would remain: once you speak about God’s inner trinitarian suffering, you are again explaining God.

This explanation can then be used to justify claims about history, progress, or divine purpose — precisely what Bonhoeffer feared.

    The cross becomes intelligible

For Bonhoeffer, the cross is finally not something we understand but something we are addressed by.

Moltmann, by contrast, gives an account of how the cross “works” within God. That makes it intellectually meaningful in a way that may blunt its disruptive force.

    Responsibility may be softened

If God is already suffering within Godself, human suffering can be absorbed into a larger divine drama. Bonhoeffer’s harsher claim — that God leaves the world to human responsibility — becomes harder to sustain.

The deepest disagreement

At bottom, the question is this:

Should theology dare to say what God is like in order to prevent abuse, or should it refuse to say what God is like in order to prevent abuse?

Bonhoeffer answers by refusal and restraint. Moltmann answers by reconstruction.

Bonhoeffer trusts discipline and silence more than theory. Moltmann trusts reformed doctrine more than silence.

Neither choice is obviously safe.

A balanced assessment

Many contemporary theologians think Moltmann does preserve Bonhoeffer’s moral insight, but not his methodological austerity.

You could put it like this:

Bonhoeffer protects God from being used.
Moltmann protects God from being indifferent.

Whether Moltmann’s move is faithful or a betrayal depends on whether you think the greater danger today is a God who explains too much, or a God who risks explaining nothing at all.

That tension is not something either of them resolves. It may be one of the permanent burdens of post-Auschwitz theology.
