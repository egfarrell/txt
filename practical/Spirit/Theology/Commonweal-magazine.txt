# A Brief History of Commonweal
- commonwealmagazine.org

- Founded 1924, Commonweal is oldest independent `lay-edited`
  Catholic journal of opinion in U.S. Longstanding interests:
    - social justice, ecumenism, just-war theory, liturgical
      renewal, women’s issues, primacy of conscience,
      Catholicism–liberal democracy dialogue.

### 1920s
    - First issue Nov 1924, shortly after Time, before The New
      Yorker. Founder Michael Williams, Canadian-born journalist,
      after failed commune and reconversion to Catholicism,
      sought to convey Catholic social and intellectual outlook
      to American public.
    - Backed by Calvert Associates—Catholic business and academic
      elites incl. Ralph Adams Cram, Fr. Lawrason Riggs, John J.
      Raskob.
    - Financial instability constant; Thomas Woodlock called it
      “an adventure, not a business.” Calvert Associates promoted
      broader mission via local chapters, radio, apologetics
      books.
    - Early reputation “liberal” due to lay independence and
      ecumenism, though tone was defensively triumphalist;
      Commonweal proclaimed itself standing on Petrine Rock
      against pagan hedonism.

### 1930s
    - Great Depression era saw support for New Deal, aligned with
      Catholic social teaching on common good. Managing editor
      George Shuster warned early about Hitler, urged U.S.
      boycott of 1936 Berlin Olympics, questioned Catholic
      support for Franco.
    - Williams instead steered magazine pro-Franco, organizing
      1937 Madison Square Garden rally; Shuster resigned,
      Williams removed amid erratic behavior. Control passed 1938
      to Philip Burnham and Edward Skillin, pledging
      non-propagandistic editorial stance. Declared neutrality in
      Spanish Civil War, losing 20% circulation but reinforcing
      independence from Catholic mainstream.

### 1940s
    - Editors divided on WWII until Pearl Harbor; united in labor
      support, racial justice, and opposition to internment
      camps. Catholicism became more grassroots and activist;
      contributors included John Cort, George H. Dunne (“The Sin
      of Segregation”). Magazine condemned atomic bomb
      unanimously. Religious content featured Fr. H. A. Reinhold,
      Dorothy Day, Thomas Merton; 25th anniversary issue (1949)
      included Maritain, J. F. Powers, Williams, Shuster. Late
      decade leadership passed to John Cogley and James O’Gara,
      joined by William Clancy and William Pfaff—liberal,
      Irish-Catholic, lay-centered cohort.

### 1950s
    - Commonweal stood nearly alone among Catholic publications
      opposing Joseph McCarthy, criticizing his reckless tactics.
      Targeted by McCarthy in 1953; editors publicly denounced in
      parishes as Communist sympathizers. Opposition cemented
      magazine’s reputation for principled dissent within
      Catholic life.

### 1960s
    - Key concerns: Kennedy presidency, Vatican II, communism,
      Vietnam. Election of JFK encouraged Catholic engagement
      with American society. Vatican II welcomed as moment of
      openness, dialogue, lay participation; extensively covered,
      incl. Hans Küng. Enthusiasm dampened by 1968 Humanae vitae.
      Magazine remained anticommunist but rejected Cold War
      binaries. Initially supported Vietnam War; by 1964 urged
      negotiation, by 1965 praised protests, by 1967 advocated
      civil disobedience against escalation.

### 1970s
    - After Roe v. Wade (1973), Commonweal criticized decision
      while warning abortion politics would be co-opted by right;
      faulted bishops’ moral credibility.
    - Shifted to biweekly publication in 1974.
    - Editor James O’Gara articulated expansive post-Vatican II
      vision: wealth redistribution, limits on papal power, lay
      participation. Influential columnists John Garvey and
      Abigail McCarthy debuted. Election of John Paul II (1978)
      raised concerns about authoritarian governance and
      suppression of theologians.

### 1980s
    - Abortion remained central topic; editors warned against
      legalism and moral simplification. Leadership passed from
      O’Gara to Peter Steinfels (1984), then Margaret O’Brien
      Steinfels (1988).
    - Editorial staff diversified; women’s role in Church
      scrutinized, especially after 1985 synod. Ownership
      transferred to nonprofit Commonweal Foundation. Edward
      Skillin’s long stewardship (1933–1999) proved decisive for
      survival and direction.

### 1990s
    - Magazine foregrounded laity as future of Church, dedicating
      annual issues to lay responsibility amid institutional
      exclusion. Peggy Steinfels argued Commonweal itself modeled
      unauthorized lay initiative.
    - Seventy-fifth anniversary issue (1999) debated “crisis of
      liberal Catholicism”: Cardinal Francis George declared
      liberalism exhausted; Peter Steinfels defended its
      necessity for self-critique and dialogue. Magazine also
      published communitarian critiques of political liberalism
      (Lasch, McCarraher).

### 2000s
    - Post-9/11 editorials warned against unilateralism and
      endless war; opposed Afghanistan and Iraq wars,
      anti-Islamic rhetoric.
    - Clerical sexual-abuse crisis exposed leadership vacuum;
      Peter Steinfels sharply criticized episcopal failures
      (2002). Commonweal condemned Rome’s 2005 removal of America
      editor Thomas Reese, SJ. Death of John Paul II marked end
      of long, disciplinarian pontificate. Paul Baumann became
      editor in 2003.

### 2010s
    - Amid political and ecclesial polarization, Commonweal
      reaffirmed compatibility of Catholicism and liberal
      democracy.
    - Criticized U.S. bishops’ partisan alignment and “religious
      liberty” rhetoric under Obama. Ninetieth anniversary (2014)
      reaffirmed dual commitments: Catholic faith and democratic
      accountability. Dominic Preziosi became editor in 2018.
      Magazine shifted to monthly format in 2019; print
      circulation stabilized while digital reach reached historic
      highs.
