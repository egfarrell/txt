CATHOLIC DISCERNMENT, PROVIDENCE AND THE DRAWING OF LOTS
=========================================================

A brief theological summary

1. TRUST IN PROVIDENCE
----------------------

The Catechism of the Catholic Church teaches that the Christian attitude
towards the future is one of confidence in Divine Providence.

CCC 2115:

    "A sound Christian attitude consists in putting oneself confidently
    into the hands of Providence for whatever concerns the future..."

But the same paragraph immediately adds that "improvidence" can itself
be a failure of responsibility.

So Christian trust in Providence does NOT mean:

    - refusing ordinary prudence;
    - ignoring physical, financial or other real circumstances;
    - demanding supernatural certainty about the future;
    - treating every unusual event as a divine sign.

Rather, it means entrusting the future to God while responsibly dealing
with what is actually in front of us.

Source:
Catechism of the Catholic Church, §§2115-2117
https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_three/section_two/chapter_one/article_1/iii_you_shall_have_no_other_gods_before_me.html


2. DIVINATION AND "LOTS"
------------------------

CCC 2116 gives an explicit warning:

    "All forms of divination are to be rejected..."

It specifically includes:

    "recourse to Satan or demons, conjuring up the dead or other practices
    falsely supposed to 'unveil' the future."

It also explicitly mentions:

    "interpretation of omens and lots"

among the practices to be rejected.

The important distinction is between:

    A. Using a random method simply to make an otherwise arbitrary
       practical choice;

and

    B. Using a lot, sign, ritual or omen as a means of discovering
       God's hidden will or obtaining knowledge of the future.

The second is what comes much closer to the Catechism's warning.

Therefore, for a major spiritual decision such as:

    "Does God want me to extend this pilgrimage?"

it is not appropriate to treat the result of a religiously charged
drawing of lots as God's revealed answer.


3. THE BIBLICAL EXAMPLE OF ACTS 1
---------------------------------

The New Testament really does contain an example of the Apostles
casting lots.

In Acts 1, after Judas's death, the Apostles identify two candidates
to replace him. They pray:

    "Lord, you know the hearts of all, show us which one of these two
    you have chosen..."

They then cast lots and Matthias is selected.

Therefore it would be wrong to say:

    "The Bible never contains the drawing of lots."

It does.

However, Acts 1 does NOT establish a general Christian rule that
individuals should use lots whenever they are uncertain about God's
will.

The situation is highly particular:

    - the Twelve Apostles are being restored;
    - two qualified candidates have already been identified;
    - the community prays together;
    - the matter concerns the unique apostolic foundation of the Church.

The New Testament does not subsequently present "drawing lots" as
the normal method of Christian discernment.


4. POPE FRANCIS AND IGNATIAN DISCERNMENT
----------------------------------------

Pope Francis has spoken directly about this in his catechesis on
discernment.

On 7 September 2022, while discussing Ignatian discernment, he said
that discernment is not:

    "a sort of oracle or fatalism"

and contrasted it with deciding by:

    "casting one's lot on two possibilities."

This is important because Ignatian discernment is not primarily about
obtaining an externally generated answer.

It involves learning to recognise movements within oneself over time,
especially movements of consolation and desolation, and examining
where those movements lead.

Source:
Pope Francis, General Audience, 7 September 2022
https://www.vatican.va/content/francesco/en/audiences/2022/documents/20220907-udienza-generale.html


5. DISCERNMENT IS NOT THE SAME AS SEEKING SIGNS
------------------------------------------------

Christian discernment does not mean:

    "Give me a sign and I will know what to do."

Instead it involves:

    - prayer;
    - freedom;
    - reason;
    - attention to circumstances;
    - Scripture and the teaching of the Church;
    - appropriate spiritual counsel;
    - awareness of consolation and desolation;
    - examination of the fruits of a movement;
    - patience and time.

In Ignatian terms, one should not judge a spiritual movement solely
by how attractive or powerful it feels at its beginning.

It is important to consider the beginning, middle and end of the
movement.

A movement which begins beautifully is not thereby guaranteed to have
a good conclusion.

Likewise, temporary confusion does not necessarily mean that the
original experience was false.


6. CONSOLATION IS NOT SIMPLY "FEELING GOOD"
-------------------------------------------

Ignatian discernment asks what a movement produces.

A genuinely good movement tends towards things such as:

    - greater faith;
    - hope;
    - charity;
    - freedom;
    - humility;
    - generosity;
    - peace;
    - greater capacity to love God and neighbour.

One should therefore ask not merely:

    "How powerful is this feeling?"

but:

    "Where is this movement taking me?"

and:

    "What kind of person am I becoming through it?"

Pope Francis repeatedly emphasises that discernment requires time
and attention to the actual fruits of spiritual movements.


7. AN IMPORTANT DISTINCTION: TRUST VS CERTAINTY
------------------------------------------------

There is a subtle difference between:

    "Lord, I do not know what tomorrow holds, but I trust You."

and:

    "Lord, please give me a sufficiently clear sign so that I can
     know exactly what You want me to do."

The first is trust in Providence.

The second can become an attempt to obtain certainty about Providence.

The Christian vocation is not necessarily to know God's plan in
advance.

It is to remain faithful while not possessing that knowledge.

Therefore:

    TRUST does not require CERTAINTY.


8. APPLYING THIS TO A MAJOR PERSONAL DISCERNMENT
------------------------------------------------

For a major decision such as extending an open-ended pilgrimage,
a drawing of lots should not be treated as establishing:

    "God has told me to do this."

If a lot has already been drawn, there is no need to panic or conclude
that the whole experience was spiritually bad.

A sensible response is simply:

    "I will not use the result of that lot as evidence of God's will."

Then return to ordinary Christian discernment.

The result can be released rather than obeyed or fought against.


9. THE ROLE OF ORDINARY PRUDENCE
--------------------------------

Trust in Providence does not eliminate ordinary prudence.

Physical health matters.
Financial responsibilities matter.
The advice of trustworthy people matters.
The judgement of a spiritual director matters.
Rest can matter.
Time can matter.

None of these things constitute a lack of faith.

In fact, CCC 2115 explicitly places confidence in Providence alongside
a warning against "improvidence".

Therefore:

    "I should continue because I must trust God"

is not automatically more faithful than:

    "I should rest, seek counsel, attend to my responsibilities,
     and wait until the situation becomes clearer."

Sometimes accepting a limitation is itself an act of trust.


10. A PARTICULAR APPLICATION TO PILGRIMAGE
-------------------------------------------

A pilgrimage undertaken in radical trust does not create an obligation
to continue indefinitely.

One can genuinely say:

    "The pilgrimage I was given has brought me here."

Reaching a destination can be a genuine completion.

Likewise, going home does not retroactively invalidate the pilgrimage.

Nor does continuing automatically prove that the original inspiration
was genuine.

The question is not:

    "How can I make the pilgrimage as radical as possible?"

but:

    "What is the faithful response to God in the circumstances
     actually given to me now?"


11. A USEFUL IGNATIAN QUESTION
------------------------------

Instead of asking:

    "Did God give me a sign telling me to continue?"

one can ask:

    "When I imagine continuing, what movement takes place in me?"

and:

    "When I imagine stopping, resting and returning home, what
     movement takes place in me?"

Then observe over time:

    - peace or agitation;
    - freedom or compulsion;
    - humility or self-importance;
    - charity or self-absorption;
    - patience or urgency;
    - willingness to accept either outcome.

Especially important:

    "Can I remain at peace if God asks me NOT to do the thing
     I currently feel inspired to do?"


12. THE SPIRITUAL SIGNIFICANCE OF "I MUST"
-------------------------------------------

An interior statement such as:

    "I MUST continue or I am failing to trust God"

deserves examination.

There is a difference between:

    "I freely desire to continue and entrust the decision to God"

and:

    "I am compelled to continue because otherwise I fear I am
     disobeying God."

The second contains an element of compulsion.

That does not automatically prove that the inspiration is false,
but it is a reason to slow down and discern rather than immediately
act.


13. A GOOD PRAYER FOR UNCERTAINTY
---------------------------------

Rather than asking for another sign, one can pray:

    "Lord, I am willing to go and willing to stay.
     I am willing to continue and willing to stop.
     Remove from me the need to force an answer.
     Give me freedom to accept whatever You give.
     Help me to recognise Your will without trying to compel
     You to reveal it to me."

This is compatible with trust in Providence and with Ignatian
discernment.

The aim is not to obtain certainty.

The aim is to become free enough to respond faithfully.


14. THE CENTRAL PRINCIPLE
-------------------------

Perhaps the simplest summary is:

    Christian trust is not:
        "God will tell me what to do."

    Christian trust is:
        "I do not know everything God is doing, but I can entrust
         myself to Him while prayerfully and responsibly discerning
         what is in front of me."

Or, more briefly:

    TRUST GOD
    WITHOUT TRYING TO FORCE GOD TO GIVE YOU CERTAINTY.

A useful sentence to remember:

     I can simply set it aside and return to discernment."

One small qualification to the summary: the Catechism's wording is specifically about "interpretation of ... lots" in the context of divination, so I would not turn it into the claim that every use of randomness or every historical Christian use of lots is intrinsically forbidden. The stronger and safer conclusion for your particular situation is that a lot should not be treated as a mechanism for obtaining God's hidden answer to a major personal discernment. That distinction matters.
