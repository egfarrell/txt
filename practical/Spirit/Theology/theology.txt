### Fowler's stages of Faith
    - Stage 6: Universalizing Faith
        - Rare; exemplars rather than a common stage
        - This stage is marked by _radical commitment_ to love
          and justice beyond tribe, religion, or self-interest.
          The self is decentered. Lives become transparent to
          compassion.
        - Fowler cites figures like Martin Luther King Jr.,
          Gandhi, and Mother Teresa as approximations—not because
          they were perfect, but because their lives embodied
          universal concern.
        - Strength: moral courage, self-giving love.
        - Limit: often _costly_, _misunderstood_, and _socially
          disruptive_.

### Moltmann on Bonhoeffer
    - The crucial insight is that Jesus calls us to this life,
      not to a new religion.
    - It is only in the midst of the world that Christ is Christ.
    - This world is not especially tied to the church. It is in
      this ‘this-worldliness’ of life and faith that Bonhoeffer
      wishes to live and to find Christ.
    - Bonhoeffer rejects the ‘inner migration’ of the bourgeois
      classes (Moltmann phrase), for a new, disciplined
      this-worldliness, with a complete awareness of and
      commitment to death as a real possibility: and in this,
      Moltmann explained, we participate with the love of God for
      this world. Thus it is only in the midst of this world that
      we can find God, and not in the separate religious places.
    - Moltmann’s comment on the importance of this Hebrew Bible
      perspective: ‘only when we know the utter unspeakability of
      God can we find and know God in this world.’

### Tritheism:
    * Definition: The belief in *three separate gods* rather than
      one God in three persons.

    * Contrast with orthodox Trinity:
        - Christianity teaches one God in three persons (Father,
          Son, Spirit) sharing *one essence* — not three gods.

    * Error: Tritheism wrongly splits the unity of God, treating
      Father, Son, and Spirit as *independent deities*.

    * Historical note: Rejected as heresy by the early Church
      (e.g., Councils of Nicaea and Constantinople).

    * Why it matters: Accusations of tritheism often arise when
      theologians overemphasize the distinctness of the three
      persons without maintaining their oneness.

    - cf: Criticism of Moltmann's 'Crucified God'
        - Some conservative voices argue Moltmann's
          social-Trinitarian language (e.g., comparing the
          Trinity to a family) leads toward a kind of tritheism
          or splits the unity of divine essence.
        - As seen on theology forums, “Moltmann doesn't believe
          in impassibility… His Trinitarian theology is
          dangerously close to tritheism.”

### Jansenism
    - A theological and moral movement within Catholicism that
      emerged in the 17th century. It was inspired by the
      teachings of Cornelius Jansen, a Dutch theologian whose
      posthumously published work *Augustinus* (1640) emphasized
      predestination, human depravity, and the necessity of
      divine grace for salvation.
    - Jansenism was particularly influential in France, where it
      sparked considerable controversy and opposition from both
      the Catholic Church and secular authorities.

    - Core Beliefs of Jansenism
        1. Original Sin and Human Depravity:
            - Jansenists believed in the profound corruption of
              human nature due to original sin, leaving humans
              incapable of achieving salvation without divine
              grace.

        2. Predestination:
            - They emphasized the necessity of divine grace,
              asserting that it was granted only to an elect
              group predestined by God for salvation.

        3. Moral Rigorism:
            - Jansenists were known for their strict moral
              standards and emphasis on ascetic practices,
              rejecting what they perceived as the laxity of
              mainstream Catholicism.

        4. Opposition to Free Will:
            - They rejected the idea of free will as promoted by
              Jesuit theologians, arguing that human freedom was
              subordinate to God’s sovereign will.

    - ### Controversies and Condemnations
            - Jansenism became a focal point of conflict within
              the Catholic Church:
            - Opposition by the Jesuits:
                - Jesuits, emphasizing free will and a more
                  lenient moral theology, became staunch
                  opponents of Jansenism.
            - Condemnation by the Church:
                - The Church condemned Jansenist doctrines as
                  heretical. Pope Innocent X formally denounced
                  Jansenism in 1653 through the bull *Cum
                  Occasione*.

    - ### Political and Cultural Impact
        1. Port-Royal Abbey:
            - A center of Jansenist thought and practice in
              France, the Port-Royal Abbey became a symbol of
              resistance to ecclesiastical and royal authority.
        2. Conflict with Absolutism:
            - Jansenists often clashed with the centralizing
              policies of the French monarchy, particularly Louis
              XIV, who suppressed the movement to enforce
              religious and political unity.

    - ### Decline and Legacy
        - Though its influence waned in the 18th century due to
          suppression, Jansenism left a lasting impact on
          Catholic theology, French literature, and political
          thought.
        - Its rigorous moral ideals and focus on personal
          conscience influenced later movements within and
          outside the Catholic Church.

        - Jansenism is often remembered as a significant episode
          in the broader struggles over authority, grace, and
          freedom within early modern Catholicism.

# The "top" 10 Christian theologians
    - 1. Karl Barth
    - 2. Dietrich Bonhoeffer
    - 3. Hans Urs von Balthasar
    - 4. Jürgen Moltmann
    - 5. Henri de Lubac
    - 6. Reinhold Niebuhr
    - 7. John Paul II
    - 8. Stanley Hauerwas
    - 9. James H. Cone
    - 10. Sarah Coakley


    - Here are ten figures who have been widely recognized for
      their influence, scholarly work, and impact on both
      academic theology and broader Christian thought.

    ### 1. Karl Barth (1886–1968)
    - Denomination: Reformed (Swiss Protestant)
    - Contribution: A monumental figure of 20th-century theology,
      Barth is best known for his work Church Dogmatics,
      a multi-volume systematic theology. He played a pivotal
      role in opposing liberal theology and sought to re-center
      theology around the revelation of God in Christ.
    - Legacy: His theology deeply influenced Protestantism,
      particularly the Neo-Orthodox movement, and remains
      foundational in theological studies.

    ### 2. Dietrich Bonhoeffer (1906–1945)
    - Denomination: Lutheran (German Protestant)
    - Contribution: Known for his resistance to Nazism and his
      theological reflections on Christian ethics, community, and
      discipleship, particularly through works like The Cost of
      Discipleship and Letters and Papers from Prison.
    - Legacy: His ideas on costly grace, Christian resistance to
      evil, and life in community are highly influential in both
      theological and social ethics discussions.

    ### 3. Hans Urs von Balthasar (1905–1988)
    - Denomination: Roman Catholic
    - Contribution: A Swiss theologian, Balthasar is known for
      his expansive theological work, including his Glory of the
      Lord series, which emphasizes the aesthetic nature of God's
      revelation and his Christocentric theology.
    - Legacy: Considered one of the most important Roman Catholic
      theologians of the 20th century, his work bridges theology
      with philosophy, culture, and literature.

    ### 4. Jürgen Moltmann (b. 1926)
    - Denomination: Reformed (German Protestant)
    - Contribution: Moltmann is known for his theology of hope,
      articulated in his work Theology of Hope. He developed
      a "theology of the cross" and a theology of eschatology
      that focuses on the future promise of God.
    - Legacy: His works on the interplay between suffering and
      hope, and on the Trinity, remain influential in Protestant
      theology, especially in liberation and political
      theologies.

    ### 5. Henri de Lubac (1896–1991)
    - Denomination: Roman Catholic
    - Contribution: A major figure in the Ressourcement movement,
      which sought to renew Catholic theology by returning to the
      sources (the Church Fathers). His work was influential
      during Vatican II, especially in shaping the council's
      theology of the Church and its relationship to the world.
    - Legacy: De Lubac's thought continues to shape contemporary
      Catholic theology, especially in areas like the nature of
      the Church, the Eucharist, and grace.

    ### 6. Reinhold Niebuhr (1892–1971)
    - Denomination: Protestant (American)
    - Contribution: A theologian and ethicist known for his
      development of Christian realism, Niebuhr's works such as
      Moral Man and Immoral Society and The Nature and Destiny of
      Man have profoundly impacted social ethics, particularly in
      political and international relations.
    - Legacy: His insights on human nature, sin, and ethics
      continue to influence Christian social thought, especially
      in public theology and ethics.

    ### 7. John Paul II (Karol Wojtyła) (1920–2005)
    - Denomination: Roman Catholic
    - Contribution: As pope from 1978 to 2005, John Paul II was
      both a pastoral leader and a significant theological
      thinker. His work on Christian anthropology, particularly
      in Theology of the Body, and his social encyclicals like
      Centesimus Annus have had lasting theological and ethical
      impacts.
    - Legacy: His papacy shaped modern Catholicism, addressing
      topics from human dignity and sexuality to social justice,
      interfaith dialogue, and the role of the Church in the
      modern world.

    ### 8. Stanley Hauerwas (b. 1940)
    - Denomination: Methodist/Ecumenical
    - Contribution: Hauerwas is known for his focus on Christian
      ethics, particularly the importance of the Christian
      community as a contrast society and his critique of modern
      liberal democracy. His notable works include The Peaceable
      Kingdom and Resident Aliens.
    - Legacy: Often called one of the most influential living
      theologians, Hauerwas's work has challenged Christians to
      reconsider the Church's role in a secular society and
      remains central in discussions of pacifism and virtue
      ethics.

    ### 9. James H. Cone (1938–2018)
    - Denomination: Protestant (American, Black Liberation
      Theology)
    - Contribution: Cone is the founder of Black Liberation
      Theology, which emphasizes the gospel's relevance to the
      oppressed, especially African Americans. His works, such as
      A Black Theology of Liberation and The Cross and the
      Lynching Tree, have had a profound impact on theological
      discussions of race, justice, and suffering.
    - Legacy: His theology continues to shape conversations about
      race, social justice, and the role of the Church in
      addressing systemic injustice.

    ### 10. Sarah Coakley (b. 1951)
    - Denomination: Anglican
    - Contribution: Coakley is a contemporary theologian known
      for her work in systematic theology, feminist theology, and
      the intersection of theology and spirituality. Her work
      God, Sexuality, and the Self represents a profound
      integration of doctrine, mysticism, and gender studies.
    - Legacy: Her interdisciplinary approach to theology has
      influenced contemporary discussions about the role of
      desire, gender, and prayer in Christian life.

    ### Honorable Mentions:
    - Thomas F. Torrance (1913–2007): Known for his work in
      theology and science.
    - Rowan Williams (b. 1950): Former Archbishop of Canterbury,
      a prominent scholar of Christian spirituality and
      patristics.
    - Elizabeth Johnson (b. 1941): A leading Catholic feminist
      theologian.

    These theologians represent diverse traditions and
    perspectives, contributing to key discussions in 20th and
    21st-century Christian theology.

### Three wills
    - In Christian theology, particularly within the context of
      Orthodox thought, the concept of the "three wills" provides
      a nuanced understanding of human decision-making and
      spiritual struggle. Here's a breakdown of how these three
      wills can be understood:

    1. __Lower Will__: ... driven by the needs and desires of the
       flesh. It includes base instincts, appetites, and emotions
       that are common to humans and animals. These needs are
       often immediate and self-serving, focusing on physical
       survival, pleasure, and comfort.

    2. __Higher Will__: ... strives to align itself with the
       divine will, seeking to discern and follow God's purposes.
       It is concerned with spiritual growth, moral integrity,
       and self-transcendence. The higher will aims to rise above
       the base desires of the lower will, aspiring towards
       holiness and union with God.

    3. __Free Will__: This concept is central to Orthodox
       Christian anthropology and theology. In this schema, free
       will represents the capacity of the human soul to _choose_
       between the lower and higher wills.
        - It is the autonomous aspect of the human person that
          allows for moral and spiritual decision-making.
        - Free will is crucial because it enables genuine love,
          obedience, and repentance.
        - It is through the exercise of free will that
          individuals can choose to follow the higher will and
          thus align themselves with God's will, or succumb to
          the lower will and its base desires.
        - In this framework, free will acts as the mediator and
          deciding factor between the conflicting demands of the
          lower and higher wills. It is through the proper
          exercise of free will, guided by grace and spiritual
          discipline, that one can overcome the lower will and
          adhere to the higher will, achieving spiritual maturity
          and closer communion with God.

    - This tripartite understanding underscores the importance of
      _human agency_ and `responsibility` in the spiritual life.
      The struggle between the lower and higher wills,
      facilitated by free will, is a central theme in the ascetic
      practices and teachings of Orthodox Christianity.

### "Throw your bread upon the waters"
    - Ecclesiastes 11:1 states:
    - "Ship your grain across the sea; after many days you may
      receive a return."
    - Other translations, such as the King James Version:
        - "Cast thy bread upon the waters: for thou shalt find it
          after many days."

    - The meaning of this phrase is open to interpretation, as
      many passages in Ecclesiastes are. Some common
      interpretations:

    1. Investment and Reward:
        - On a practical level, this could be advice about trade
          or investment. Sending grain (or bread) across the sea
          means taking a risk by selling or trading it, with the
          hope that in time this action will yield a return or
          profit.

    2. Generosity:
        - On a more figurative or spiritual level, this phrase
          can be understood as an encouragement to be generous
          without expecting an immediate return. By giving or
          helping others (casting bread), you may receive
          unexpected blessings or rewards later in life.

    3. Uncertainty and Faith:
        - Given the context of Ecclesiastes, which frequently
          reflects on the unpredictability and enigma of life,
          this passage may also encourage faith and action in the
          face of uncertainty. Even if one doesn't see the
          immediate benefits of one's actions, it's worth moving
          forward and acting in faith.

### Theories of Atonement
    - https://dwellcc.org/learning/essays/christian-doctrine-substitutionary-atonement
    - (These types of speculation a blind alley?)
    - Moral-Influence Theory
        - Our feelings of alienation and estrangement from God
          present themselves in many different ways. For
          instance, we may not realize that our rebellion and
          disobedience towards God is a source of pain to God; or
          we many not understand that in spite of all that we
          have done, God still loves us. And yet, if we would
          just repent and turn back to God, there would be
          reconciliation. The problem does not have to do with
          God’s ability to forgive. Nothing in his nature
          requires payment for our sins. The difficulty is with
          us.3 Bushnell views sin as a type of sickness from
          which we must be healed. This, then, is why Jesus came:
          to heal us.
        - Unlike more objective theories of the atonement, which
          view Jesus’ death as central to the purpose of his
          coming, advocates of the moral-influence theory believe
          that Jesus came solely as an expression of God’s divine
          love. His death was merely one aspect of his love for
          us. Consequently, Jesus’ death was an opportunity or
          a circumstance to demonstrate his love. As Bushnell
          explains:
            > “[Jesus’] sacrifice, taken as a fact in time, was
            > not set before him as the end, or object of his
            > ministry –that would make it a mere pageant of
            > suffering, without rational dignity, or character
            > –but, when it came, it was simply the bad fortune
            > such a work, prosecuted with such devotion, must
            > encounter on its way.”
        - Far from squaring up the account of our sin or
          satisfying God’s divine justice, Jesus’ death was not
          the central purpose of his coming; it was a consequence
          of his coming.
        - Thus, Jesus’ death should be seen as accomplishing
          three things:
            1. Jesus’ death was aimed at removing our fear of
            God.
            2. Jesus’ death causes us to feel remorse that
            ultimately leads to repentance.
            3. Jesus’ death gives us inspiration.

    - Ransom Theory
        - It may be fair to say that the ransom theory was the
          standard theory of the atonement in the early church.
          Giants of the early church such as Augustine, Origen
          and Gregory of Nyssa held this view of the atonement,
          and it dominated the church’s understanding of the
          atonement until the time of Anselm (1033-1109).
        - The primary text that Origen relied upon in his
          understanding was Jesus’ statement in Mark 10:45: “The
          Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve, and
          to give his life as a ransom for many” (emphasis mine).
          One may very well ask, “To whom was this ransom being
          paid?” Certainly, God did not pay a ransom to himself.
          So, then, to whom was Jesus given as a ransom? Origen
          thought that it was to the evil one, Satan, who held us
          captive.
        - The ransom theory endured for nearly six centuries as
          the primary way people understood the atonement. But
          with the rise of Anselm and Peter Abelard’s theories of
          the atonement, the ransom theory fell out of
          prominence. In recent times, however, Gustaf Aulen has
          resuscitated it. He has termed this view the “classic
          view” and has maintained that the atonement primarily
          centered upon God’s triumph.13
        - To sum up this view, then, the ransom theory suggests
          that the ultimate purpose of Jesus’ death was to
          liberate the human race from the evil one’s bondage.
          Unlike the moral influence theory, which views the
          atonement as aimed primarily at humans, the ransom
          theory views the atonement as directed towards Satan


    - Substitution Theory
        - A final theory of the atonement is commonly referred to
          as the substitution or satisfaction theory. This theory
          suggests that the primary purpose of Jesus’ death was
          to satisfy God’s justice. Thus, the primary force of
          the atonement was not directed towards restoring
          humanity or prevailing over the evil one; instead it
          served as a payment to God for wrongdoing committed
          against him.
        - The earliest and most prominent advocate of this theory
          was Anselm (1033-1109), archbishop of Canterbury.
          Anselm’s lived during a time when violations of law
          were no longer thought of in a judicial sense, as they
          were during the Roman Empire. Instead, he lived in
          a feudal society where justice and law were viewed as
          more of a personal matter. Breaking the law was thought
          to be an offense against the person of the feudal
          overlord. Immersed in this way of thinking, Anselm
          pictured God as a feudal overlord who, in order to
          preserve his honor, insisted that adequate reparations
          be made to compensate for his loss.14
        - Anselm rejected Gregory’s formulation of the ransom
          theory and even Augustine’s modification of it. His
          contention with the theory had to do with its central
          claim that Satan had a right to possess the human race.
          According to Anselm, no one has the right to possess
          the human race besides God. He believed that we are, by
          right, God’s possession. Even Satan himself belongs to
          God. Therefore, God did not have to purchase humanity
          from Satan.15
        - By way of contrast, to both the moral influence theory
          and the ransom theory, the substitutionary theory of
          the atonement is focused Godward. Unlike the former two
          theories, substitutionary atonement is directed neither
          toward humanity nor toward the evil one. Rather,
          substitutionary atonement sees Jesus’ Jesus’ atonement
          as primarily aimed at satisfying God’s justice.
