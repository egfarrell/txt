Here is a two-part answer addressing your query, drawing from
Bonhoeffer's work and that of other significant Christian
thinkers.

# 1. Bonhoeffer on Discerning the Will of Christ

- In The Cost of Discipleship and his later work, Ethics,
  Bonhoeffer does not provide a simple checklist for discerning
  God's will. He was wary of any system that made knowing God's
  will an easy or purely intellectual exercise.[1] Instead, he
  points to a posture and a process.

### Key Indications from Bonhoeffer:
- It Is Not a Set of Rules: Bonhoeffer was clear that God's will
  is not a pre-determined system of rules that can be applied to
  every situation. He wrote that the "will of God is not a system
  of rules which is established from the outset; it is something
  new and different in each different situation in life."[1]
- A Moment-by-Moment Testing: Discerning God's will is a dynamic,
  daily process. It involves a constant dialogue with God where
  one's heart, mind, observation, and experience collaborate in
  the task of testing what God requires in a specific
  moment.[2][3]
- Obedience Precedes Understanding: This is a core Bonhoefferian
  concept. He states, "It is no use asking questions; for it is
  only through obedience that you come to learn the truth”[4].
  For Bonhoeffer, you don't figure everything out and then act;
  you begin to understand by taking the first step of obedience
  to the clear commands of Christ. The act of following is itself
  a method of discernment.[5]
- The Test of Self-Sacrifice: A crucial difference between
  authentic discernment and self-deception is the element of
  self-sacrifice.[2] The will of God will generally lead to
  a path of self-denial and costly grace, not self-serving
  ambition.[2][6] This was exemplified by Bonhoeffer's own
  decision to participate in the plot to assassinate Hitler, an
  act he saw as morally complex but a necessary, self-sacrificial
  response to extreme evil.[2][3]
- Grounded in Community and Scripture: Discernment is not
  a purely individualistic or mystical experience. Bonhoeffer
  emphasized the importance of the Christian community studying
  scripture, praying, and reflecting together.[4][7] This
  communal process serves as a safeguard against distorted or
  purely subjective interpretations of God's will.[4]

### On Abandoning the World: The "Fool for Christ"
- Your question about being called to abandon the "normal" world
  is very insightful. Bonhoeffer's answer is a nuanced "yes."
- The call of Christ is absolute and requires a radical break.
  When Jesus calls a disciple, they must leave their nets and
  burn their boats, forsaking their old life to exist for Christ
  alone.[8] Bonhoeffer saw this as a move from relative security
  to "absolute insecurity" in the world, which is simultaneously
  the "absolute security" of fellowship with Jesus.[8] In his
  famous line, "When Christ calls a man, he bids him come and
  die," he meant a death to self-will, ambition, and worldly
  security.[6][9]
- However, for Bonhoeffer, this did not mean a withdrawal from
  the world into a monastery. Rather, he argued that Christians
  should not retreat from the world but act within it.[5] The
  disciple's life is to be lived "from the cross, for the
  world."[10] The abandonment is of self-will and "bourgeois
  respectability," not of responsibility for the world.[8] It is
  a radical engagement with the world on Christ's terms, not
  a flight from it.[11]

# - 2. Discerning God's Will: Perspectives from Other Christian
Writers

- Other major 20th and 21st-century Christian thinkers have also
  provided guidance on this crucial topic, often complementing
  Bonhoeffer's themes with different practical frameworks.

### C.S. Lewis (1898-1963)
- Lewis, while not writing a manual on discernment, provided key
  principles through his works. His approach is grounded in
  reason, Scripture, and the character of God.
- Alignment with Scripture: The first test is always God's
  revealed will in the Bible. God's voice will never ask a person
  to act against what He has already clearly spoken in His
  Word.[12]
- The Presence of Peace: God's voice leads toward a deep,
  internal peace, even if the path is difficult or
  frightening.[12] This is contrasted with the turmoil, shame,
  and fear that come from one's own anxieties or
  insecurities.[12]
- Consistency with God's Character: What you feel called to do
  must be consistent with the unchanging, loving, and truthful
  character of God as revealed in Jesus Christ.[12]
- Trust in God's Larger Plan: Lewis emphasized trusting God's
  ultimate purpose, even when immediate circumstances are
  confusing or painful.[13] Disappointments can be part of
  a larger, unseen plan for good.[13]

### Dallas Willard (1935-2013)
- Willard's book Hearing God is a modern classic on this subject.
  He shifts the focus from "finding God's will" for big decisions
  to living in a constant conversational relationship with God.
- A Conversational Relationship: The goal is not just to get
  guidance but to become the kind of person who is in such close
  relationship with God that hearing Him becomes natural.[14][15]
  He believed spiritual people are those who "draw their life
  from a conversational relationship with God."[16]
- Focus on Being, Not Just Doing: Willard taught that God is
  often more concerned with the person we are becoming than the
  specific decision we make.[14] Leading the kind of life God
  wants for us leaves significant room for our own initiative and
  responsibility.[14][17]
- Practical Steps for Listening: He advocated for practical
  disciplines like meditating on Scripture, praying constantly
  and specifically about all matters, listening deliberately in
  silence, and seeking counsel from respected believers.[16]

### Timothy Keller (1950-2023)
- As a pastor, Keller offered practical and wise counsel,
  demystifying the process and cautioning against becoming
  paralyzed by the search for a perfect "sign."
- Wisdom Over Mysticism: Keller taught that God guides primarily
  by giving us wisdom. He often told people asking for guidance
  to simply "make a decision."[18][19][20] He argued against
  waiting for an inner feeling of "peace," noting that Jesus
  himself did not have peace about going to the cross.[20]
- God's Guidance is a Current: He described God's guidance as
  something you are already standing in, like a current. "It's
  more something God does than something God gives."[18]
- The Three-Fold Framework: For vocational and major life
  decisions, Keller suggested a practical grid:
- Affinity: What are your passions? What needs in the world
  resonate with you?[21]
- Ability: What are you good at? Where have others affirmed your
  effectiveness?[21]
- Opportunity: What doors are open? What needs are present in
  your community or church that you can meet?[21] When these
  three align, it is a strong indicator of God's calling.
