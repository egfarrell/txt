What you describe sounds less like a need to produce a catalogue
of “bad things I have done” and more like an opportunity to let
the light of Christ reach the deeper dispositions of the heart.
After 25 years of serious Christian life, it is quite natural
that what becomes visible to you changes: the obvious things may
diminish while subtler movements — self-protection, vanity,
judgement, comfort, control, resentment, subtle uncharitableness
— become easier to see.

One thing I would particularly suggest for these days at Lourdes:
don't make the examination primarily an exercise in
self-analysis. Let it become prayer. You are not trying to catch
yourself out. You are allowing yourself to be seen by Christ.

You might take one question each hour, or simply sit with the
ones that provoke some recognition.

Ten questions for prayer

1. “Where do I choose myself rather than love?”

Not merely where do I break a commandment, but:

Where do I instinctively put my own preferences first?

Where do I want things arranged around my comfort?

Where do I resist inconvenience, interruption or sacrifice?

Where does “I deserve a little peace” subtly become an excuse not
to give myself?


And perhaps the deeper question:

“Lord, where am I still asking You to fit into my life rather
than allowing my life to belong entirely to You?”


---

2. “What happens inside me when another person inconveniences
   me?”

This can be very revealing.

Think of colleagues, strangers, family, difficult personalities.

What is the first interior movement?

Irritation? Contempt? Superiority? Suspicion? Impatience?
A desire to dismiss them?

You don't necessarily sin simply by experiencing an involuntary
first movement. The interesting question is what happens after it
arrives.

Do I entertain it?

Do I enjoy it?

Do I construct a case against the person?

Do I quietly place myself above them?

Or do I hand the person back to Christ?


---

3. “How quickly do I turn another person into an object of
   judgement?”

This seems particularly relevant to what you described.

When you meet someone new, ask:

“What story do I immediately construct about this person?”

You might notice:

analysing their personality;

deciding what sort of person they are;

noticing their faults;

categorising them;

wondering whether you like or dislike them;

mentally comparing yourself with them;

detecting their insecurities or weaknesses;

feeling subtly superior because you can “see through” them.


Then ask:

“Could I simply allow this person to exist before me without
needing to evaluate them?”

That can be a surprisingly profound form of charity.


---

4. “What do I enjoy that I would be embarrassed for Christ to see
   me enjoying?”

Not necessarily anything objectively sinful.

Rather, consider the little pleasures of the ego:

being right;

being admired;

being interesting;

having superior knowledge;

being recognised as spiritual;

having people appreciate your sacrifices;

possessing an interesting interior life;

being thought generous;

being the person who understands things deeply.


There can be an almost invisible pleasure in being the sort of
person we want to believe ourselves to be.

Ask:

“Where am I secretly attached to an image of myself?”


---

5. “What happens when I am not noticed?”

This is an excellent examination of hidden pride.

If I do something good and nobody knows, am I genuinely content?

If someone else receives credit for something I did, what happens
inside?

If another person is praised for qualities I possess, do
I rejoice?

If someone I consider less capable than myself succeeds, can
I simply be happy for them?

If my good intentions are misunderstood, can I leave that
misunderstanding with God?

These questions can expose very subtle forms of vainglory.


---

6. “Whom do I find difficult to love — and what do I tell myself
   about them?”

Don't only name the people who have actually harmed you.

Think about the people you simply don't like.

Then ask:

“What justification have I built for not loving this person?”

Sometimes the justification is legitimate. Sometimes it is partly
true. But underneath it there may be:

> “I don't have to love them because I have correctly diagnosed
> them.”



That is a very subtle temptation.

Christian charity doesn't require liking everybody. But it does
require willing the good of the other — and refusing to take
pleasure in their defects.


---

7. “Where do I avoid surrender by remaining in my head?”

This is another possibility in someone who has spent years in
serious spiritual formation.

Analysis can become a refuge.

I can analyse myself rather than repent.

I can analyse other people rather than love them.

I can analyse my spiritual condition rather than simply pray.

I can seek ever more precise understanding of myself while
avoiding the simple act of saying:

“Jesus, I surrender this to You.”

So ask:

“Where has my desire to understand become a subtle way of
avoiding trust?”


---

8. “Where have I resisted love because love cost me something?”

Look particularly at the small sacrifices.

Someone wanted my attention when I wanted solitude.

Someone was tedious when I wanted peace.

Someone needed something when I wanted to rest.

Someone was emotionally demanding.

Someone interrupted my plans.

Someone failed to appreciate what I had done.

Someone needed forgiveness before I felt ready to give it.

Then ask:

“What did I choose in that moment: love, or myself?”

Not to accuse yourself endlessly, but to see the pattern.


---

9. “What do I do with the movements of grace?”

This one is easy to overlook because an examination of conscience
usually concentrates on failure.

But ask:

When Christ prompts me to pray, do I respond?

When I feel prompted to apologise, do I?

When someone comes to mind whom I should contact, do I?

When I perceive an opportunity to serve, do I take it?

When I feel moved towards silence, prayer or generosity, do
I follow?

How often do I negotiate with grace because something else is
more comfortable?


Sometimes the deeper issue isn't simply “What sins have
I committed?”

It is:

“How often have I quietly said no to love?”


---

10. “If Christ were to remove everything except love, what in me
would resist?”

Perhaps sit with this one for a long time.

Imagine Christ saying:

> “I want to take away your need to be comfortable, admired,
> understood, right, superior, secure and in control. I want only
> love.”



Then notice what objects.

What would I find difficult to surrender?

That resistance may tell you more about the deepest attachments
of your heart than a long list of individual faults.


---

One distinction that may be very helpful

Try to distinguish between:

temptation → movement → consent → deliberate action → habit

For example:

> “I meet someone and immediately think, ‘What an irritating
> person.’”



That initial movement may be spontaneous. You haven't necessarily
chosen it.

But then perhaps:

> “I entertain the thought, analyse them, mentally mock them,
> tell myself why they are inferior, and enjoy the judgement.”



Now there is something worth bringing to confession.

Likewise, an unwanted thought, feeling, attraction, irritation or
impulse isn't automatically a sin. The moral significance lies
substantially in what we freely do with it.

This matters because otherwise an examination of conscience can
become almost pathological: “I noticed another imperfect thought,
therefore I have sinned again.” That isn't necessarily the
Christian understanding of conscience.

The purpose of examining these things is not to become fascinated
by your own defects.

It is to become more free to love.

A particularly fruitful examination: “What happens in the first
five seconds?”

You might spend part of your retreat observing yourself without
immediately correcting anything.

Someone walks into the room.

Someone speaks.

Someone disagrees with you.

Someone boasts.

Someone is awkward.

Someone is attractive.

Someone receives praise.

Someone criticises you.

Someone interrupts your prayer.

Someone gets something you wanted.

Then simply notice:

What rises in me?

Don't condemn it. Don't explain it.

Just notice.

Then:

What do I do with what rises?

That second question is often where the examination becomes
spiritually fruitful.


---

And one caution about “subtle sins”

Given your background — Lourdes, the Carthusians, Carmelites,
retreats, 25 years of faith — I would be particularly careful not
to turn this into an increasingly sophisticated project of
self-purification by introspection.

There is a strange trap whereby someone becomes increasingly
aware of subtle imperfections and simultaneously becomes
increasingly preoccupied with themselves.

The result can look like humility while actually becoming another
form of self-occupation.

You don't have to discover every hidden defect before going to
confession.

You don't have to understand yourself completely.

You don't have to produce an exhaustive spiritual diagnosis.

You can simply say to the priest something like:

> “There aren't many grave outward sins that I need to confess,
> but I have become increasingly aware of patterns of interior
> judgement, subtle pride, attachment to comfort, gossip,
> self-protection and failures in charity. I would like to bring
> these honestly before God and ask for help in becoming more
> loving.”



That gives the priest plenty to work with.

And if, during confession, something else comes to mind, say it
simply. Don't feel obliged to provide a theological dissertation
on every sin.

Perhaps let Lourdes itself frame the whole thing

There is something beautiful about the timing of what you are
doing.

You have walked for three months towards Lourdes, and now you are
stopping.

Perhaps these few days need not be about “finding everything
wrong with myself.”

They could be about standing before Christ and allowing Him to
say:

“Let Me show you where you are not yet free to love.”

And then, after He shows you something, resist the temptation to
stare at the defect.

Turn towards Him.

“Jesus, I see that in me. Have mercy.”

“Jesus, I don't know how to change this. Teach me.”

“Jesus, I give You this person whom I judge.”

“Jesus, I give You my comfort.”

“Jesus, I give You my pride.”

“Jesus, I give You my need to be right.”

“Jesus, make my heart like Yours.”

That movement — from seeing → to repentance → to surrender → to
love — is probably more fruitful than trying to excavate every
last imperfection.

And when you go into the confessional, you need not go in as
someone presenting Christ with the results of a successful
investigation. You go in as someone who has allowed himself to be
seen and is asking for mercy.

The subtle sins matter. But the mercy of Christ is vastly more
important than your ability to identify them.
