Yes. What you are asking gets very close to the heart of the
Eucharistic mystery, and I think the distinction between symbolic
and real is actually too crude to capture what the early
Christians meant.

Given your familiarity with Carmelite and Carthusian
spirituality, I would put it this way:

> The Eucharist is not “Christ symbolically represented by
> bread”. It is bread which, through Christ's word and the action
> of the Holy Spirit, becomes a sacramental mode of Christ's real
> presence.



The word sacramental is doing a great deal of work here.

1. What does "Christ is present" actually mean?

Catholic theology says something quite precise: Christ is present
"truly, really and substantially" in the Eucharist — Body, Blood,
soul and divinity. But this does not mean that Christ is present
in the same way that your body is present in a room.

Your senses continue to perceive bread and wine. You don't see
Jesus sitting inside the Host, and chemically the Host remains
indistinguishable from bread.

Rather, the Church makes a distinction between appearance and
being.

After consecration:

what appears to us remains bread and wine;

what the bread and wine are at the deepest ontological level has
been changed;

Christ himself is therefore present under the sacramental signs.


This is what the later Church calls transubstantiation. The term
is medieval, but the underlying claim is considerably older. 

And there is an important qualification: "real presence" doesn't
mean that other ways of encountering Christ are unreal. Christ is
present in Scripture, in the gathered Church, in the poor,
through the Holy Spirit, etc. Eucharistic presence is called real
in a particular sense because it is a substantial sacramental
presence. 

So when you kneel before the tabernacle, Catholic theology is
saying something extraordinary:

you are not merely praying in front of a reminder of Jesus. You
are praying before Christ himself, sacramentally present.

That is why Eucharistic adoration makes sense within this
theology.


---

2. But what does "Body" mean?

This is where modern English can mislead us.

When we hear "body", we instinctively think:

> physical biological material.



But "body" in the biblical and ancient Christian context is
richer than that.

Paul can speak of the Church as the Body of Christ, for example.
"Body" denotes not merely a collection of physical molecules but
the person in his embodied, relational, living reality.

And Christianity says that the Eucharist gives us Christ himself
— not a detached piece of Jesus' physical tissue.

This is why the traditional Catholic formulation says that Christ
is present whole and entire: Body, Blood, soul and divinity. 

In other words, receiving Christ's Body means receiving the
living Christ.

And that immediately makes sense of something mystical Christians
have always experienced:

> Communion is communion with a Person.



The purpose isn't simply that Jesus has somehow entered your
digestive system. The Eucharistic eating is ordered towards
union:

> "He who eats my flesh and drinks my blood abides in me, and
> I in him."
— John 6:56



The Catechism explicitly interprets the Eucharist in these terms:
communion with Jesus, and through Jesus communion with his Body,
the Church. 


---

3. And yes — Passover is absolutely central

Your instinct about the Passover lamb is very important.

The Last Supper wasn't simply:

> Jesus has dinner with the disciples and invents a symbolic
> ceremony.



It occurs in the context of Passover.

And Passover itself wasn't simply a meal about something that
happened in the past.

Israel's liturgical remembrance had a very different conception
of "remembrance" from our ordinary use of the word.

When Jews celebrated Passover, they weren't merely saying:

> "Let's think about something our ancestors experienced 1,500
> years ago."



They ritually entered into the saving action of God.

This is part of what lies behind the biblical idea of memorial
(anamnesis).

So when Jesus says:

> "Do this in remembrance of me"



the Christian understanding eventually becomes:

make present sacramentally the saving event I am accomplishing.

The Eucharist is therefore not merely a reminder of Calvary.

It is the sacramental making-present of Christ's once-for-all
sacrifice.

The Catholic liturgical theology is explicit about this: the
Passion, death and Resurrection are not repeated, but the one
Paschal Mystery is made sacramentally present. 

That is why the Mass can simultaneously be:

the Last Supper,

Calvary,

the Resurrection,

the heavenly liturgy,


without Christianity believing that Jesus is being crucified
repeatedly.

The one event of Christ's self-offering is made present
sacramentally.


---

4. And now the really interesting part: did the earliest
Christians actually believe this?

Yes. Very strongly.

This is probably the most historically decisive part of your
question.

We don't have to wait until the Middle Ages to find Christians
talking about the Eucharist as Christ's actual Body and Blood.

One of our earliest post-New-Testament witnesses is Ignatius of
Antioch, who died probably around AD 107.

Writing around the beginning of the second century, Ignatius
criticises Christians whom he says:

> "abstain from the Eucharist ... because they confess not the
> Eucharist to be the flesh of our Saviour Jesus Christ"



That's remarkably unambiguous. 

Ignatius isn't writing about a medieval theory of
transubstantiation. The word doesn't yet exist.

But he clearly believes that the Eucharist is genuinely Christ's
flesh.


---

5. Justin Martyr is even more explicit

Around AD 150, Justin Martyr describes Christian worship to
a pagan audience.

And he says Christians don't receive the Eucharistic food as
ordinary bread and drink.

His explanation is essentially:

Christ became flesh and blood for our salvation, and the
Eucharistic food, after the prayer of thanksgiving, is understood
as the flesh and blood of that incarnate Jesus. 

That's only roughly a century after Jesus.

And Justin describes an actual Eucharistic liturgy:

1. readings from the apostles and prophets;


2. exhortation;


3. prayers;


4. thanksgiving;


5. bread and wine;


6. Communion;


7. taking the Eucharist to absent Christians.



So this isn't an isolated mystical metaphor appearing centuries
later. It is embedded in the ordinary Christian worship Justin
describes.


---

6. What about the New Testament itself?

There are three particularly important strands.

A. The words of institution

At the Last Supper:

> "This is my body."



> "This is my blood."



Notice what Jesus doesn't say:

> "This represents my body."



That's not necessarily decisive by itself, because "is" and
"symbol" can have complicated relationships in biblical language.

But it is certainly striking.


---

B. Paul

Paul is also extraordinarily strong.

Writing probably in the AD 50s, decades before the Gospels were
written, he says of the Eucharistic cup and bread:

> "The cup of blessing that we bless, is it not a participation
> in the blood of Christ?"



and:

> "The bread that we break, is it not a participation in the body
> of Christ?"



Then he makes the astonishing statement that someone who receives
the Eucharist unworthily is guilty concerning the body and blood
of the Lord.

That's difficult to make sense of if Paul thinks the bread is
merely a psychological reminder.


---

C. John 6

Then there's John 6.

Jesus says:

> "Unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his
> blood, you have no life in you."



And John repeatedly uses unusually strong language concerning
eating his flesh.

Christians have debated precisely how John 6 relates to the
Eucharist, and one shouldn't simply flatten the whole chapter
into a Eucharistic instruction manual. But the language certainly
belongs to the same theological world.


---

7. So is it "symbolic" or "literal"?

I'd actually reject the opposition.

The Eucharist is symbolic and real, because "symbol" in the
ancient sacramental sense does not necessarily mean "merely
symbolic".

We tend to use "symbol" to mean:

> "This thing reminds us of something else, but isn't actually
> that thing."



For example:

> A wedding ring symbolises marriage.



The ring isn't the marriage itself.

But a sacramental symbol is doing something deeper. It makes
present and communicates what it signifies.

So, roughly:

ordinary symbol:

sign → points towards reality

sacrament:

sign → makes present and communicates reality

That is why the ancient Church can speak simultaneously about:

bread and wine,

Christ's Body and Blood,

sacrifice,

communion,

remembrance,

the Holy Spirit,

participation in Christ.


These aren't competing explanations. They're different dimensions
of the same mystery.


---

8. The "eating" is therefore essential

This is one of the most beautiful aspects of Christianity.

God doesn't merely say:

> "Think about Christ."



Nor even:

> "Believe that Christ loves you."



He says, in effect:

> Receive me.



The Eucharist is extraordinarily physical Christianity.

Christianity isn't ultimately about escaping the material world
into a purely spiritual realm. The Word became flesh.

And then the incarnate Word gives himself under the forms of food
and drink.

There's a beautiful symmetry:

Incarnation

God → becomes flesh → so that we may have communion with God.

Eucharist

Christ → gives his flesh as food → so that we may have communion
with Christ.

And then:

Communion

We receive Christ → and are incorporated more deeply into Christ
→ becoming his Body.

That last step is particularly important. The Eucharist isn't
simply:

> "Jesus enters me."



It is also:

> "I am incorporated into Christ."




---

9. This is why the Eucharist is connected to the Church as
Christ's Body

There is a profound paradox here.

You receive the Body of Christ in order to become the Body of
Christ.

St Augustine famously developed this theme: when you receive what
you are, you are called to become what you receive.

Paul's theology makes the connection explicit:

> "Because there is one bread, we who are many are one body."



So Eucharistic communion isn't private mystical consumption.

It is incorporation.

The Eucharist makes the Christian increasingly capable of saying:

> "It is no longer I who live, but Christ who lives in me."



And this brings us rather close to the Carmelite experience you
described.


---

10. Where the mystical tradition comes in

I think this is where your experience of silence may actually
help rather than hinder your understanding.

The Eucharist isn't something you are necessarily supposed to
figure out intellectually.

The Church's word is mysterium fidei — "the mystery of faith".

That doesn't mean:

> "It is nonsense."



It means:

> there is a reality here which reason can approach but cannot
> exhaust.



The Carmelite tradition is very comfortable with this.

St John of the Cross would not expect you to produce an
appropriate mental picture of "Jesus inside the Host".

In fact, trying too hard to manufacture an interior image could
take you away from the mystery.

Instead:

Christ is here.

Christ gives himself.

I receive him.

I do not need to manufacture a particular feeling.

I remain in faith before the reality I cannot see.

And that distinction may be especially important given what you
said about occasionally receiving a profound peace in prayer.

The feeling of peace is not the criterion for Christ's presence.

The Eucharist reverses the usual order.

In contemplative prayer, you might experience God's presence as
peace, darkness, sweetness, silence, or sometimes nothing at all.

In the Eucharist, the Church says:

> Christ is present whether or not you experience anything.



Your senses say:

> bread.



Your emotions may say:

> nothing in particular.



Faith says:

> Christ.



That is an extraordinarily austere form of contemplation.

And, in a certain sense, very Carthusian.


---

11. One final historical nuance

There was development in Eucharistic theology.

The early Christians did not have the complete conceptual
vocabulary that developed later.

You won't find Ignatius explaining "transubstantiation" in the
Aristotelian terminology of the thirteenth century. You won't
find Justin discussing "substance" and "accidents".

Those are later philosophical tools used to articulate an older
belief.

The development is roughly:

1st century

Jesus: "This is my body... this is my blood."

Paul: participation in Christ's Body and Blood.

early 2nd century

Ignatius: the Eucharist is the flesh of Christ. 

mid-2nd century

Justin: Eucharistic food is not received as ordinary bread and
drink but as Christ's flesh and blood. 

later Fathers

Increasingly elaborate reflection on how Christ is present.

Middle Ages

The philosophical concept of transubstantiation is developed to
explain the mode of this presence.

Council of Trent

The doctrine is defined with great precision.

So there is unquestionably development of explanation, but the
historical evidence makes it difficult to sustain the idea that
"the early Christians merely regarded Communion as a symbolic
memorial and the medieval Church later turned it into literal
Body and Blood."

The early evidence points in the opposite direction.


---

Perhaps the simplest way to hold it

If you were sitting in a monastery chapel and wanted one sentence
to carry into prayer, I wouldn't make it a complicated
theological definition.

I would make it:

> The Eucharist is Christ giving himself to me, really and
> sacramentally, so that I may be united to him and, in him, to
> God and to his Body, the Church.



And then perhaps the most important word is "giving".

The Eucharist isn't primarily about an unusual substance that
happens to be inside a piece of bread.

It is about self-giving.

The same Christ who says on the Cross, "This is my body, given
for you," says in the Eucharist, in effect:

"Receive the gift."

That is why the Passover connection is so profound. The Lamb is
not merely looked at, remembered, or admired.

The Lamb becomes food.

And Christianity's astonishing claim is that the Passover Lamb is
Christ, and that the risen Christ gives himself as food — not
dead flesh, but the living, risen Christ. The Eucharist is
therefore simultaneously sacrifice, banquet, communion and
presence. 

If you want to go one level deeper, the really fascinating next
question is why the early Church thought the Eucharist was
simultaneously the Passover sacrifice and a participation in the
heavenly liturgy. That takes you into Hebrews, Revelation, John
6, the Jewish Temple, and the writings of the early Fathers — and
it makes the structure of the Mass look rather different from
a "service with Communion tacked onto the end."
