Thank you for sharing that—it’s clear you’re thoughtful,
compassionate, and self-aware, and you're navigating a really
nuanced tension between care for others and care for yourself.
What you’re describing is not just reasonable—it's human. You’re
already acting with deep kindness by continuing to show up for
your brother despite how draining it is. But the fact that you’re
beginning to rebel internally is a sign that your current
approach might not be sustainable—and that’s important to listen
to.

Here's how you might think about moving forward:
1. You Are Allowed to Have Boundaries

It’s okay—and healthy—to have limits, even when the other person
is someone you care about deeply. You’re not failing your brother
by acknowledging that prolonged social interaction is draining.
You're recognizing your own needs, and that’s not selfish—it's
self-respect.

Staying in nearby accommodation and planning shorter visits
sounds like a wise step. It can help you protect your energy
while still remaining connected.

2. Redefine What Kindness Looks Like

Kindness doesn’t always mean availability. Sometimes it's about
honesty and consistency. If you shift from masking to being
gently truthful about your limitations, you’re also modeling
authenticity, which can be a gift to those around you—even if
it's initially misunderstood.

For example, you might say something like:

    "Hey, just so you know, I really enjoy seeing you, but I get
    tired quickly with lots of conversation. I might keep things
    shorter when I visit—it’s not about you, it’s just how
    I manage my energy."

3. Small, Predictable Rituals Can Be Enough

Instead of lengthy outings, could you and your brother share
a shorter but regular routine that suits you better? For
instance:

    A monthly 30-minute walk.

    A shared interest you can talk about more sparingly (like
    a book, documentary, or hobby).

    Texting or sending a photo of something you saw on a walk, if
    that's easier than in-person.

This lets you maintain connection without overextending.

4. Allow Yourself to Grieve the Gap

It can hurt to feel like you should be able to connect more
easily with someone you care about, especially a sibling. It’s
okay to feel sadness or guilt about that gap. But remember: your
brother being lonely isn’t your fault. You’re not his only
connection—and you’re not his therapist or social life.
5. Start Small, Stay Honest

You don’t need to overhaul everything at once. Try staying in
separate accommodation on your next visit and limiting the nature
outings. See how that feels. If he asks, you can be truthful in
small, digestible ways. That gives you space, and him clarity,
without hurting the bond.

It sounds like you’re trying to live a life of kindness without
self-erasure. That’s admirable—and possible. Authenticity and
charity can coexist when you give from a place of wholeness, not
depletion.
