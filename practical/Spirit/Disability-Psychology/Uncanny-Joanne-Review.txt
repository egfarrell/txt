### The Woman Who Thought Too Much: A Memoir by Joanne Limburg
| Book review Hilary Mantel

- A clear-eyed exposé of life with a compulsive disorder
  impresses Hilary Mantel

- In the diary she kept when she was a student at Cambridge, the
  poet Joanne Limburg described herself as "an underwritten
  character". Her memoir has rectified that. Her jacket
  photograph shows the serene face of a bespectacled madonna. The
  self-portrait in her text leaves a different impression; she
  stands before the reader picking at her skin and "hopping, skip
  stepping, chewing on clothes, nose wrinkling, stammering and
  toe scrunching", a sad human assemblage of unlovely compulsions
  and unsightly tics. It is usual to praise the authors of memoir
  for their honesty, though it seems the least they can offer
  a reader. Can a writer be too honest? At times you want to
  close this book to protect its subject from your scrutiny. As
  a teenager, Limburg says, she would confide in friends about
  her bad habits, bad thoughts – as if friends might give her
  some kind of absolution for "my neediness, my moods and my
  dramas". They didn't, of course; they avoided the shrinking
  young girl with her unravelling narrative.

- As chapter headings Limburg chooses extracts from a check list
  called the Padua Inventory, for sufferers of
  obsessive-compulsive disorder. Each item could be an extract
  from a suicide note: "When I hear about a disaster, I think it
  is somehow my fault . . . I sometimes have an impulse to hurt
  defenceless children or animals." For a long time she believed
  depression to be her chief problem, and that would have been
  unsurprising; within a short time she suffered a succession of
  crushing bereavements. Gradually – after experiments, more or
  less successful, with different types of therapy and with drugs
  – she began to see that her habitual anxiety was even more
  disabling, though "anxiety", as she says, is a feeble word for
  the rib-squeezing terror that gripped her on a daily basis. She
  was relieved when she found she fitted the OCD category. Unlike
  many sufferers, she didn't order her life by rituals of
  counting and sorting, and she was unfazed by domestic dirt and
  disorder. But she was afraid of almost everything: of sex, of
  men, of crossing the road. When she conquered the fear of the
  first two, settled into a relationship and became pregnant, she
  miscarried and blamed herself. She went on to have a successful
  pregnancy, but motherhood pumped up her anxieties; if you're
  frightened of moving traffic, how much worse is it when you
  have a toddler in tow? Her own thoughts, their dark and
  obsessive nature, were what frightened her most of all.

- As a child, Limburg was a fearless tree climber. As an
  adolescent, she became afraid of walking downstairs. What
  happened? The typical OCD sufferer, she tells us, is
  "law-abiding, conscientious, exquisitely self-conscious and
  excruciatingly eager to please. We set ourselves the highest
  standards, and we are disgusted with ourselves when we don't
  live up to them. We are for ever scanning our own faces for
  flaws and other people's faces for signs of disapproval. We
  cannot forgive ourselves for ever having acted stupidly; we
  cannot bear to make a mistake. We can be destroyed by the
  merest hint of criticism, but we criticise ourselves all the
  time."

- So: perhaps the trouble was womanhood? Half the women I know
  have the traits she ascribes in this passage to a pathology. Is
  Limburg's malady social, then? One GP tells her "It comes from
  thinking too much," which is a problem for "you young girls".
  It seems that Limburg is only an extreme variant of a familiar
  type, but that extremity brings suffering. "My default position
  is to feel entirely responsible for the feelings of everyone
  I come into contact with, for as long as I come into contact
  with them." Empathy is her problem, as it's also her strength,
  and she writes well enough for us to empathise with her in
  turn. There is no limit to the ingenuity of the catastrophising
  imagination. A cup of water is the deep end of a swimming pool,
  a lighted match an inferno, nail scissors are guillotines;
  woods hide rapists, a cold sore is a cancer, and every country
  lane is the M25 in a foggy rush hour. Vigilance must be
  perpetual and what is most delightful is also most unsafe. She
  goes on honeymoon to Venice, "the perfect venue for generating
  both romantic memories and imaginary near-drowning accidents".

- Books save her. They shelter her and give her a trade. She
  repels the temptation to suicide by reading Al Alvarez's book
  The Savage God. Her sensitivity creates the potential to write,
  her hobbling perfectionism stops her getting words on the page.
  She craves reassurance, yet writing is the least reassuring of
  trades; it demands a high tolerance of ambiguity and
  incompleteness. There is something hasty and unresolved about
  Limburg's book, and no doubt it was a painful triumph to part
  with it. As she says wryly, you can't turn yourself into
  Jonathan Miller in 12 months. She sounds like a woman in
  a hurry to explain, and sometimes like a navigator just
  glimpsing a chart which shows calmer waters. She brings insight
  and a rueful wit to her story, which is interesting not only
  for her fellow walking wounded, but for writers and would-be
  writers. It was a great day in her life when she met
  a therapist who "took me seriously as a poet – it wasn't
  a symptom to her".

- Limburg has published two collections, Feminismo and
  Paraphernalia. At one stage of her writing life she was afraid
  that her desk chair would catch fire and she would burn alive
  in her own office. In the course of reading her book I looked
  around websites to pick up uncollected poems, and came across
  a review of her work on a site hosted by another woman poet
  – a review so priggish, so envious and so expressly aimed to
  wound that I felt ashamed of the critic's trade. Limburg's fear
  – a blazing workplace – took on a metaphorical coherence. This
  talented and thoughtful young woman must be braver than she
  imagines, to step into the fiery circus where the modern writer
  performs her tricks.
