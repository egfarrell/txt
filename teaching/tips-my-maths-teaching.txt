### Understanding the Core of Education and Cognitive Processes
    - Central to learning—and thus education—are two distinct
      cognitive processes defined by Daniel Kahneman: 
        - `System One` (fast, intuitive judgments) and 
        - `System Two` (slow, deliberate reasoning). 
    - Education's purpose is to exercise and develop System Two
      to the point where certain skills become so practiced they
      transition into System One, allowing for effortless
      execution. 
    - Historical attempts at educational revolutions have
      misunderstood the importance of nurturing this transfer.
        - AI, therefore, needs to complement existing educational
          methods rather than replace them.

    - https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0xS68sl2D70
    - In this livestream, @veritasium's Derek Muller explores how
      AI might change how we teach and learn, drawing on insights
      from past tech shifts and core principles of cognitive
      science. While AI presents exciting opportunities, it also
      introduces real risks—especially when it comes to how our
      brains build knowledge and expertise.
    - The speaker explores how AI is influencing education, while

      methodologies.

      potential for AI in education.
    - Historical claims of technological "revolutions" in
      education—from motion pictures to MOOCs—often did not
      materialize as expected.
    - Education effectiveness is more about social interaction,
      critical thinking, and active engagement than simply
      delivering information.
    - The talk references Daniel Kahneman's "Thinking, Fast and
      Slow," discussing system 1 and system 2 thought processes.
    - System 1 is automatic and quick, while system 2 is
      deliberate and effortful; education should focus on knowing
      when to use each.
    - Practical implications for education include minimizing
      extraneous cognitive load and limiting intrinsic cognitive
      load through pacing and scaffolding learning.
    - Mastery and repetition are crucial for transferring skills
      and knowledge into long-term memory.
    - Discovery learning without adequate support can hinder
      effective learning processes.
    - AI can provide valuable feedback but might undermine
      effortful practice if used improperly.
    - The potential impact of AI on reducing the necessity for
      effortful learning in writing and art is a concern.
    - Insights suggest focusing less on revolutionary claims and
      more on supportive classroom experiences.
    - The speaker believes effective education relies on
      a communal, interactive, and motivational environment.
    - Audience Q&A touched on AI's role in learning efficiency
      and policy influence, as well as challenges with AI-enabled
      cheating.

### Authority
    - Establishing authority comes from a belief in yourself:
      belief that you are in charge and if you give a reasonable
      instruction, the class must follow those instructions fast.
      Have clear rules. Three rules I use are:
        - Follow instructions fast
        - Stay on task
        - Work without disturbing others

    - If students choose to break the rules, then give sanctions.
    - Give plenty of praise to those who follow the rules.
    - Remember YOU are the teacher and it is YOUR class and YOU are the BOSS.

    - I work as a supply teacher and as you know supply teaching
      is tough. Once I got it into my head and truly believed
      I was in charge and took control with my rules, my life
      became a lot easier. Everyone has choices. I choose not to
      shout and to speak calmly reminding everyone about what to
      do and the students will choose whether to follow or not.

    - I think Pepper is right: the starting point is knowing,
      absolutely knowing, you are in charge.

### Pycon: teach like an expert
    - Know your audience
    - what motivates them
    - set expectations:
        - state the goal of your lesson
        - what will students to take away from your lesson?
    - empathise with your audience
        - what is their level of understanding
        - dont assume!
        - dont make anyone feel "less than"
        - don't talk down to people
    - remove distractions
        - what might get in the way of learning
        - eg font size of PowerPoint
        - unexplained acronyms
    - use live coding sparingly
        - students looking at their screen, then your screen
    - remember your journey
        - where are they in their journey
        - noone gets it all right first time
    - smash JARGON
        - dont use insider speak, explain everything
    - keep it conversational - speak plainly
    - use specific examples, not `foo/bar/baz`
    - be practical, how will students use this,
        - dont just teach syntax, teach context
        - ask yourself: Why? When would I use this?
    - teach one concept at a time
    - model good behaviour: use good clear code yourself
    - MISTAKES happen- explain how to fix it, do mistakes on
      purpose
    - know when to HOLD BACK - answer the question that was
      asked, try not to go off on a long tangent
    - NUANCE isn't always helful- teach the most common
      situations
    - dont cover too much material - people have limits
    - foster a love of learning
        - foster curiosity
        - good magic phrase: "I dont know, lets find out"
    - celebrate progress  - positive feedback encourages growth
    - move about (e.g. people holding cards to teach slicing)
    - we learn by teaching
        - create opportunities for students to teach others


### Cummings Education thoughts
    - To improve English state schools, it will require good
      Academy chains to integrate:
      - A) more demanding and interesting maths/science curricula
        (such as the Gowers and Warner projects), including hard
        skills in modelling and problem-solving, statistics such
        as conditional probability (the inclusion of ‘natural
        frequencies’ in the new GCSE is a step forward);
      - B) more focus on essay writing and serious projects;
      - C) the use of frequent testing to see if pupils are
        learning, particularly ‘Concept Inventory‘ tests used in
        US undergrad physics courses by Nobel-winner Carl Wieman,
        and in Professor Warner’s Cambridge University physics
        16-18 project. (These tests show whether students have
        actually understood fundamental concepts or the teaching
        was a waste of time);
      - D) teacher hiring, firing, pay, and training;
      - E) a learning feedback loop (idea -> experiment ->
        learn-embed-new idea) inside the chain that is connected
        to a broader external network (including the research
        community) which allows incremental improvements in
        performance based on solid information about what works.
