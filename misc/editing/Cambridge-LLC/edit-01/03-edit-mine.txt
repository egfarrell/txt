Barlow (2004) observes that psychological interventions are
efficacious and safe for the treatment of social anxiety
disorder. More recently, following a systemic literature review
and large scale meta-analysis, Mayo-Wilson et al. (2014)
concluded that cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) was actually
more effective than pharmacotherapy with selective serotonin
reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), such as sertraline, for the
treatment of social anxiety. In addition, while setraline has
been viewed as the gold standard of pharmacological
interventions, Gordon (2013) lists numerous negative side effects
amongst adolescent patients, indicating that sertraline may not
be the most appropriate treatment. Nevertheless, while CBT may be
a safer and more effective treatment, it can also be taxing in
terms of the human resources required. Priyamvada (2009) reports
a 17-session CBT treatment regimen for social anxiety, with each
session lasting one hour. Similarly, Morris (n.d.) describes
a 12-session regimen at 2.5-hours per session, leading to the
conclusion that CBT demands a substantial time investment.

In an Australian context, this time commitment might prove
onerous, given that clinical psychologists comprise the bulk of
cognitive behavioural therapists. In 2014, there were 23,878
registered clinical psychologists in Australia (Australian
Institute of Health and Welfare, 2016). This indicates a ratio of
87 clinical psychologists per 100,000 population, which is
inadequate in terms of the problem posed by anxiety disorders.
Moreover, not all psychologists work in healthcare, so the actual
figure is certainly considerably lower. Although training new
clinical psychologists might seem like an effective response to
this shortfall, the minimum training time for clinical
psychologists is 6 years. Either our approach to training must
change, or we must look elsewhere to meet the demand for
cognitive behavioural therapists, in order to respond to the high
incidence of social anxiety.
