Barlow (2004) obversed that psychological interventions are
efficacious and safe for the treatment of social anxiety
disorder. More recently, following a systemic review and large
scale meta analysis, Mayo-Wilson et all. (2014) concluded that
cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is actually more efective then
pharmacotherapy with selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors
(SSRI's), like sertraline, for the treatment of social anxiety.
And while setraline has been seen as the gold standard of
pharmacological interventions for social anxiety, Gordon (2013)
list many side affects amongst teenager patients that suggest
that sertraline might not be the greatest treatment. But while
CBT may be safer treatment and more effective for social anxiety
than SRIs, it would also be taxing on resource usage. Priyamvada
(2009) reports a 17-session treatment regimen for social anxiety
with it being treated over 17 1 hour sessions. Morris describes
12 sessions at 2.5 hours per session, so we can conclude that CBT
demands an investment of time. 

In Australian, this commitment might be a bit difficult given
clinical psychologists comprise the bulk of cognitive behavioral
therapy. In 2014, there were 23,878 clinical psychologists in
Australia in 2014 (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare
2016). This shows a ratio of 87 clinical psychologists per
hundred thousand population, which is no adequate in terms of the
scope of the problem that anxiety disorders pose. Moreover, not
all psychologists are working in health care, so the actual
figure’s surely considerably lower. Training new clinical
psychologists might seem like a good response to this shortfall,
but, the minimum training time to be a clinical psychologist is
6 years. Either our approach to training clinical psychologists
has got to change, or we have to look somewhere else to meet the
demands for cognitive behavioral therapists, in order to respond
to the high incidents of social anxiety.
