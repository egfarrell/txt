### - Introduction
    - Copy-editing is the process of checking a document for
      mistakes, inconsistencies and repetition. The copy-editor
      produces copy fit for publication.
    - A copyeditor focuses on both small details as well as the
      big picture. Therefore, they must be meticulous readers
      with highly technical knowledge, while still being aware of
      the overall themes of a text.

### THE JOB OF A COPY-EDITOR
    - We must establish the differences between copy-editing and
      proofreading.
    - Editing has a different function to proofreading and occurs
      earlier in the publication process. Several drafts are
      produced in that process, and copy-editing is generally
      applied to the second-to-last version. (before
      proofreading).
    - Copy-editing and its close relative, sub-editing, involves
      a close reading and rewriting of this penultimate draft.
      The copy may be improved by excising redundancies,
      tautologies or repetition. The structure of the author’s
      argument is strengthened by inserting linking sentences
      between paragraphs, transitional phrases or concluding
      sentences.
    - Ultimately, a copy-editor aims to hone the text's sense
      & logic.
    - As we have seen, proofreading takes place after this final
      edit, using the lastest draft of the text. A proofreader
      checks for mistakes that may have escaped the entire
      editing process – or mistakes accidentally introduced
      during it.
    - Once all such errors have been corrected, a clean final
      copy should remain, ready for publishing or printing.

### - What a Copy-Editor Does
    - The copy-editor takes the raw material (aka the ‘copy’) and
      prepares it for publication as a finished product, whether
      that be a book, magazine article, newspaper item, website
      page, blog entry, podcast or radio script, menu, etc
    - The purpose of the copy-editor is to ensure that whatever
      appears in public is accurate, accessible, easy to follow,
      fit for purpose and free of errors, omissions,
      inconsistencies and repetition.
    - The copy-editing process picks up embarrassing mistakes,
      double-meanings and anomalies, alerts the client to
      possible legal problems and analyses the document structure
      for the publisher, typesetter or designer.
    - A copy-editor helps present the information effectively and
      attractively.
    - They begin by making sure that the text of the copy is
      complete. This might include checking that chapter titles
      and other parts of a book match the contents list and that
      all the illustrations have been delivered in a publishable
      form.
    - They may also check captions, footnotes, endnotes, and
      which system of referencing might be required.
    - They then ‘clean up’ the document, which may include fixing
      the page structure and set-up, the spacing and fonts,
      cutting out unwanted formatting, creating an actual style
      sheet (for the proofreader and other editorial staff to
      work from) and identifying any other problems.
    - As the copy-editor works through the material, he or she
      corrects errors in spelling, punctuation, grammar, style
      and usage, and also shortens over-long sentences and the
      overuse of stylistic issues such as italics, emboldening,
      capitalisation, punctuation marks and awkward usage of the
      passive voice.
    - He or she will query and correct factual mistakes, weak or
      vague arguments, any holes in the plot and anomalies or
      gaps in any numbering.
    - In works of fiction, they will also check that certain
      characters haven’t changed their name or eye colour, check
      for wrongful changes in tense or points-of-view and keep an
      eye on the timeline to ensure all is correct.
    - The overall watchword is _consistency_.
    - The main philosophy of a copy-editor is to always be aware
      of the bigger picture.
    - The major parts of a piece of writing which must be checked
      thoroughly, including the following items:

### - Content and structure
    - The copy-editor should ask:
        - Is anything missing or redundant?
        - Is there a logical order to events?
        - Do headings accurately describe what follows them?
        - Are any footnotes necessary and could supporting
          material be placed in an appendix?
        - Is a reference section, bibliography or glossary
          needed?
        - Are clickable links needed for electronic documents?

### - How to present information
    - This will depend on the intended readership, the type of
      material and the medium through which it will be read.
    - A rule of thumb is that sentences should generally be short
      and straightforward and paragraphs should begin when new
      ideas are introduced to assist readability and to break up
      the page.
    - _Crossheads_ can also help to break up a text and make it
      more easily digestible. Ask yourself if there are the
      correct amount of them. Either way, the structure may need
      rethinking or rearranging. The typesetting used can affect
      line length and an experienced copy-editor will judge how
      to allow for this.

### - Figures, illustrations, graphs & tables
    - Images need to be relevant and support the text, and the
      captions should also be self-explanatory. The body of the
      written text which accompanies these images or figures
      should _comment on the data_, rather than just repeat it.
    - A copy-editor will _advise_ a typesetter on the position of
      each element in the finished pages of the publication, as
      well as checking that all the artwork submitted is suitable
      for publication or reproduction on the internet.
    - He or she will also keep track of the permissions
      (including filing them where they can be accessed in future
      if there are any problems) and hone the acknowledgements.

### - Wording
    - Is the language pitched at the correct level for the
      intended readership?
    - Does any jargon, technical terms or abbreviations need
      explaining?
    - Is the tone, style and vocabulary of the text appropriate
      and do they add authority to or undermine the author?
    - A copy-editor is generally aware of the current acceptable
      usage and best practice of English, and will make decisions
      on slang or the usage of foreign words.

### - Six Rules for Copy-Editors
    - George Orwell’s six rules for writers (from ‘Politics and the English Language’ (1946))
        - Never use a metaphor, simile or other figure of speech that you are used to seeing in print
        - Never use a long word where a short one will do
        - If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out
        - Never use the passive where you can use the active
        - Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word or a jargon word if you can think of an everyday English equivalent
        - Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous

### - Consistency
    - While working, a copy-editor will keep a list of decisions
      on alternative spellings, hyphenation, italics, capitals,
      units of measurement, how quotations are presented and much
      else.
    - This will usually be the standard style guide, which is
      constantly updated whenever a problem arises and an
      editorial decision is made on how to proceed thereafter.
    - A text must never contradict itself. Likewise with
      accompanying illustrations, tables, graphs and captions.
    - All internal links or _online cross-references_ must work
      (though these sometimes fall into disuse after publication)

### - Accuracy and anomalies
    - Writers are always responsible for what they write, but it
      is a copy editor’s job to spot misquotes, factual error,
      misspelt names, misused wordage, incorrect calculations and
      incomplete references; in this role, you must always check
      or query such errors and anything else that does not seem
      to make sense or looks out of place.
    - The copy-editor scrutinises facts, dates, quotations and
      references, but you shouldn’t routinely check every single
      one unless this is priced for and agreed with a client.

### - Legalities
    - It is a copy editor’s job to flag plagiarism, breaches of
      copyright, potential libel, possible obscenity and
      incitement to racial hatred;
    - However, the ultimate responsibility for these decisions
      remains with the writer, editor and publisher – you can
      only offer judgement and advice.
    - Some are very cautious while others will cite the Duke of
      Wellington’s (alleged) maxim: “Publish and be damned!”

### - Length
    - Is the text too long or short? The writer of a book,
      magazine or newspaper article will usually have an agreed
      word count. (approximate)
    - Some writers will need more guidance than others,
      particularly if they are less experienced.
    - The writer might want to change or add material after they
      have submitted the work. As a copy-editor, you can suggest
      ways to reduce its length or how to make better use the available space
      without reducing the size of the typeface.

### - Technical issues
    - With experience, a copy-editor will learn much about the
      technical aspects of publishing and will be able to discuss
      issues regarding layout, printing or web publishing.
    - Details such as the text’s extent, page breaks, special
      characters to be used and image types will be agreed with
      the client, editor-in-chief, designer, typesetter or
      printer, with an eye on keeping costs to a minimum and
      meeting publishing schedules.

### - The 'Seven Cs' of Copy-editing
    - There are many minor tasks involved in copy-editing, but
      the end result should be:
        - Clear
        - Correct
        - Coherent
        - Complete
        - Concise
        - Consistent
        - Credible
    - You will have to be ultra-professional – the only safety
      net preventing you from failure is the proofreader they
      only correct minor technical errors.

### - What a copy-editor doesn't do
    - There are many tasks in writing and publication and
      a copy-edtior is not responsible for the following:
    - EXTENSIVE REWRITING OR RESTRUCTURING
        - This is substantive editing and should have been done
          in the original writing or developmental stage
    - GHOSTWRITING
        - This is still part of the writing stage and should have
          been done by a paid professional ghostwriter
    - PROOFREADING
        - This comes after copy-editing and is your last bulwark
          against mistakes
    - TEXT OR COVER DESIGN
        - On small press books or magazines; this can be done by
          the whole editorial team, but generally it's not
          a copy-editor's job
    - INDEXING
        - This is a job of paid professionals and is its own
          distinct discipline
    - RESEARCH
        - To be done by actual researchers or authors (though you
          may do light research in the form of fact-checking)
    - PICTURE RESEARCH
        - Again, a discipline in itself
    - COPYRIGHT PERMISSIONS
        - Check they have been sought, but it isn't your job to
          actually get them
    - PROJECT MANAGEMENT
        - this is usually the job of an in-house departmental
          manager or publisher
    - A large number of professional editors do offer some or all
      of these services, but this requires separate fees and
      contractual negotiation.

### - glossary of copy-editing terms
    - AA                                 : Stands for author's alteration and indicates changes made by the author him- or herself on a set of proofs.
    - Abstract                           : The synopsis of an academic paper which usually appears before the main text.
    - Air                                : The white space on a printed page.
    - All Cap                            : This abbreviation means that the text is all in capital letters.
    - Ampersand                          : The full name of the '&' ('and') character.
    - Art                                : Encompasses all the illustrations, maps, graphs, photographs and drawings to be included in a text.
    - 'At' sign                          : The full name of the '@' character, usually prefiguring an email, Twitter or Instagram address.
    - Bibliography                       : The list of sources cited or consulted in the writing of a book or paper, which are usually published as part of the end matter.
    - Blockquote                         : An extract or quoted passage set out of, or next to, the running text without being enclosed by quotation marks.
    - Boilerplate                        : This is text or copy that is reused without any changes.
    - Box-out                            : This is text or type that is framed by a border to make it stand out from the rest of the text or to highlight discrete blocks of information.
    - Character                          : This describes an individual letter, number, symbol or even a space between actual characters in the case of online text.
    - Citation                           : An entry – usually numbered or alphabetised – which directs the reader to other texts that serve as source materials or factual support.
    - Clean up                           : The action of incorporating the author's responses to the copyediting into the final hard copy or computer file (usually before it is proofread).
    - Copy                               : A manuscript which is to be typeset.
    - Corrigendum or addendum            : A printer's error which has been discovered too late to be corrected in a document or published product and has had to be included in a separately printed list.
    - Cross-reference (aka x-ref)        : A phrase which mentions another part of the same document and is usually referred to in a standard, often abbreviated, way.
    - Curly quotes or smart quotes       : A way of saying double inverted commas which are curved and are usually placed around the quoted text.
    - Dead copy                          : Describes a manuscript that has been finished, typeset and proofread.
    - Dingbat                            : An ornamental character, such as a smiley face, which is used on printed matter (sometimes in a similar way to emoticons online).
    - Display type                       : This is the large type used for chapter titles, headings and crossheads.
    - Ellipsis                           : The correct name for the three dots ('. . .') character which indicates a pause or absent material.
    - Em dash                            : The name for the longest dash ('—') character, which is often rendered in manuscripts as two hyphens ( '–' ).
    - En dash                            : Name of the medium-length dash ('–') character.
    - End matter                         : The material found at the end of a manuscript or book, including the appendices, endnotes, glossary, bibliography and indexes.
    - Figure                             : An illustration or graph printed within and as part of the continuing text.
    - Folio                              : Describes the page number in a type-set text. Further to this, a 'drop folio' is a page number found at the bottom of a page while a 'blind folio' has no page number, even though that page is included in the numbering of the text.
    - Font                               : Describes the characters which are part of a given style and size of a typeface.
    - Footer or running foot             : Describes one or two lines of copy which are set at the bottom of every page of a document.
    - Frontmatter or prelims             : The usual material to be found at the front of a manuscript or book, which can include the title and copyright pages, the preface, the table of contents, lists of maps and illustrations, the acknowledgements and the introduction.
    - Galley                             : The initial printed version or proof of a document.
    - Gutter                             : Describes the space or margin between facing pages; at the printers, this often has colour tests on it before the pages are trimmed.
    - Hard copy                          : Any text that appears printed on paper at any stage of the proofing process.
    - House style                        : The editorial style preferences of a publisher which cover almost every aspect of the final published page.
    - Justify                            : Items of typeset so that the margin is aligned to them. Book pages are generally justified to the left and right, while other documents can often be justified only to the left (a condition called 'ragged right')
    - Kill                               : To instruct the deletion of text or illustrations or to edit out a story (perhaps you remember the old comedy series set in a press room called Drop the Dead Donkey?).
    - Lead or lede                       : The journalists' term to describe the first few sentences or paragraph of a story.
    - Leading or linespacing             : Describes the spacing of lines in a text.
    - Legend or caption                  : This is the explanation that accompanies an illustration or a figure.
    - Manuscript                         : The initial text of an author's work which has been submitted for editing and publication.
    - Mark up                            : To insert composition, proofreading or editing instructions into text or on copy or layouts.
    - MS                                 : The abbreviation for a manuscript.
    - Page proof                         : The printed version or proof of a document in the page form in which it will appear when published.
    - Pass                               : The read-through of a manuscript by a copy-editor.
    - PE                                 : The abbreviation for 'printer's error'.
    - Pica                               : A printer's unit of measure for type and letters.
    - Plate                              : A page of images or illustrations separate from the text (though they can certainly have text on them).
    - Point                              : A unit of measurement in typesetting which is used to indicate the various font sizes.
    - Proof                              : One of potentially several trial sheets of printed material produced to be edited, checked and corrected. Hence 'proofed' and 'proofreading'.
    - Redline                            : The on-screen or hard-copy version of a manuscript which indicates which text has been added, deleted or edited since the previous version or proof.
    - Reproduction proof                 : Describes a high-quality proof produced for the final review before printing.
    - Rough                              : An initial, preliminary page layout.
    - Rule                               : A vertical or horizontal line on a page, often used to position text and images or illustrations.
    - Running head or header             : One or two lines of copy set at the top of each page of a document, which usually includes the title.
    - Sans serif                         : A particular typeface that does not have a serif or crossline on the main strokes of the characters.
    - Sentence-style or initial cap only : A capitalisation style for headlines, headings and titles in which all the words are in lower case except those that would normally be capitalised in a standard sentence.
    - Serial comma or Oxford comma       : A comma which precedes the word 'and' or 'or' in a sentence involving a list of items (for example: “one, two, and three”). This is usually only used in US English.
    - Sidebar                            : A short piece of writing, article or news story that complements or amplifies the point of a major feature or story.
    - Sink                               : Describes the distance from the top of a printed page to any element placed on that page.
    - Specs                              : Short for specifications, this indicates the typeface used, point size, spacing and margins, or other technical details.
    - Stet                               : This term indicates that text marked for deletion should be restored and derives from the Latin for “let it stand.”
    - Style sheet or style guide         : A form or document created by an editor or copy-editor to record editorial decisions applied to the text used in a particular company or publication.
    - Trim or boil                       : To reduce the length of a story or piece of writing.
    - 'Typo' or typographical error      : A misprint.
    - Upper case                         : The publishing and printing industry term for capital letters.

### - Basic Copy-editing
    - Copy-editing is full of nuanced detail and judgement calls.
    - It is best done as a disciplined routine and consists of
      the following steps:
        - Scan the copy carefully for errors or ways it can be
          improved. As with proofreading, this can be done
          on-screen or via a printed proof.
        - Edit the text in Word, making corrections on-screen
          using `Track Changes` (or PDF equivalent)
        - Having made the corrections, complete an _edit summary_
        - Preview your changes, and ensure they are saved them.
        - Pass the edited document to the Project Manager, editor
          or proofreader, depending on the protocol.

### - Common mistakes
    - its and it's
    - there, their and they're
    - your, you're and you
    - lose and loose
    - lie and lay (in various tenses)
    - who's and whose
    - bias and biased

### - Capitalisation and formatting
    - Words defined, described or referenced as themselves should
      be either italicised or placed in single inverted commas.
      For example “The term style (or 'style') also refers to the
      layout of an article”
    - Headings always begin with a single capital letter in
      'sentence-style'. The only other capital letters in in
      headings are proper names and acronyms. (PTC course
      different)
    - Headlines are different and can be all caps or all initial
      caps letter, apart from prepositions and conjunctions;
      e.g.: “Man Left his House to Bite Dog”
    - The titles of works of art, plays, musicals and operas, TV
      series, films, novels and non-fiction books and long poems
      (such as Beowulf or The Wasteland) are usually _italicised_
      rather than put in single or double quotation marks;
        - e.g., The Fall of the House of Usher by Edgar Allan Poe
    - The titles of songs, short stories, individual episodes of
      television series and short poems should be in quotation
      marks; e.g., “Yesterday” (or 'Yesterday') by The Beatles.

### - Punctuation
    - Location sentence constructions such as London, England,
      generally require a comma after both elements. e.g.: “He
      now resides in London, England, after leaving London,
      Ontario, for work.”
    - Dates can be rendered in several ways and a client must
      choose a specific style. e.g.: 18 March 1964; September 6,
      2016; 18th March 1964.
    - Decade names should not include an apostrophe before the
      letter 's' – this is a genuine grammatical error.
        - Correct:   “He was born in the 1960s."
        - Incorrect: “He was born in the 1960's."
        - In some cases, decades can be referred to as the 60s or
          70s, but it is best to include the century signifier.
    - Watch out for "greengrocers' apostrophes" and ensure all
      the required apostrophes are included.
    - Always check for unnecessary words, over-complexity and
      redundant phrases. Effective writing is usually accessible,
      clear and concise.
    - Quotations should not be changed (with the exception of
      trivial spelling and typo errors). If original errors are
      included, then `[sic]` can be placed after the error to
      indicate that the editors have seen and allowed it to
      stannd.
    - Expedient insertions and omissions in quotations may be
      acceptable if they are marked as `[...]`

### - Do not change
    - A number of style guides advise against grammatical
      constructions such as the passive voice, split infinitives,
      beginning a sentence with a conjunction and ending clauses
      with a preposition.
    - However, these are common in high-quality publications and
      should not be “fixed” without considering the effects of
      any changes.
    - Turning a passive phrase into an active one may change the
      topic or 'feel' of a paragraph inappropriately.
    - Any attempts to improve the language must be based on
      careful consideration of tone, clarity and consistency,
      rather than dogmatic adherence to stylistic rules .

    - Spelling
    - Spelling corrections should be one of the most important
      considerations of the copy-editor. The proofreader needs to
      be painstaking but they should receive fairly 'clean'
      copies.
    - Points to watch:
        - Correcting spelling mistakes and typos
        - Making sure the text consistently uses either UK or US
          English and spellings match accordingly (e.g. flavour,
          colour and centre versus flavor, color and center)
        - Articles about a certain region may use the region's
          dialect spelt out phonetically if you are using quotes,
          as long as it is clear this is being done;
        - Rendering unusual spellings in italics is one common
          protocol in this context
        - Do not rely on spell-checkers!

### - The copy editor's mindset
    - According to Butcher (2006): “The good copy-editor is
      a rare creature: an intelligent reader and a tactful and
      sensitive critic; someone who cares enough about perfection
      to spend time checking small points of consistency in
      someone else's work but has the good judgement not to waste
      time or antagonise the author with unnecessary changes.”
    - The job is a nuanced one, relying on your language skills
      and sensitivities to the tone and purpose of the writing
      and its prospective audience.

### BASIC RULES OF COPY-EDITING:

### - Consistency (Style guides)
    - No matter the type of text, there must be consistency
      regarding spelling, capitalisation and terminology.
    - The most efficient way to ensure this is to create a 'style
      sheet' or 'style guide'.
    - If you are editing a document for a publication or
      institution, many stylistic decisions will have been made
      previously and incorporated into the style guide. However
      further changes and decisions are at your own discretion
      and must be recorded.
    - Items to list in a style guide:
        - Which words should be hyphenated or unhyphenated. e.g.,
          'copy-editor' or 'copyeditor',  'co-ordinates' or
          'coordinates'. etc
        - These decisions are often a matter of taste, but should
          be rigorously consistent
        - Whether you can use 'em' dashes
        - Should italics be used to emphasise stress
        - How reference lists should be constructed
        - The point size etc of text and headings
        - When slang or Latin words are allowable
        - Which terms will be used to describe important objects
          in the piece?
            - e.g., if a health study refers to participants as
              'patients' at the beginning, it should continue to
              do so throughout and avoid terms such as 'cases',
              'participants', or 'subjects'.
        - Which words should be capitalised; e.g., 'The society'
          or 'The Society'
        - The format of headings and subheadings. ie. should main
          headings be in bold and subheadings in italics etc
        - Will punctuation occur at the end of entries in lists?
        - `In this course: bullet points omit full stops while
          numbered lists do have full stops.`
        - This is neither right nor wrong but a stylistic
          decision which must be consistent.

### - Never trust your spell checker
    - Spell checkers are never perfect. They are rigidly
      programmed and more nuanced human mistakes can be missed.
    - Always read through your work one final time, even after
      using a spell-checker.
    - Note: spell checkers won't detect missing words such as
      'not', which can change the entire meaning of a text. It
      also misses correctly spelt words, but which appear in the
      wrong place.
            - Examples include:
            - their/there
            - in/it
            - that/then/than
            - causal/casual
    - Spellcheckers often cannot check technical academic terms,
      especially in scientific writing.

### - Abbreviations
    - Any abbreviations of proper nouns must be defined on first
      usage by placing the appropriate acronyms in brackets.
      E.g.: the Royal Society for the Protection of Birds (RSPB)
      or 'typo' for typographical error.
        - After you've made this apparent, you should continue to
          use the abbreviation for the relevant word throughout
          the rest of the text.
    - An exception is when editing an academic paper. There are
      also some abbreviations which are so well known that you
      can assume that all readers will be aware of what they
      mean; e.g., TNT or DNA.

### - Numbers
    - There are many different stylistic ways of rendering
      numbers. In this course numers up to nine are spellt out as
      words and numbers from 10 onwards are written as numerals;
      e.g.: “It took three games of darts before Ken scored 180.”
    - However, there are many exceptions: abbreviations of
      measurement should always be given as numerals (e.g.: '3
      cm' or '2 L')
        - and abbreviation will always be singular.
    - Very large, rounded numbers may be rendered as
      a combination of words and numerals (e.g., '6 million'
      instead of '6,000,000') or entirely in words ('six
      billion').
        - However, you will have to make a style decision on how
          to do this and you should stick to this decision
          throughout the text you are editing.
    - Another rule is that sentences should never begin with
      a numeral; e.g.,
        - correct:
        - “In a survey, nine out of 10 respondents said”,
        - incorrect:
        - “19 respondents said …”.
        - Or it may be accept to say:
        - “Nineteen respondents said that … ”.

### - Consider the potential audience
    - Is the language style appropriate for its intended
      readership?
    - e.g., a friendly, chatty style should be avoided for
      a conference report, but also ensure that informal
      documents don't sound overly dry and academic.
    - You also need to assess whether the text is pitched at the
      expected knowledge of the readership, particularly if the
      finished document is likely to be read by children.

### - Beware catchphrases and clichés
    - It is often tempting to include popular phrases to help to
      liven up dry writing. Or inject some humour.
    - However, the copy-editor must ensure such phrases have been
      used correctly, particularly if they come a culture the
      copy-editor is not very familiar with; e.g., the writings
      of a politician would look be incongruent in modern London
      street slang.
    - Another example from medicine: “These results show that the
      procedure is a risky business” would be better rendered:
      “These results show that there are risks associated with
      the procedure.”

### - Levels of editing
    - Other tasks performed by a copy-editor may include:
    - Checking grammar, spelling, syntax and punctuation
    - Checking for errors in continuity
    - Checking for factual inaccuracy, particularly in
      non-fiction manuscripts. Also check that names and dates
      are correct and correctly referenced.
    - Checking for potential legal problems, particularly to
      ensure that a text does not libel other parties. Sometimes
      a lawyer will need to be consulted
    - Checking for inconsistency within a story. This includes:
      contradictions, character descriptions, plot development
      and descriptions of locations and seasons.
    - Do the characters remain true to type throughout the story?
    - There may be conflicting descriptions of a building, such
      as commenting on a house as being “mock Tudor” in the
      opening chapter and later mentioning the “pebble dashing on
      the front of the house.”

### - The client's brief
    - The client will inform you what the work will entail.
    - This is referred to as the `client's brief` and can range
      from an initial email to a multi-page document, accompanied
      by a style guide and legal disclaimers.
    - You will need to be certain what the job entails before
      supplying a quote to the client.
    - The following slides explain what to expect but nothing
      beats experience!

### - Clarify the brief
    - The client's brief may cover proofreading or other
      affiliated tasks.
    - However, you may not have exact details of what the client
      means by 'proofreading'. The brief must be clarified.
    - You must ensure you and the client share a common
      understanding of the task. You need to ensure all
      requirements, costs and timings are clear to both parties.

    - The following are key questions for your client:
        - Is there a style guide or style sheet to work from?
        - Are there other references or detailed instructions to
          deal with?
        - Will the work involve text only, or will there be
          figures, tables and illustrations?
        - Will the work involve a straightforward text document
          or is it contained within a PDF or InDesign file for
          correcting?

    - Such questions are important because they will help you
      calculate how much time the job may take. You will then be
      able to quote an accurate price.

    - Here are tips when pricing up the client's brief:
        - Ask to _see the text_ before you start work, or request
          a sample
        - Find out whether the text has been copy-edited already.
        - Has the client been happy with previous work if they've
          employed a proofreader before, and what did or didn't
          work for them?
        - ensure you have accounted for all predictable tasks
          before quoting a final price, even if this involves
          a lot of upfront communication. You may need to seek
          further further clarification.

    - The text you've been given might need extra work but you
      won't know unless you ask for a representative sample.
    - An ideal sample is between  5–10 per cent of the total
      wordage, preferably extracted from the body of the text.
    - This is because most writers will pay more attention to
      grammar and spelling at the beginning and end of their
      documents, and slacken inbetween.
    - This sample will show how much effort is required for the
      work. It may be that the text is not at an appropriate
      stage for proofreading. This is an opportunity to either
      add pricing for copy-editing or decline the job. In either
      case, inform the client of the problems that have arisen.

### - Getting your quote right
    - Now that you know what the client expects, you will need to
      quote a price.
    - If you're new, you may well suffer from self-doubt or
      indecision about what the correct fee should be. However,
      even if you've only begun freelancing, you shouldn't
      underprice yourself.
    - Remember, you're a skilled professional and, while it might
      be tempting to work for a pittance or for free when you're
      starting out, the price should reflect your abilities.
    - Refer to the CIEP's minimum rates which are regularly
      updated to reflect industry standards:
        - https://www.ciep.uk/resources/suggested-minimum-rates/
    - Also check freelancer websites to guage current pricing.
    - Remember, these prices are advisory minimums and you must
      calculate how long the job will take and what you think
      your time is worth.
    - Factors which influence pricing are:
        - How long is the source text?
        - How many corrections will it need?
        - How complex or technical is the subject matter?
        - Are non-proofreading jobs included?
        - When is the deadline?

### - Realistic assessment
    - Novel 100,000 words.
    - Proofing Rate: 2,000 words per hour
    - Working day: 6 hours per day.
    - Therefore the work could take up to 8.5 days.
    - At a suggested minimum hourly rate of £25, you will be
      charging the client `£1,250`.
    - If you charge by the word, and a minimum suggested
      professional rate might be _2.5 pence per word_, the client
      will be paying £2,500.
    - That is a reasonable price for a book of that length that
      has been well written and copy-edited.

    - In real life, the text could have significant mistakes and
      inconsistencies. Such a document will seriously slow down
      your progress and is likely to require some copy-editing as
      well as proofreading.
    - It is possible that you might only be able to cope with
      1,000 words per hour – a scenario that could see you
      advance through the text for a lengthy 100 hours.
    - At the novice's minimum rate of £25, this will cost the
      client £2,500, while at a more standard experienced rate
      you could be talking about a £5,000 fee.
    - If you charge by the word, 5 pence is more realistic for
      this work and would see you billing the client for the same
      final bill of £5,000.
    - Therefore, seeing a representative sample is essential.

### - Do not quote low
    - You may be tempted to give a low quote to gain experience.
    - However, there are several reasons not to do this:
    - The client may give you more work but having underpriced
      yourself initially, you may be expected to continue at
      those rates.
    - If you quote a more professional rate per hour or word,
      based on the predicted work rate (that is, the time plus
      price) expected of a more experienced copy-editor or
      proofreader, the job will probably take longer than
      expected.
        - That means that you will indeed get a bit less money.
          However, as you gain experience, you will work faster
          and the money will come up to speed, too
    - Quoting low does not always mean you'll get the work. It
      may actually give the impression you are less professional.

### - The deadline
    - Agree on a deadline and stick to it. If it seems likely the
      deadline will be missed, inform the client as soon as
      possible.
    - Most clients will understand as long as you explain the
      difficulties well and, if you meet the deadline anyway, you
      will look efficient and professional.
    - With experience, you might be offered short-notice work and
      be able to charge more accordingly.
    - However, a novice needs to be sure that they can do the job
      in the time allotted, so this course of action isn't
      recommended.
    - To avoid too many problems, ask for as long a run-up as
      possible.

### - Check everything
    - When the work arrives, check that everything you need is
      included and the files are complete and uncorrupted.
    - If you cannot start the job immediately, at least check
      everything is present and correct; otherwise delays may
      occur later.
    - Always ensure you save and back up your work. This act of
      insurance could well be very valuable.

### - Be efficient
    - When you start the job don't dive in headfirst.
    - Instead, spend some time on preparation. This ensures
      nothing is forgotten.
    - Many proofreaders make a _simple checklist_ to work
      through. They can add tasks to the list as they arise and
      check them off when complete etc
    - This is also psychologically advantageous, giving the
      feeling of progression.

### - Get sectioned!
    - Part of being organised is splitting the job into
      manageable sections – these are known as separate `passes`.
    - This means you are less likely to miss out anything and can
      focus on one task at a time.
    - This is a particularly useful plan if you're working on
      a complex document containing images, tables, figures and
      illustrations, as many non-fiction books do.
    - Checklist items include:
        - Page and chapter numbers
        - Consecutive headings and crossheads
        - Chapter titles
        - The contents list (cross-referenced with the chapter
          headings to ensure that they match)
        - Numbered tables, figures, illustrations, maps or lists
    - It is often best to handle these before starting on the
      text itself. It gets the 'fiddly bits' out of the way and
      allows you to cross-compare them with the text later.

### - The style guide revisited
    - A key quiestion is to ask the client if a `style guide`
      exists (see ./02-Style-Guide.txt).
    - If not,  you can use a standard style manual or make use of
      a previous style guide from another client.
    - If there is a style guide, it may well not cover everything
      you encounter. Therefore you will have to update the style
      guide by keeping notes about the decisions made and then
      referring back to them as you work. (to ensure consistency)
    - A separate style sheet should be compiled for every
      project.
    - This will demonstrate your professionalism while ensuring
      you remember all decisions made.

### - The art of the query
    - There will almost certainly be a Author queries as the work
      progresses.
    - To appear professional, group all queries together in one
      email rather than sending them randomly.
    - Of course, some may need an urgent response. Otherwise, it
      is easier to track all queries and answers in one email (or
      query document).
    - However, ensure that all queries are relevant to the work.

### - Editing with the reader in mind
    - Writing and reading are all about communication.
    - To ensure that the ideas behind a piece of writing are
      adequately communicated you must ensure that a text is
      directed towards the intended audience in terms of style,
      tone of voice and expected level of knowledge.
    - This is where editing comes into its own.
    - When you come to copy-edit a document, the text will have
      already gone through several passes and should have
      a settled structure. Firming up this structure is the job
      of the editor and sub-editor.
    - As a copy-editor, you will be ensuring consistency and
      accuracy in the text. Also fine-tuning it with the
      potential readership in mind.
    - It will be your job not only to make the text grammatically
      correct but also for ensuring the story or narrative is
      engaging for the reader.
    - Good writing tells a story and takes the reader on
      a journey. The main aim is some kind of satisfaction,
      whether that be a broadening of knowledge, amusement,
      intrigue or just appreciation of the craft of writing.
    - However, even if the story is good, poor editing can spoil
      the experience and trigger doubts in the reader about the
      skills of the writer.
    - Your job is to ensure the 'reading journey' is comfortable
      and to compel the reader to remain on that journey.

    - This is why you need to see a sample of the text in advance
      before beginning to editing.
    - Ask yourself about the likely readership if it will meet
      their expectations. Use your own knowledge of the market
      and experience of reading to assess this.
    - Use questions such as:
        - Is this children's book aiming too low or too high?
        - Does the author of the popular science article display
          enough technical knowledge to be convincing while still
          explaining a difficult subject to the layperson?
        - Is this news report obviously biased and untrustworthy?
    - Only you can answer these questions, and you must pitch
      pitch each text at the correct level for its audience.
