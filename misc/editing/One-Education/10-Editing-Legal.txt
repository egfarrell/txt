# Copy-Editing: Legal Checks

### - Introduction
    - If you are writing about real events involving real people
      or re-using another writer's work your own, you must be
      aware of the legal implications.
    - An important part of a copy editor’s job, particularly in
      news and journalism, is to ensure that printed information
      is accurate and legal.
    - This module covers:
        - The key publishing laws
        - How to apply publishing laws (+ examples)

### - The Key Publishing Laws
    - The copy-editor is a gate-keeper between the writer and the
      reader, and must have enough legal knowledge to thwart any
      likelihood of any legal action.
    - Some knowledge is plain common sense but you should also
      understand the following topics:
        - Copyright
        - Plagiarism and other illegal uses of copyrighted
          material
        - Fair use and fair dealing
        - Libel
        - Contracts
        - Data protection
        - Privacy
        - Contempt of court
    - The following information is quite basic and the reality is
      often more complex.
    - However, it serves as an introduction to legal matters in
      publishing with the proviso that you will need to deal with
      each case individually and consultation with a lawyer could
      also be an option.

### - Copyright
    - Writers automatically own the copyright of all the work
      that they produce under UK and US law, which means that no
      one else can use that work without their permission.
    - A writer’s copyright lasts from the moment the work is
      created until 70 years after the author’s death.
    - A standard notice of copyright is found in all published
      works, usually on the reverse of the title page.
    - It generally comprises of the word ‘copyright’, the
      standard copyright symbol, the author’s name and the
      publication date.
    - e.g.: “Copyright © 2019 David Callahan.”
    - This is the basic copyright claim, but some copyright
      holders include longer copyright notices that list the
      activities permitted or not permitted and contact details
      to follow-up on extra permissions.
    - Copyright is a complex topic, but a good overview is
      available online at the IPO Intellectual Property Office :
        - https://www.gov.uk/government/organisations/intellectual-property-office

### - Plagiarism
    - Plagiarism is the act of passing off someone else’s work as
      your own.
    - Plagiarism is not actually a crime but expose a writer to
      prosecution for copyright infringement if it happens.
    - Several well-known writers branded plagiarists in recent
      years simply by quoting large amounts of existing work or
      being lax in attributing sourced work.

### - Fair Use and Fair Dealing
    - It is allowable to include a small amount of pre-existing
      material by another author – this is called ‘fair use’ or
      ‘fair dealing’.
    - However, the law says that you must give “sufficient
      acknowledgement” of the source of the text you’ve included.
      This should clearly state the origin of the material and
      the amount used should be limited to that which is not
      deemed _substantial_.
    - It is difficult to assess what might constitute
      a 'substantial' part of a work and is best to err on the
      side of caution and seek advice.
    - e.g., if a writer has extracted several paragraphs from
      a short story for inclusion in their own published work,
      the publisher of the original work may be within their
      rights to seek legal redress and compensation.
    - After you’ve sought legal opinion, it may be a good idea to
      ask for the direct permission of the publisher.
    - In the UK, uses which qualifies as ‘fair’ in publishing are
      quite well defined and include reviews, criticism, private
      study and the reporting of current events or news.
    - However, quoting even small sections of film dialogue and
      song lyrics is often very limited.

### - Libel
    - Libel is making defamatory remarks in print (or recorded
      media) about a person or organisation that have no provable
      basis in fact;
    - It is not the same as 'slander', which is defined as
      defamation via the spoken word.
    - A film disclaimer such as “Names and characters are
      fictitious. Any resemblance to actual persons, living or
      dead, is purely coincidental …” is to avoid any living
      person considering themselves libelled. (if they believe
      a character has been based on them). This may lead to
      creative methods of dodging such issues in work based on
      real people.
    - A quirk of UK law is that libellous remarks don’t have to
      be aimed at a particular person to be actionable – you can
      be taken to court by anyone who feels that they’ve been
      injured by something you have written.
    - UK libel laws are so wide-ranging that many plaintiffs
      choose to sue international publications in the UK. It is
      sufficient for a lawyer to show that someone’s reputation
      has suffered as a result of something written or published;
        - in other words, the text caused a “reasonable person to
          have thought worse of” the person (or company) in
          question.
    - It is somewhat harder to sue for libel in the EU or US and
      each country has different definitions as to what
      constitutes libel. If you’re editing something for
      a foreign or international market, it is best to consult
      a legal specialist on these matters.

### - Check Fictional Names
    - Be careful checking characters’ names in a story.
    - In fiction, a character’s name must be believable and, to
      be believable, it must resemble an existing name.
    - You may wish to use the name 'Paul Newman' for your serial
      killer character, but it must be made clear there is no
      relation to the movie actor.
    - A more unusual name should be checked on Google or in the
      phone directory to see if it exists.
    - Common combinations of first and surnames don’t usually
      cause these problems. Most people realise when a character
      is fictional and not necessarily based on a real person.
    - If an author needs to include a specific address in
      a story, you should ensure the address is fictional (either
      the house number doesn’t exist, or the street itself
      doesn’t). Also ensure there is no one of a similar name to
      your protagonist who lives at a similar address.

### - Contracts
    - Contracts are a minefield as there are no standard
      agreements and you may need legal advice to create and
      interpret each document.
    - That said, many contain standard clauses (known as
      ‘boilerplate’) but may vary widely in the details.
    - Even within the same organisation, new talent may get more
      favourable terms than others and you will need to refer to
      each individual contract.
    - In most contracts a writer will have agreed to produce
      a certain amount of work by certain deadlines. There may be
      occasions where a copy-editor will consult the author’s
      contract to check the terms.
    - It is less usual for article/feature writers to have
      contracts for specific articles. However they will have
      general employment contracts or be contracted to write
      certain articles.
    - As a copy-editor you may have a query or problem with the
      contractually agreed output of a writer but any decision
      should be taken by the editor, publisher or proprietor.

### - Data protection
    - New laws introduced by the EU in 2018,  specify mandated
      ways of dealing with people’s personal data to ensure its
      security.
    - As a copy-editor, you must ensure these laws and
      regulations are observed. They are particularly pertinent
      in news and journal editing.
    - Essentially, publishers must be able to answer 6 questions
      with a positive: See https://www.whatsnewinpublishing.com
        - Can you show how the data you hold will be used and
          what it will be used for?
        - Can you prove the data collected will be used only for
          the purposes explicitly specified at the time of its
          collection?
        - Can you limit your data collection to specifically what
          is necessary to serve the purpose for which it is
          collected?
        - Can you prove the data held is accurate?
        - Are you going to store the data for only as long as
          necessary for its intended purpose?
        - Can you prove that you can prevent the data from being
          used by unauthorised parties and/or accidental loss by
          putting appropriate security measures in place?
    - This EU-approved data protection legislation is: 'GDPR'
      – General Data Protection Legislation.
    - GDPR ensures the responsibility for accountability is
      placed with the publisher, who must be ablet to demonstrate
      its compliance with GDPR principles and how they are being
      managed and tracked.
    - You must maintain accurate records of how and why personal
      data was collected as well as the processes and used.

### PRIVACY
    - Regarding the individual, GDPR exists to protect their
      privacy in both the digital and physical realms.
    - Regarding your role as a copy-editor or proofreader, GDPR
      protects any personal details held by you, your client or
      your employer, and means you must exclude such details from
      any text unless the person concerned explicitly agreed to
      their inclusion in writing.
    - It also governs the usual protections of personal data to
      be found in any office or company.
    - There are seven principles underpinning GDPR:
        - Lawfulness, fairness and transparency
        - Purpose limitation
        - Data minimisation
        - Accuracy
        - Storage limitation
        - Integrity and confidentiality (security)
        - Accountability
    - The ramifications of these principles for publishing
      companies will now be explored:

### - Explicit Consent
    - Publishers must hold documented consent to gather and
      process personal data. They should:
        - ask consent before using or processing anyone’s
          personal information
        - clearly explain their data privacy policy
        - make it easy for a person to withdraw their consent
        - to verify the age of any minors whose data is being
          stored and used, and obtain consent from their legal
          guardians
        - immediately inform those affected each time they update
          their data privacy policy
    - In short, the principle of explicit consent to collect and
      process data is based on an explicit confirmation from the
      person concerned, once a publisher has informed them of the
      purpose of data use.

### - Accountability
    - Publishers should meet the following requirements:
        - A senior member of staff should set up a ‘GDPR
          compliance map’
        - All contracts and data protection policies should be
          kept updated
        - Outsourced contractors who process personal data on the
          publisher’s behalf update their own data protection
          policies and contracts
        - Improved IT procedures and security must be implemented
        - A system must be in place to regularly review data
          protection policies in order to implement effective
          changes and be able to handle changes in other
          countries to which data might be outsourced.

### - Consumer Rights
    - The new GDPR laws were created to protect the rights of
      consumers.
    - To comply with GDPR, a publisher must:
        - efficiently supply information about personal data if
          requested by policymakers or individual consumers
        - Have a system in place to allow consumers to easily
          update their own personal information
        - Delete personal data when it is no longer being used or
          needed for the purposes originally stated
        - delete personal data immediately on request and also to
          have the ability to immediately cease processing data
          if asked.
        - quickly deliver data on request within a stipulated
          time frame to a person or third party
        - Stop automated decision-making or profiling when it has
          been objected to by a consumer
        - Report breaches involving personal data to the local
          authority and to the person affected within 72 hours
    - It is important you are aware of this information, because
      you are likely to use or come across personal data at
      various points in your editing work.

### - Value Exchange
    - As a copy-editor, you will also potentially be editing
      marketing materials, mailing lists and lists of data as
      references.
    - Therefore you will sometimes be a conduit for the
      publisher, who needs to:
        - Explain to consumers how opting into data sharing will
          improve their experience
        - Make it clear to the consumer what the ‘value exchange’
          of sharing data will hold for them and its benefits
        - Maintain awareness among decision-makers about the
          potential impact of the GDPR guidelines
        - Train staff in the awareness of data protection and the
          potential influences of GDPR on business practices
        - Implement new marketing strategies (you may produce
          ideas and edit these)
        - Inform consumers about GDPR, how it will affect them
          and how the publisher will be complying with the laws
        - Renew, refresh and update published products if
          neccessary

### - Other Considerations
    - There are wide-ranging ramifications of GDPR and
      a publisher should also address the following issues
      (editorial staff should also keep these in mind):
        - If based outside the EU, you should also have at least
          one representative within the EU, if using data there
        - Data you hold should only be transferred outside of the
          EU to countries who offer an equivalent level of
          protection
        - Data protection standards will remain the same for
          UK-based publishers after the UK leaves the EU
        - The Data Protection Bill & EU (Withdrawal) Bill will
          enshrine GDPR into UK law with few changes

    - This module has examined GDPR in detail because it is
      legally and morally esstional to comply with these laws
      Non-compliance could prove very expensive for
      your client and also risk your own job .

    - Fines can be punitive, potentially reaching €20 million or
      four per cent of the company’s annual global sales in some
      cases.

    - If a robust GDPR strategy is not in place, punishment can
      be even harsher if personal data is lost.

    - If in doubt, check the full GDPR legislation:
    - https://gdpr-info.eu/.

### - Contempt of Court
    - Reporting restrictions, restrictions on public access to
      legal hearings and contempt of court are all serious legal
      issues which journalists, editors and publishers must
      consider when covering criminal or civil court cases,
      particularly when proceedings are ongoing.
    - According to the Crown Prosecution Service
      https://www.cps.gov.uk:
        - "contempt of court is established, if unfortunate, name
          given to the species of wrongful conduct which consists
          of interference with the administration of justice."
    - Interference may take many forms which come under the
      headings of criminal and civil contempt. The Contempt of
      Court Act 1981 states that it includes writing and
      publishing anything that:
        - “creates a substantial risk of seriously prejudicing
          ‘active’ criminal proceedings” (proceedings are deemed
          ‘active’ as soon as a suspect is arrested).
    - The contempt of court laws are meant to ensure that people
      get fair trials by preventing juries from being influenced
      by anything other than court evidence.
    - The rules apply to journalists, publishers, TV and radio
      companies as well as the public posting comments on social
      media, and even the jurors themselves.
    - It is also possible to be found in contempt by taking
      photographs or videos, recording court proceedings or
      talking to a jury member while a case is still ongoing, or
      even afterwards (jurors are sworn to secrecy).
    - Ignorance or deliberate transgression of the contempt of
      court law can have serious consequences:
        - if a trial is interfered with, the defendant or
          defendants can walk free and a new trial may have to be
          held, incurring substantial extra costs and time, as
          well as the possibility of further mistrials occurring.
    - Contempt of court can incur a two-year custodial sentence
      in itself, along with the possibility of an unlimited fine.
    - The burden of proof of contempt of court – whether criminal
      or civil – is at the criminal level.
        - If you disrupt a civil trial, it still counts as
          criminal contempt under the law.
    - A recent contempt of court case concerned the far-right
      activist Stephen Yaxley-Lennon (aka ‘Tommy Robinson’) who
      was found to have committed contempt of court outside Leeds
      Crown Court, West Yorkshire, in May 2018.
    - Yaxley-Lennon – who claimed to be a journalist despite
      having no professional training – was found to have
      breached contempt law by breaching reporting restrictions
      outside the court while a trial was in progress.
    - The publication of any information on the trial was
      restricted until after the trial ended to prevent the jury
      being unduly influenced. However Yaxley-Lennon broadcast
      live videos from outside the court and aggressively
      confronted defendants on bail, potentially forcing them
      into non-cooperation.
    - He was initially sentenced to 13 months in jail,
      underlining how seriously the courts take this offence.

### EXERCISES
    - These exercises may be useful in developing your ‘legal
      judgement’ during a copy-editing job.

### - Exercise One
    - Scenario:
        - You are checking the sources of photographs used in
          a book about the landscapes of Finland. It becomes
          apparent that no permission has been sought for these
          images.
        - However, the image files have been sourced from
          a photographer whose work is uploaded to
          https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Main_Page
        - Are these images governed by the principle of fair use?
    - Answer:
        - The best way to proceed is to go to the Wikimedia
          commons website and find the images, either by putting
          them into a Google Image search or searching the
          website itself for similar photos.
        - You can then see exactly how the images need to be
          credited from the website’s terms and conditions, and
          which permissions will be needed to use them.
        - Sometimes it is nothing more than a credit with the
          website address but some images are more restricted in
          their use.

### - Exercise Two
    - Scenario:
        - You are editing a novel for a publisher and the author
          has quoted four lines of song lyrics from a real song
          that two characters are singing along to.
        - Do you need to clear this with the copyright owners of
          the song or can you simply include the lyrics as is?
    - Answer:
        - With an image, you are probably going to use
          a substantial part of, if not all of, the entire work,
          but with song lyrics are only using a small portion.
        - Using the full set of lyrics would certainly violate
          copyright, but a small section is much more nuanced.
        - Many long-standing music publishers will have
          guidelines for such a quandary and you will need to
          contact them to see if you are covered. Some will grant
          permission, rationalising that it might be good
          publicity for the song, but others are less lenient.
        - In this case, ‘fair use’ may or may not cover the four
          lines as there is _no legal limit set for a word
          count_.
        - Probably you should contact the music publisher and
          agree on the use in writing or come to a mutually
          agreeable licensing arrangement.

### - Exercise Three
    - Scenario:
        - An important court case is taking place where a gang of
          armed robbers is being tried at the Old Bailey and
          reporting restrictions are in place.
        - A reporter has turned in copy that was partly sourced
          by accosting people entering the court building and
          posing loaded questions. Should you publish before the
          court case has finished?
    - Answer:
        - Absolutely not!
        - The reporter has potentially committed a criminal
          offence and the article should not be edited and
          published. (Consult as well with the legal team.)
        - Check to see if the journalist was acting under
          instructions from editorial staff and advise against
          publication. Record your concerns in writing so that
          there is a record of them.
