# Proofreading On-Screen

### Introduction
    - It’s becoming more common for proofreaders to mark-up
      documents and text directly on screen.
    - Unfortunately, on-screen proofreading has pitfalls that
      need to be avoided.
    - The screen resolution is significantly less than the
      printed page and makes it more difficult to spot subtle
      details or mistakes.
    - It’s much easier to let your concentration wander when
      reading from a screen. The simple act of scrolling a page
      etc can limit your attention span.

### Methods & techniques for proofreading on-screen

### - Setting yourself up
    - Make sure you are using a well-lit workstation, following
      ergonomic guidelines
    - Give your eyes a rest – they are the chief asset of
      a proofreader!
    - You can avoid too much eye strain by working in larger
      fonts, turning down the backlight on your screen, changing
      the angle or position of the screen to eliminate glare and
      making a more comfortable choice between contact lenses or
      prescription glasses if needed.
    - Get up and walk around at least once an hour to give your
      body, eyes and brain a little exercise or rest. This is not
      only better for your general health but you will make
      considerably more errors if tired.
    - Humans are more productive overall when they take breaks;
      perhaps use your phone timer to ensure breaks.

### - How to adapt the ‘straight edge’ trick to Word documents
    - It is often helpful to use the edge of a piece of paper to
      allow yourself to concentrate on one line of text as you
      work through a document.
    - This can still be done if you’re working on-screen, either
      with a piece of blank paper or ruler.
    - However, using a ruler to read an electronic document on
      a computer monitor can be inconvenient or awkward.

    - Ways to edit or proofread an on-screen document
    - Begin by using your mouse to set your cursor to the
      beginning of a line and move it down until you reach the
      end of the section of the text, thus highlighting the whole
      area of copy you are working on.
    - This can help you keep track of where you are and help you
      to concentrate on reading slowly – but remember to scroll
      down regularly to ensure your eyes are not constantly
      straining at the bottom of the document.

### - Don’t be too reliant on spellchecking
    - Like ‘Track Changes’, ‘Spell Check’ can be a fantastic
      tool. However, only the proofreader will know the correct
      spelling in the context of a particular sentence. You must
      also ensure the corrections made by spell checking are also
      correct.
    - Never believe that spell check negates the deployment of
      careful reading: homonyms have a way of sneaking into
      documents. Your spell checker will allow words like ‘too’
      in place of ‘two’ or ‘to’ etc...

### - Proofreading on a PDF document
    - Nowadays, some clients seek on-screen PDF proofreading
      (though most professional publishers will use _InDesign_
      and do their proofing and editing on paper before inputting
      corrections).
    - Marking up corrections directly on PDF documents is
      a cost-effective way for publishers to save on postage and
      printing costs.
    - It also means that your rate of payment is better, as most
      work is on a fixed fee and on-screen marking up is quicker.
    - PDFs can be marked up using comment boxes, sticky notes and
      annotations.
    - If your client prefers mark-up using built-in tools,
      remember that these are not the best way to do it and
      perhaps suggest _PDF stamps_, which make comments and
      symbols more obvious and can be added more quickly.

### - How to check for common errors
    - The following is a list of tried and trusted strategies to
      help concentration:

    - Once your proofreading is complete, re-read the text
      thoroughly – it’s always best to print it off for this.

    - Verb tense
        - Check the whole text to ensure the tenses are
          consistent throughout. Make sure the writing doesn’t
          start in the past tense and then switch randomly to the
          present.

    - Parallel structures
        - Skim the copy but stop at keywords that usually
          introduce structures known as ‘parallel structures’,
          such as for ‘not only … but also’, ‘either … or’ and
          ‘neither … nor’
        - Make sure that the various forms of a word connected by
          the parallel structures are in the same grammatical
          form.

    - Fragments
        - Look at each sentence to make sure it contains an
          independent clause, which is a group of words that
          contains a subject and a predicate (that is, a phrase
          such as ‘went home’ in the bigger phrase ‘John went
          home’)
        - Watch sentences beginning with subordinating
          conjunctions such as ‘because’, ‘while’ and ‘since’, or
          phrases such as ‘for example’. These may indicate that
          a sentence should be attached to the previous one.

        - Example
        - Incorrect: “I like to swim. Because I like the warmth
          of the pool.”
        - Correct: “I like to swim because I like the warmth of
          the pool.”

    - Commas connecting two independent clauses
        - Watch when a comma is used in place of a semi-colon or
          other punctuation mark – that is, when they separate
          two causes that are independent of each other.
        - Try skimming your paper for commas placed in complete
          sentences on either side of the comma.
        - If those cases, correct the sentence by either
          converting the punctuation to a full stop or semi-colon
          or by rewriting one of the clauses to be dependent by
          adding a conjunction.

        - Incorrect:
        - “Tony’s mum came to London last week, she really
          enjoyed seeing Buckingham Palace.”
        - Correct:
        - “Tony’s mum came to London last week; she really
          enjoyed seeing Buckingham Palace.”
        - Alternatively:
        - Tony’s mum came to London last week and really enjoyed
          seeing Buckingham Palace.”

        - When a comma separates a subject and verb
            - Firstly, check your copy for commas
            - If there is a comma between the subject and verb,
              consider removing it if the comma isn’t for an
              adjectival clause. Remember not to separate the
              subject and verb with a comma.

            - Incorrect: “Tony’s mum, came to London last week.”
            - Correct: “Tony’s mum came to London last week.”

        - Missing commas in an adjectival or relative clause
            - Check your text for commas
            - See if there is a comma at the beginning of
              a relative or adjectival clause. If this is the
              case, a closing comma is needed to finish the
              clause

            - Incorrect: “Tony’s mum, who came to London last
              week works as a personal assistant.”
            - Correct: “Tony’s mum, who came to London last
              week_,_ works as a personal assistant.”

    - Apostrophes
        - Check your text, stopping only at the words ending in
          the letter ‘s’.
        - Consider whether any of the words beginning with ‘s’
          need an apostrophe
        - If an apostrophe is necessary, you will also be able to
          invert the order of the words to write ‘of’ or ‘of the’
          between them and it will still make sense.
        - Also, check whether the noun to which the ‘s’ belongs
          is singular or plural and place the apostrophe before
          or after the ‘s’ accordingly.
        - Watch for the ‘greengrocer’s apostrophe’ mistake which
          is so often seen on handwritten signs in shops (for
          example: “fresh tomatoe’s” are sold here” rather than
          “fresh tomatoes are sold here”)

        - Incorrect: “Tonys wife is very attractive.”
        - Correct: “Tony’s wife is very attractive.”

    - Left-out or doubled words
        - Reading aloud helps in this situation, especially while
          pointing to every word. It is best not to let your eye
          move ahead till you are sure that you have seen each
          word.

        - Incorrect: “Every day Tony goes to the mall with his
          brother.”
        - Also incorrect: “Every day, Tony goes to the mall with
          his brother every day.”
        - Correct: “Every day, Tony goes to the mall with his
          brother.”

    - Capitalisation
        - Check all proper nouns – that is, the names of people,
          cities and towns, countries, streets and the titles of
          books, songs and films.
        - Are they capitalised? (‘upper case’).
        - Do they need to be capitalised? Remember a few brand
          names and other proper nouns can be exceptions – for
          example, the poet 'e e cummings' or 'iTunes'. It is
          best to avoid starting a sentence with these
          exceptional names, as deciding whether to capitalise
          them is a stylistic nightmare.
        - Ensure capitalisaiton follows the style guide.
        - Also, triple-check that all sentences begin with
          a capital letter and, unless instructed otherwise, all
          acronyms (e.g. RSPB, CIA or TNT) are also rendered in
          upper case without full stops.

        - Incorrect: “Sarah just finished reading an article entitled: ‘How to help Students Learn’.”
        - Correct:   “Sarah just finished reading an article entitled: ‘How to Help Students Learn’.”

    - Quotation marks or inverted commas
        - Using Word ‘find’, locate all inverted commas and
          ensure that every opening quotation mark is paired with
          a closing one.
        - Also, ensure that phrases or sentences inside the
          quotation marks are direct quotes and not paraphrases.
        - Place all full stops and commas _inside_ the pair of
          inverted commas, unless the style guide says otherwise.
        - However, ensure semi-colons and colons are placed
          outside the quotation marks unless you are directly
          quoting from the text that has been extracted from
          already published work.

        - Example
        - Incorrect: Sarah just finished reading the article “How to Help Students Learn”.
        - Correct:   Sarah just finished reading the article “How to Help Students Learn.”

### Useful tips for proofreaders
    - You must have an excellent knowledge of punctuation and
      grammar for professional proofreading.
    - Avoid distractions and ensure you are in a quiet
      environment without disturbances and  avoid social media or
      emails.
    - Take frequent breaks from the job. Set yourself a fixed
      period to work, after first realistically considering how
      long you can read for without losing concentration, and
      then take a break.
    - Remember you aren’t just checking the words and
      punctuation, you are also trying to spot inconsistencies in
      style and formatting.
    - Look out for headings that suddenly switch font size or
      type & notice whether there has been a change in the size
      or style of bullet points.
    - Be methodical by taking one line at a time and trying to
      perform the required tasks by following a _set routine_.
    - Concentrate on reading one word at a time. This takes
      practice. During ‘normal’ reading, we only skim over the
      words in a sentence, fixing our eyes on about four words
      per line and filling in the rest with what we expect to be
      there.
    - We are largely relying on our not very precise peripheral
      vision to register the letters on either side of the centre
      of where we are staring.
    - A conscious effort needs to be made to stare at each word
      in turn and that is the skill of the proofreader.
    - With practice, you can even to learn to read faster than
      average this way, while still noticing mistakes.
    - If you are proofing your own piece of writing, leave
      a reasonable period between finishing your writing and
      checking it for mistakes. You’ll spot far more errors if
      you read it with a fresh approach.
    - Take your proofreading off-screen, if possible.
    - Check, is it possible for a colleague to do a proof-read
      too?

### Proofreading for common errors
    - Fragments
        - Fragments are groups of words written as a sentence
          that doesn’t express a complete thought. A fragment is
          a grammatical non-sequitur and should be expunged.
        - The best way to sift for fragments is to use our
          now-established strategy of reading the words
          _backwards from the end_, sentence by sentence.
        - To check if a sentence is complete and not a fragment,
          ask yourself: “Does this make sense?”
        - Fragments can usually be corrected by connecting them
          to the sentence before or after they occur, sometimes
          by adding a conjunction.

    -  ‘Comma splices’ (Ania!)
        - A good way of finding comma splices is to start at the
          beginning of the text and skim through it, stopping at
          every comma. At each stop, read the part of the
          sentence that precedes the comma to see if it makes
          sense by itself?
        - If so, now read the part of the sentence that follows
          the comma to see if those words make sense on their
          own. If that is the case, then the sentence in question
          almost certainly has a comma splice, a kind of run-on
          sentence which can easily exist on its own and is not
          grammatically correct if kept in a single sentence with
          the other part.
        - A comma splice can be corrected in three different
          ways:
            - Adding a coordinating conjunction such as ‘and’,
              ‘but’ or ‘or’ directly after the comma to connect
              the two phrases or fragments.
            - Replacing the errant comma with a full stop.
            - Replacing the comma with a semi-colon if the two
              clauses are closely related to each other and sound
              convincing together.

    - Run-ons
        - Run-ons are sentences that have been connected while
          expressing two or more complete thoughts or ideas, but
          without having the correct or appropriate punctuation
          or connecting words added.
        - A good way to detect a run-on sentence is to read the
          text out loud. Carefully listen to your voice and when
          it naturally drops and hesitates before continuing to
          read, it is possible that you might have reached the
          end of a complete sentence.
        - Some run-ons can be corrected by inserting a full stop
          or semi-colon to separate two or more complete
          sentences.
        - However, other run-ons are better corrected by
          inserting a comma and a coordinating conjunction.

    - Spelling and typographic errors
        - Never rely on ‘Spell Check’ to detect all spelling
          mistakes, as some will need your human perception to
          decide if correct.
        - As we have seen, one of the most helpful ways of
          detecting spelling mistakes and typos is to read your
          paper backwards from the end to the beginning, word by
          word. However, this can be extremely time-consuming if
          the text is long. Backwards reading is ineffective in
          the case of homophone errors (such as the interchange
          between ‘to’, ‘too’ and ‘two’) or spelling errors of
          near-identical words such as ‘quit’, ‘quite’ or
          ‘quiet’, which are easily typed as one of their
          incorrect alternatives. Spell check won’t catch those
          mistakes either.
        - The best way to catching spelling errors is to become
          familiar with the writer’s error pattern. It can help
          to keep a list of words the author misspells in
          alphabetical order to use as a reference.

### Proofreading for STYLISTIC Problems
    - The passive voice
        - While the use of the passive voice is sometimes
          expected, if the style guide don’t indicate that you
          should use it, then the active voice is better. This
          will generally make your writing more engaging for the
          reader.
        - In a sentence which uses the active voice, the
          performer (the ‘doer’) of an action is the subject of
          the sentence.
        - Example
        - “The essay was written by the student.”
        - Here, ‘was’ is the perfect tense of the verb ‘to be’
          and ‘written’ is a past participle. Ask yourself who
          wrote the essay? “The student wrote the essay.”

    - Nominalisation
        - Nominalisation means the author used a word in its noun
          form when its verb form would make the sentence more
          interesting or concise.
        - Two ways to eliminate this problem.
        - Method 1:
            - Find every occurance of the preposition ‘of’. 
            - Check the word immediately preceeding ‘of’ – it
              will certainly be a noun. If the noun has a verb
              form, then rewrite using the verb form.
            - Incorrect: 
                - “The teacher wrote an explanation of the
                  passive voice.”
            - Correct (rewritten): 
                - “The teacher explained the passive voice.”
        - Method 2:
            - Check for the verbs ‘to have’, ‘to make’ and ‘to
              be’ in their various declensions. 
            - If the nouns that follow have verb forms, then
              replace with the verb form.
            - Incorrect: 
                - “Tony _made a suggestion_ we start at 3 pm.”
            - Correct (rewritten): 
                - “Tony _suggested_ we start at 3 pm”.

    - Removing expletives
        - In grammar, _expletives_ aren’t rude or obscene words,
          but inessential words added to a sentence to fill space
          or add to its rhythm. An example would be in the
          sentence “obviously, this isn’t true”, where the word
          ‘obviously’ is unnecessary – because if the falsehood
          is untrue, it is already obvious without stating that
          fact.
        - ‘There’ is often such an expletive when it begins
          a sentence. Use the ‘find’ function to locate sentences
          that begin with the phrases ‘There are’, ‘There is,’
          and ‘There were' . Rewrite them putting the subject
          where it belongs: in the subject position.
        - Incorrect (awkward): “There are many people who believe
          we shouldn’t go to war.”
        - Correct (rewritten): “Many people believe we shouldn’t
          go to war.”

### Marking up Word documents
    - The best way to proof a document is to print it off.
      However many clients prefer on-screen proofreading.
    - The simplest method in MS Word is to highlight errors in
      red by switching the font colour after highlighting the
      letter, word or section in which the mistake was located.
      However this is very inefficient.
    - Instead of switching font colours to create a digital ‘red
      pen’, use the ‘Track Changes’ function in the ‘Review’ tab.
      This records any changes made during proofreading and also
      enable comments.
    - ‘Track Changes’ highlights all your edits, making it
      unnecessary for the editor to reset font colours and can
      automatically take care of some proofreading marks, such as
      deletion strike-throughs.
    - You can also add appropriate symbols into your ‘Review’
      notes by going to the ‘Insert’ tab and opening the
      ‘Advanced Symbol’ window.

    - How to use ‘Comments’ and ‘Track Changes’
        - Microsoft Word supports two types of proofreading tool
          on its ‘Review’ tab which work in tandem.
        - To mark precise areas of text (or even letters) for
          removal or to make an insertion, turn on ‘Track
          Changes’. This will underline and make every change you
          add to the document appear in red. Be careful though:
          this will include _stets_.
        - A deletion won’t remove any text, however, but will
          mark the characters with a strikethrough. This allows
          the author to accept or reject the correction.

    - Insert less common proofreading symbols
        - You can also use ‘Track Changes’ or ‘Comment’ to insert
          common proofreading symbols such as insertion carets
          (aka ‘insertion marks’).
        - To do this, you may need to know the symbol’s Unicode
          number.
        - To add a symbol not found on a standard keyboard, such
          as the paragraph or Pilcrow mark, open the ‘Insert’
          tab, click on ‘Advanced Symbol’ and choose a font. Now
          find the mark you need and click on Insert.
        - If the symbol isn’t on this tab but you already know
          the symbol’s Unicode number – such as ‘00B6’ for the
          ‘start new paragraph’ or Pilcrow mark – then type the
          number directly into the document or comment and press
          `Alt-X` simultaneously on your keyboard.

### Marking up PDFs
    - Many proofreaders hardly ever use paper. Manuscripts are
      mainly written in Word and after the lay-out and editorial
      stage, most page proofs will be presented in PDF form.
    - Corrections can be made directly into `InDesign` or
      `QuarkXPress`, but these will mainly be done by editorial
      staff after you have marked up a Word document or PDF with
      your corrections.
    - Mark-ups are usually done in Adobe Acrobat. Acrobat Reader
      is available free and contains all the tools proofreaders
      need to insert corrections into page proofs.

### - PDF Software
    - Most PDF software can perform the following tasks:
        - text strike-through, insertion, deletion and
          underlining
        - simple shapes and lines
        - Highlight text and comment on it
        - Attach ‘sticky’ notes
        - Attach ‘pin’ files to the document
        - Add comments/suggestions for the editor or designer
        - Format typed text eg. font, size, alignment and colour
          etc etc

    - More advanced options:
        - _PDF Stamps_: These are official digital proofreading
          marks from the BS 5261C standard and are available as
          online palettes. Eg:
            - $MY_IMAGES/misc/editing/stamps-palette.jpg
        - 'Futureproofs': This platform should be paid for by
          your client if you are to use it
        - 'PDF-XChange': An excellent alternative to Acrobat Pro.
        - Acrobat Professional: Well-trusted but expensive.
        - Adobe Reader: Free with has functionality needed for
          a proofreader.

### - PDF marking: Potential pitfalls

### - Ensure you know what your client wants
    - You should ask your client what their preferences are
      rather than making assumptions.
    - Some publishers will have streamlined their production
      stages and will have a strict set of guidelines concerning
      which annotation tools should be used for digital
      proofreading. These are sometimes specified in the style
      guide.
    - Some will want you to use digital stamps based on the
      standard publishing-industry mark-up symbols, but other
      clients may insist on a particular PDF editor’s onboard
      mark-up tools.
    - It is also possible that some will want you to edit the
      PDF, rather than just mark-up and comment on it, though
      this is rare due to its inherent layout risks; also, you
      should charge more if you are expected to perform what is
      essentially an edit.

### - Test the platform
    - Once the basic setup is established, you should always _do
      a small test_ to check that the client can see and
      understand the same annotations and that your mark-up
      remains in place during the delivery stage; delivery
      methods can sometimes wipe or change such insertions.
    - An example of the process:
        - You are instructed to use the PDF-XChange stamping
          tool.
        - Therefore, extract a single page from the PDF proofs
          and annotate it using several varying stamps and the
          onboard commenting tool, as well as using
          strike-through and the polygonal and highlighting tools
        - Save the page and re-open it in Adobe Reader to check
          that it has been saved properly
        - If everything is ok, compile a list in Word of all the
          test changes you’ve made, giving line and paragraph
          locations where they should occur
        - Only then, do deliver the list and marked-up PDF page
          to the client
        - Await confirmation that the mark-up appears correctly
          on the client’s software and tallies with the record
          sheet

### - Problems with the file size
    - In the case of magazines, books and conference summaries,
      the PDFs can be massive and only increase radically in size
      after proofreading.
    - You will need to check that your email provider can handle
      very large files and, if not, you will have to agree on an
      alternative delivery system with your client.

### - Making sure your software is up to date
    - This is essential, as most client’s and companies’ software
      will be automatically updated, and your work will be sent
      in the most recently upgraded form of the programs you have
      agreed to use.
    - Make sure that you always install the regular updates and
      upgrades for the latest versions of all software you are
      likely to use. This can help to reduce the risk of
      incompatibility and readability issues between your
      software and that of the client.

### - Why use Adobe Acrobat?
    - Your first step is to learn the traditional UK
      proofreader’s marks (see Module 4). The `Annotations` tool
      in the most up-to-date version of Acrobat Reader XI mostly
      mimics these marks.
    - Secondly, make the ‘Comment’ pane accessible in the
      document while you work by clicking the ‘Comment’ in the
      upper right-hand corner of the screen.
    - Customising your toolbars: right-click a grey area on the
      toolbar and select ‘More Tools’ to open a list of all the
      options and check those tools that you want to be shown.
      Note: toolbar customisation is not available in the free
      version of Reader.

### - Three tools to help your PDF mark-ups
    - A common problem when marking up PDFs is the difficulty in
      producing traditional proofreader’s marks by using
      a `mouse`.
    - It can be done with plenty of practice, but there are a few
      convenient alternatives available for you to use to get to
      this stage more rapidly.
    - The three most useful alternatives are:
        - A stylus
        - Annotation tools
        - PDF Stamps

### -- A stylus
    - If you have a touchscreen device such as a tablet, this
      useful pen-shaped tool you can use to ‘draw’ or input
      commands via the screen, will enable you to ‘e-write’ in
      a quick and natural way.
    - Unfortunately, not only do you have to have the right
      device but there is a cost involved (though many
      proofreaders and copyeditors swear by them) – expect to pay
      somewhere between £50 and £400.
    - A stylus is the most ergonomic choice.

### -- Annotation tools
    - These are specific to PDFs but work a lot like the ‘Track
      Changes’ function in Word. They are referred to in Acrobat
      as ‘Text Edit’ tools and help you to perform electronic
      mark-ups by mimicking the symbols that proofreaders and
      editors write in by hand.
    - ‘Text Edit Tools’ can be accessed by either the menu or
      toolbar and makes it simple to highlight the text to be
      changed and either delete or type over it.
    - The changes and original will be preserved and the
      resulting blue box containing the changes will be tagged
      with your initials or name, just like in ‘Track Changes’ in
      Word.

### -- PDF Stamps
    - These can be used to place ready-made proofreader’s marks
      on your document wherever they are needed, including
      ‘insert’ and ‘delete’ and an array of BS-standard margin
      annotations.
    - You can create original marks for stamps at:
    - helpx.adobe.com/support/acrobat.html
    - or download comprehensive sets from ‘the celery’ at
    - http://www.copyediting-l.info/
        - click on the ‘Resources’ tab then look under the
          ‘Miscellaneous’ heading for Diana Stirling’s zip file
          of red and black editing marks.
    - Custom stamps can be created in jpegs and in Photoshop and
      Illustrator, and while it can take some time to create all
      the marks you need, it will become quicker to insert them
      once you’ve got into your stride and have a large number
      saved.
    - You can also find a set of UK-standard stamp marks at:
      https://www.louiseharnbyproofreader.com/

### Alternative mark-up techniques for PDFs
    - In addition to the above methods, `drawing tools` located
      on the Acrobat ‘Comments and Mark-up’ toolbar can be used.
      These include rectangle, oval and polygon shapes and the
      pencil icon.

### - Acrobat’s drawing tools
    - If you aren’t using PDF Stamps to place your proofreading
      marks on the page, then you should perhaps try using the
      Adobe Acrobat drawing tools.
    - The pencil tool can be used to put carets (that is,
      insertion marks) and ‘delete’ marks in place.
    - You can even assign various colours to each proofreader or
      editor who will be making those correction marks – maybe
      try blue for your changes, orange for the copy-editors and
      green for the editor’s;
    - By changing the user settings, you can even append each
      person’s name to the marks when they are drawn. When the
      marks are being placed on a coloured background, you can
      also change the properties to make the marks visible on
      that background.

### - Typing in the text boxes
    - Dont use drawing tools for written instructions. They will
      be too big. If you want to make sure your notes are clear
      and stand out, then type them into a `text box`.
    - Try not to use ‘Comment’ boxes, as these are the bane of
      editorial departments because they can hide behind other
      pieces of furniture on the page.
    - The text box method is clean and straightforward, and the
      editorial team or editor can copy text from it.
    - For longer inserts, some editors prefer the more
      traditional method of attaching an entire Word document
      containing the required text. It is possible to attach
      a Word document to Acrobat and pin it into place, but not
      all members of the editorial team will know where the
      document.

### - Changing the properties of an item
    - You can change any of the properties of any element you
      have added to your PDF by selecting it with your cursor and
      then selecting ‘Properties’.
    - To set an item's default properties, there are 2 options.
        - Select the tool and change its properties before use
        - or change the properties by right-clicking and
          selecting the `Make current properties default`.
    - Changing the default properties of a tool can be very
      useful when you want to identify previous editorial marks
      by colour so that you don’t accidentally try to undo them
      when making your edits.
    - Changed default settings will only be saved when you exit
      the program; otherwise, they’re merely temporary
      adjustments during your editing session.

### - Making a list of your mark-ups
    - Acrobat can provide a neat list of every single mark and
      comment you make in a text document. It can even mark the
      PDF up with number tags that will correlate to that mark-up
      summary.
    - It is common for an editorial department to use this list
      as a checklist of changes to be implemented.
    - To do this, look for the `Summarise comments` options in
      the ‘Comments’ drop-down menu. In that menu, you will see
      that there are several options for producing a checklist of
      changes.
