### - Introduction
    - All writing is based in the real world to some extent.
    - Even in science fiction stories, we can still compare
      descriptions in the story with our perceptions of the
      world. Blue leaves on a tree may seem strange but that is
      only because we know them to be green, brown or yellow.
    - Because of this, there isn’t really any form of writing
      which doesn’t need some polishing up with regard to
      accuracy in its meaning or use of facts.
    - Copy-editing involves a fair amount of fact-checking
      because even though a sub-editor can also do this, they
      won’t have spotted everything.
    - And if you’re the only editor on a project, you’ll have the
      responsibility for doing it all.
    - This module equip you with the skills to spot factual and
      accuracy errors and correct them before passing the work
      onto a proofreader.
    - Module topics:
        - Analysing words and phrases for accurate meaning
        - Identifying and correcting factual errors

### ANALYSING WORDS AND PHRASES FOR ACCURATE MEANING

### - Fact-checking
    - Fact-checking is an essential part of the production
      process and ideally should occur before copy-editing .
    - This is because, if an error is discovered, the author may
      need to re-write some sections which must be checked by the
      structural editor, delaying the process if it has been
      missed in the first stage of editing.
    - Some publishers use specialist fact-checkers, but usually
      a copy-editor identifies major errors after the basic edit.
    - Check with the writer, project manager or chief editor on
      the appropriate next steps and ensure you this in writing.
    - Never leave yourself open to legal action over a libellous
      comment or factual error.
    - Fact-checking starts with the author. However, each fact
      should be checked against two sources. Names are one aspect
      that should be carefully checked (for mispellings).

### - What you need to check
    - All factual elements of a text must be checked. This
      includes:
        - Names (of people and organisations)
        - Job titles and ranks
        - Dates (historical or future)
        - Addresses and phone numbers
        - URLs and email addresses
        - Sums of money
        - Direction details
        - Geographical locations
        - General instructions
        - Laws, regulations and rules

### - How to check facts
    - The best way of fact checking is to use the information in
      the text yourself: follow directions, check URLs, call
      phone numbers, look up dates, call sources to check their
      names, ranks and job titles – in other words, go directly
      to the source of these facts.
    - You must religiously query any discrepancies and flag up
      anything that seems debatable.
    - Potential legal issues and libellous material need care.
    - Anything that might leave you, the publisher or client open
      to legal action should be referred to a lawyer.
    - Leave a query or comment in the margin and also mention the
      issue when sending the edited document off to the client.
    - It is the editor’s responsibility to flag items and suggest
      changes.
    - However, ultimately the author and publisher is responsible
      for ensuring the final product is free of legal risk.

### - Know how much fact-checking is expected
    - A copy-editor should always be conscious of what
      publication stakeholders are expecting, as well as the core
      readership demands. These factors will determine how
      detailed the fact-checking should be.
    - E.g., The satirical magazine Private Eye is careful not to
      unfairly represent real people. When its American
      equivalent, The Onion, published an image of a fake
      headstone listing victims of violence, the editorial team
      ensured none of the names listed were actual victims of
      violence.
    - Also, many newspapers and magazines pride themselves as
      authoritative sources of history and fact, and these
      reputations cannot be maintained without rigorous
      fact-checking.
    - However, many publishing outlets have cut their editorial
      staff in recent years and the factual robustness of their
      articles has declined as a result.

### - Triaging’ the facts
    - However, with a looming deadline or limited budget,
      copy-editors won’t always have time to check every fact
      rigorously. Consequently, the ability to triage is needed.
    - This will involve the art of compromise and expediency.
      A copy-editor needs to prioritise issues that may be most
      damaging to the text. In reality, this means fixing issues
      of meaning and clarity over marking-up every Oxford comma.
    - You must also prioritise those facts that, if proven
      incorrect, could spell legal trouble for the author,
      publisher etc. Also prioritise facts that could potentially
      embarrass the publication or are crucial to the article.
    - e.g. you may be profiling a company 'IT Gagets' where
      a 'Joanna Smith' is listed as a deparment director. You may
      be unable to find the precise job title is. The triaged
      compromise would be to credit her as “Joanna Smith,
      a director of IT Gagets” rather than digging further, (if
      not essential to the story).

### - ensure a quote is a quote
    - It is always necessary both the content of the quote and
      who the quote is attributed to.
    - The author is ultimately responsible for the text, but
      a copy-editor should support authors by verifying any quote
      used.
    - Check at the beginning of a project if this support is
      needed, as it can be very time-intensive.

### - Sharpen your detection skills
    - Certain media outlets may display political bias, but
      others may not be so readily pinpointed. A certain
      editorial position will be taken by nearly all
      publications.
    - To stay away of biases and trends in the publishing market,
      try to stay well-informed and read a wide variety of
      material.
    - You must develop the ability to identify which sources are
      the most reliable for particular subjects. Also, check
      facts from a variety of sources to ensure better accuracy.
    - As social media is prevalently used today, you will need to
      triple-check any ‘facts’ drawn from those sources.
    - If you are editing stories about content posted on social
      media, retrieve the original posts to check them. Also take
      screenshots of these posts.

### - Watch or listen to audio or video sources
    - If a piece of writing refers to a particular audio or
      visual broadcast, do not spend excessive time tracking down
      a transcript. Simply watch the quoted source instead.
    - An often-quoted piece of advice for journalists is worth
      repeating:
        - “If someone says it’s raining and another person says
          it’s dry, it’s not your job to quote them both. Your
          job is to look out the window and find out which is
          true.”

### - Begin with what you already know
    - Some facts may be difficult to check. If your search for
      sources reaches an impasse, use your own basic knowledge
      and skills to verify it.
    - E.g.:
    - A manuscript states that 10 per cent of survey respondents
      were more than satisfied with their new smartphones.
    - However, you don’t have access to the original survey
      results and methods to fact-check.
    - Check the survey company’s website for the relevant data
    - If none found, use Google search to identify other reports
      on the survey
    - If still unsuccessful, contact the company directly
    - If the staff are unable to help, search the company's
      website for ‘cellphone satisfaction’ etc
