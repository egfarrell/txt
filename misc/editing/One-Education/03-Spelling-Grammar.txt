### Introduction
    - Your target as a proofreader is to spot all potential
      errors in the text and to maintain consistency throughout.
    - The most important part of this _complex and nuanced_ job
      is to ensure that spelling and grammar – usually under the
      aegis of the relevant style guide – is rendered perfectly
      and consistently.
    - You will need to be a voracious and critical reader, with
      good written skills and a solid grounding in general
      knowledge.
    - You will need to demonstrate these basic skills with
      evidence:
        - published writing or work experience,
        - robust references and links to online examples,
        - a blog or online publication

    - Above all, your job will involve the constant and
      unwavering checking of style, spelling and grammar.

    - This module will concentrate on this essential
      proofreader’s skill: the checking and correction of style,
      spelling and grammar.

    - Topics:
        - How to read proofs
        - Reliable spelling and grammar references
        - Spotting and correcting errors

### How to read proofs
    - We have already explored the fundamental basics of
      proofreading and what it entails. It is usually done after
      the bulk of the editing, but before publication.
    - It can be done manually or with the use of various online
      tools. However, though online proofreading programs can be
      fast and accurate, it is usually only the hand and eye of
      a real person that can spot the very subtle mistakes in
      meaning and style that make all the difference to reader
      enjoyment.
    - Some simple and effective proofreading tips follow, which
      can help you identify and correct errors in an efficient
      way.
    - You will always need to go through the entire text _several
      times_ but these tips will minimise the time spent.

### -- Punctuation
    - Paying attention to punctuation involves more than merely
      looking for errors; you will need to check that the phrases
      used are correctly connected to each other and consider if
      they make sense to the reader. If they don’t make sense to
      you, they won’t to anyone else!
    - A misplaced or omitted comma can change the whole meaning
      of a phrase. Remember the cliché about pandas eating shoots
      and leaving!
    - Another quick tip is to make sure that _exclamation marks_
      are not overused. The one in the paragraph above should be
      fine, but the one following this phrase is extraneous!
    - Start a proofreading session _refreshed_.
    - Once you have been given some copy or a text to work on,
      try to take a break before you start, then read it through
      without making anything other than mental corrections.
        - This will help you grasp the whole piece and its ‘feel’
          before you start to adjust the minutiae of its content.
    - After reading it through, take a small break, and you will
      find it much easier to identify the mistakes.
    - Use online tools
    - In today's environment you will be able to find innumerable
      free online tools that can error-check your text.
    - However, no matter how lightning quick and robotically
      efficient they are, you still need to check using your own
      sober and jaundiced eye.
    - Online spellcheckers can easily highlight repetition in
      words and phrasing, and they will usually let you know if
      the writer has missed out or transposed letters. They won’t
      be able to help you spot _transposed letters_ which create
      other valid words, though.
    - Online proofreading tools are a shortcut but far from the
      whole journey – they haven’t made proofreaders redundant
      just yet!

### -- Read the text out loud
    - Even if you feel self-conscious, reading out the text  is
      a really good way to find mistakes.
    - Even phrases that look alright on the page can sound clunky
      or ‘not right’ when read out.
    - Perhaps the writer didn't use the most appropriate verb or
      missed a word out.
    - Reading your text out loud (under your breath), is the best
      way to notice mistakes and correct them immediately, as you
      will have to read every single word; this will notably
      increase the likelihood of you finding a typo when the
      ‘blindness’ and familiarity of repetitive reading may fail
      to spot mistakes.

### -- Print the copy before reading
    - A good tip if you spend a lot of your time working in front
      of a computer is that your eyes will sooner or later become
      tired and you will miss spotting more typos and grammatical
      and spelling errors.
    - The best way to circumvent this problem is to print out
      your work and proofread from the paper version. You can
      still carefully make the corrections on-screen after you’ve
      been through the text.
    - The best way to do this is to use a pencil or red pen to
      highlight mistakes on the paper proof, using the standard
      proofreading symbols (see Module 4). Then move onto the
      laptop or PC to properly correct them.

### -- Split the copy up into sections
    - If the text you are proofreading is too long or has a lot
      of numbers, headings and bullet points, try to break it up
      into sections and correct one at a time.
    - Eg, you could correct the introduction, the main body under
      different headings or _crossheads_ and any conclusion or
      final paragraphs separately.
    - When finished, you can correct the crossheads for accuracy
      and relevancy. Leave figures, numbers and bullet points
      last and initially focus on the bulk of the text.

### -- Pay attention to detail
    - It's the details that make a difference when you proofread.
    - The task will not only involve checking the text but also
      mean that you have to check the credits of any _pictures_
      used, the details of any _references_, any _table headings_
      and _l_ists, as well as any other formatted writing.
    - It is particularly important to check for errors in the
      numbers and symbols.

### -- Take your time
    - Patience is a virtue when proofreading, even if you are
      busy and you cannot afford to procrastinate or waste time.
    - Read the copy carefully the first time because it is a lot
      more effective to do this than go through the complete text
      twice.
    - Read every word, sentence and paragraph carefully.
      - `Slowly does it`.
    - Pay close attention to names (proper nouns) and numbers.
    - _Names and numbers_ are factually important in both fiction
      and non-fiction, and you must spell and use them correctly.
      These are things which online spellcheckers can repeatedly
      miss. Pay even closer attention to foreign names
      (especially those accents!) and double-check all numbers,
      particularly the longer ones.

### Reliable spelling and grammar references
    - Besides style guides, the best reference to ensure 100%
      accurate spelling is a standard dictionary.
    - This will also make sure that you are using the correct
      word in the correct context.
    - The first reliable English dictionary was published by
      Samuel Johnson in 1755, but by far the most referred to and
      comprehensive is the `Oxford English Dictionary`, which has
      been the standard since 1884.
    - This has been abridged as the `Shorter Oxford English
      Dictionary`, which is usually enough for most people’s
      purposes. But if you encounter more arcane usage, then fork
      out for the full-length version, which goes much deeper
      into historical words and definitions.
    - If you get work in `US English`, the best dictionary is
      `Webster’s Third New International Dictionary`.
    - Grammar is in many ways a more complex issue than spelling
      (which is now fairly set in stone in UK English).

    - Key References are:
        - Longman Grammar of Spoken and Written English    1999 Biber, Conrad, Johansson and Leech
        - A Grammar of Speech                              1995 Brazil
        - Oxford Practice Grammar Advanced                 xxxx Eastwood
        - An Introduction to Functional Grammar (3rd ed)   2004 Halliday and Matthiensen (eds)
        - Grammar and Context                              2004 Hewings and Hewings
        - Oxford A-Z of Grammar and Punctuation            2013 Seely
        - Collins Cobuild English Grammar                  1990 Sinclair

    - For American English:
        - McGraw-Hill Handbook English Grammar & Usage (2e) 2012 Lester and Beaston
        - English Grammar in Use                            2012 Murphy
        - Blue Book of Grammar and Punctuation (11th ed)    2014 Straus, Kaufman and Stern

    - You can also use _software_ provided by grammar websites
      but remember: they won’t catch everything and you must
      check the text painstakingly and carefully by eye before or
      after you use the software – don’t just make changes
      because the program told you too!

    - Ensure the program is set to either UK or US English according
      to the style you’ve been instructed to use.

### Spotting and correcting ERRORS

- This important task is the final challenge before publication.

- In this spirit of responsibility, special care must be taken
  and every letter and word must be checked for accuracy, sense,
  style and correctness.

- The best way to do this is to have a routine or _checklist_ of
  tasks as you go through the proofreading process.

- The copy will already have been read by its author and an
  editor, but this doesn’t mean that there will be few mistakes
  left. Take as much care as possible.

### -- Proofreading Stages

    - Print a copy out
        - Even if you are proofing online text, you are much more
          likely to catch errors when reading a printed version
          of the copy. This is because you can often see less of
          the body of the text on-screen and you will tend to
          focus more on a smaller amount of copy while looking at
          it. Also, the screen light can be somewhat dazzling,
          even if you don’t normally notice this.

    - Change how the text looks
        - Another tip when reading on-screen is to change the
          font, style, size and type. As in every other aspect of
          proofreading, you must keep this consistent in the
          finished work and often the final font will be
          specified in the style sheet, whether the work is for
          the internet or a printed publication

    - Work on one section at a time
        - Long documents can be quite exhausting to proofread,
          and you will begin to tire during long expanses of
          letters and words. To avoid this as much as possible,
          break the copy down into logical sections and work on
          each one on its own in consecutive order. This method
          will make what might seem like a completely
          overwhelming task into a much more manageable one.
          Again, this will help you to catch most errors

    - Be patient
        - Read every word, phrase and sentence slowly and
          carefully. Lots of proofreaders will tap or point to
          each word with a pencil or trace each line as they are
          reading it

    - Check for spelling errors first
        - Most desktop publishing and writing programs on
          computers have built-in spell-checkers and this is your
          first line of defence against retaining errors. You
          should make sure that the spell checker is set to the
          correct form of English (generally the UK or the US)
          and that all then other relevant option boxes are
          ticked, too

    - Check for punctuation
        - Next, look for punctuation mistakes, particularly
          regarding issues like the incorrect usage of semicolons
          and colons. Some proofreaders like to identify
          punctuation errors by picking out every single
          punctuation mark with a pencil or red pen – this method
          is painstaking but can be very effective

    - Check for missing words
        - Words that have been omitted are very easy to not
          notice because our brains are very good at
          automatically filling in the gaps as we read to create
          meaning – and people very often read what they are
          expecting to see

    - Proofread for only one type of error at a time
        - Assuming the piece of writing isn’t too long, it
          sometimes helps a proofreader to focus on the task at
          hand by checking for single kinds of mistake on each
          run through. For example, do one run through for
          transposed letters (such as ‘from’ instead of ‘form’)
          and another to decide upon semi-colons or n-dashes

    - Identify the weaknesses in the text
        - Every writer has personal foibles such as a tendency to
          overpopulate their writing with semi-colons or
          exclamation marks or using double quotation marks when
          style indicates that they should be single. The
          subject-verb agreement can cause many writers a lot of
          trouble, so make a point of checking for those. Not all
          errors are recurring, but a good number of them will be
          and you can eradicate these quickly by checking for
          them specifically once you have spotted them

    - Try reading aloud
        - Try slowing down and really paying attention to a piece
          of writing by reading aloud (this can be stridently or
          under your breath, depending on the situation and how
          shy you are). You might still read aloud what you’re
          expecting to see, so it is a good idea, if you get the
          chance to ask a colleague or other person to read the
          text aloud for you or, failing that, you could even try
          to use text-to-voice software, though this also has its
          drawbacks in terms of inadvertent errors

    - Read the text backwards
        - Another proven proofreading technique is to start at
          the end of your text and read your text backwards,
          beginning at the very end and progressing sentence by
          sentence. This can be awkward for some and does sound
          a little unusual, but it can be very effective because
          it forces you to focus on every word individually

    - Have a colleague check the text
        - If you are working in an office or with an editorial
          team, you can be at an advantage in that it is good to
          have more than one pair of eyes looking over the work
          and they will almost certainly be able to do this – in
          fact, in smaller teams, they may be obliged to. This
          isn’t always possible to do in all work situations,
          particularly if you work on your own from home, but it
          is sometimes possible to have a reciprocal arrangement
          with another proofreader

    - Check your work with proofreading software
        - Online proofreading software can be a very useful tool
          for picking up many of the issues that can arise in
          a text, as well as even suggesting ways to improve it.
          However, it will still need a real pair of eyes to do
          a final perfect check – yours!

    - It is important to remember that, no matter how
      carefully a document is proofread, something will always
      slip through the net.

    - Every reader has spotted a typo or two in published work
      that has almost certainly gone through multiple pairs of
      eyes during the many stages of its professional
      proofreading and editing.

    - It is a professional editorial maxim that `you will do your
      best proofreading after you hit ‘send’`.
    - It is your job to try and contradict that saying.

      for errors and how to enhance your checking techniques:

        - Using online proofreading tools
        - Printing the text out
        - Splitting it into easily digestible sections
        - The value of patience
        - Check specifically for problems with numbers & symbols
        - Which dictionaries and grammar references to use
        - The best identification routine to use
