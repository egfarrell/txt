### Introduction
    - When reading a text to spot mistakes, it is better not to
      cover the sheet with handwritten instructions but mark up
      the copy for correction using a shorthand of symbols and
      simple instructions.
    - Not only should the instructions be simple, but they should
      use standardised symbols and abbreviations.
    - In a longer piece of writing, this will help you remember
      the errors you noticed earlier on.
    - Fortunately, proofreaders have already developed
      a standardised set of symbols that are used all over the
      English-speaking world for proofing printed documents.
    - A professional proofreader should know these off by heart
      but keeping a reference sheet handy while you start is also
      a good idea.

### Standard symbols for proofreading on paper
    - The Society for Editors and Proofreaders – based in Putney,
      London – approves a set of symbols produced by the British
      Standards Institute under the code `BS 5261C`. These
      symbols should be used for all proofreading in the UK. They
      are universally understood among editorial staff.
    - Proofreading can be done on paper or electronically on
      scree.
    - British Standard (BS) mark-up on ‘hard copy’ (paper) is
      more rapid and clear, although on-screen BS mark-up (using
      ‘PDF stamps’) is more useful for making source file
      corrections.
    - However proofs are corrected; each mark-up symbol must be
      crystal clear for any editorial operative to understand.

### - Marking up a text on paper
    - Under `BS 5261C` standard symbols, each mark in the text is
      complimented by another mark or abbreviation in the margin.
    - Generally, a proofreader will use a pencil or red pen to
      delete punctuation marks and change letters in mistakes
      resulting from the typographer’s work.
    - They can sometimes alternate to a blue pen for an editorial
      error, such as changing another letter.
    - Colour coding can also result in the proofreader
      incorporating an author’s corrections in, say, black.
    - Some publishers have their own colour codes, but this
      method mentioned is frequent in places where proofs are
      still corrected on paper by hand.

      symbols as specified by `BS 5261C`.

### Methods & techniques of proofreading

### - Proofreading on paper and screen
    - Before starting: Ensure the ‘bigger picture’ aspects of the
      text have been done by the copy-editor.
    - You shouldn’t make corrections at the word and sentence
      level if work is still required on the focus and
      arrangement of the text – this is a copy-editing job.
    - If you spot major errors or incoherence, try to find
      a polite way of querying with the copy-editor or sub-editor
      and get it changed.
    - Check whether any other editing jobs seem incomplete and
      incorporate those into your polite query.
    - Check the electronic document for any _editorial notes_.
    - An editor may leave comments for the proofreader in the
      ‘Track Changes’ mode of a Word document. You should read
      these notes before embarking on changes.

### - What to do when you begin proofreading
    - Work from a print-out rather the computer screen. More
      mistakes tend to be picked up from a printed copy.
    - Scroll through the document in Word to spot red underlining
      that indicate a mistakes.
    - _Read_ the proofs out loud. This is particularly useful for
      noting run-on sentences, but you’ll also spot other
      problems rather than reading silently.
    - Try using a _blank sheet of paper_ to cover the lines below
      the one you’re reading – this will keep you from skipping
      ahead of mistakes in a hurry.
    - Use the _Search_ function in the electronic document to
      locate mistakes everyone makes.
        - e.g. search for `it`, so that you can locate ‘its’ and
          ‘it’s’, which are often absent-mindedly confused and
          can slip past even a very vigilant proofreader.
        - If you habitually use open brackets or quote marks and
          leave out the closing one, then search for a left-hand
          bracket;
        - spellcheckers miss prepositions. Search for `adn`, etc
          too.
    - Try the following order of checks:
        - read through the whole text once (backwards, sentence
          by sentence) to locate ‘fragments’;
        - then read through it again the right way round from the
          beginning to ensure that the subjects and verbs agree;
        - then read it through once more to check pronouns and
          antecedents;
        - lastly go through your established ‘search’ checks
    - Ensure you finish with a full computer spellcheck.
    - Note that a spell-check program won’t catch homonym
      mistakes such as ‘they’re’, ‘their’ or ‘there’ – or typos
      such as ‘he’ for ‘the’.

### Marking up copy clearly and accurately
    - As we have already seen, proofreaders in UK English should
      use BS 5261C, but if you’re working on a US English
      document, you can also use the international standard `ISO
      5776` which is based on that.
    - To ensure clarity and accuracy, take your time. The more
      you rush, the more you will miss errors, defeating the aim
      of your task.
    - This is difficult when you are faced with a deadline, but
      any reasonable employer will realise the importance of you
      doing your job well.
    - Another reason to take your time is to make sure you add
      each standard proofreading symbol.
    - Scrawled handwriting can be confusing. It may be unclear
      which symbol and instruction have been used.
    - Remember, on a prolonged proofing session you may not be
      able to read your handwriting.

### Summary
    - In this module, you have learnt:
        - Proofreading on the printed page in UK English is
          usually done using the British Standard proofreading
          symbols known as ‘BS 5261C’
        - Proofreading in US English should be marked up using
          the international symbols known as ‘ISO 5776’
        - There are several different methods of checking
          a document which you should perform in a logical order
        - All marking up should done clearly and accurately
          – take your time!
