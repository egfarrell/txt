### Introduction

- A piece of writing can have many styles, intents, purposes,
  formats and readers but published writing in any form must
  communicate accurately and with professionalism.

- The best way for any written communication to be understandable
  and get its message across is for it to conform to _high
  standards_ of grammar, spelling and style.

- To ensure the highest possible standards, most professional
  organisations employ proofreaders and copy-editors to
  conscientiously read correct the text to the point of
  perfection.

- This is where you will come in! As a copy-editor or
  proofreader, you will make sure the printed word retains its
  authority, accuracy, information, entertainment value and
  comprehensibility.

- Most readers will judge an author, writer, publisher or piece
  of writing on the quality of its content and will take it less
  seriously if it’s poorly presented, unclear or inconsistent.
  A reader’s time is valuable – make sure they understand you are
  aware of this by making the text accessible in terms of both
  language and presentation, to make the time spent reading it as
  easy as possible.

- Once you have developed the necessary skills for these tasks,
  you stand a good chance of being able to make a living
  correcting and sharpening copy to make sure that all the work
  passing through your hands is of a very high quality.

- Bear in mind a good copy-editor spots and corrects about 80% of
  errors, while a good proofreader will spot around 80% of the
  _remaining mistakes_ in the same text.

- No work will ever be perfect as much depends on the eye of the
  beholder – but you will be ensuring it gets as close to
  a perfect state as possible.

- You'll start by learning the basics of what proofreading
  involves and what a copy-editor does. This course will outline
  the core tasks of a proofreader’s work before going into the
  details and giving tips and ideas on how to perform these tasks
  to the best of your ability. Similar information on
  _copy-editing _will be given from _Module 6_ onwards.

- The major topics to be dealt with in Module 1 are:

    - What a proofreader does
    - A proofreader’s personal qualities
    - Proofreading terminology
    - The publishing process for proofreaders

### WHAT A PROOFREADER DOES

### - What is proofreading?

- Proofreading is the term that describes the job of checking the
  written content and general appearance of text, which is
  intended to be published in the marketplace.

- In other words, spotting errors and mistakes in writing,
  typing, spelling and grammar, and making sure that it is
  presented in a style that meets the requirements of a client or
  acceptable general standards of English (preferably both!).

- Proofreading is a constantly evolving skill that requires time,
  concentration and patience. However, the more you perform the
  work required, the faster and easier it will be for you to
  notice any corrections that might need to be made.

### - Why is proofreading important?

- Proofreading is essential because it completes the final stage
  of the writing process, enabling the reader to consider the
  resulting piece of work as serious and worthy of their valuable
  time and interest.

- After all the effort a writer puts into researching, collating
  and writing work, it should be presented in the manner it
  deserves to show that his or her work is interesting (or,
  hopefully, even definitive).

- Essentially, a well-presented piece of writing indicates to the
  reader that a writer and/or publisher cares about their work
  and has a professional attitude towards it.

### - How much care should you take?

- Proofreading is a time-consuming process and it is easy to
  delude yourself that it can be done in the time it takes to
  read through a piece a couple of times. This is always
  a mistake.

- Therefore, it is a good idea to allocate a generous portion of
  time for any proofreading task. You will be able to estimate
  this more precisely with experience – this course can
  potentially be your first step towards gaining this on-the-job
  experience in advance of working as a full-time proofreader.

- It is particularly important that you follow each module of the
  course in a sequence. As the journey progresses, pay special
  regard to each section to ensure that you concentrate on
  consistency in referencing, formatting, line spacing, paragraph
  indentation and margination, as well as concentrating on the
  level of grammar and spelling needed for each piece on which
  you work.

- Here's a basic checklist for when you are proofreading:

    - Grammar
        - This is incredibly important to convey meaning but,
          despite this, it can come in several different forms:
          you could be working in standard English, American
          English or a house style specified by the client or
          employer you are working for

    - Spelling
        - Again, this is one of your most important tasks. It is
          very easy to rely on the spellchecker in Microsoft Word
          or other word-processing software, but this program
          cannot pick up typographic errors (‘typos’) which
          inadvertently form genuine words. For example, ‘from’
          and ‘form’ or ‘been’ and ‘bean’ can slip through the
          net in such cases as “I have just come form Spain” or
          “I have just bean to Spain”. To be completely sure of
          your spelling, make sure that you use a recognised
          standard dictionary to check; don’t just guess or ‘have
          a stab’ at it!

    - Verb tenses
        - you must always make sure that the subject of
          a sentence agrees with the verb and that the same tense
          is used consistently throughout a piece or section of
          work

    - Standard language protocols
        - Capital letters should be used everywhere they are
          required, particularly with regard to proper nouns
          (that is, names specifically associated with an entity,
          such as London or David) or at the start of a sentence.
          Other examples to avoid would be ‘text speak’ (for
          example, ‘lol’ and ‘rofl’) and to ensure your
          punctuation is used correctly

### - Proofreading references

    - Using the correct style – are you working on styling sheets
      or on the guidance that your employer has given you or have
      you been given grammar and spelling references to use? You
      will always need to refer to an officially sanctioned
      protocol for the sake of …

    - Consistency – this is a keystone of proofreading. Make sure
      that you use the same style, references, formatting,
      spelling and grammar throughout

    - Quotes – have any quotes used been properly attributed or
      referenced and have you used quotation marks or any other
      method of indicating speech according to the style and
      standards required by the employer?

    - Formatting and presentation – Along with checking for
      mistakes in the text, also make sure that you maintain
      consistency in line spacing, margination, font type, font
      sizes, heading points and heading styles.

### - The job of a proofreader

- Proofreaders check text documents for mistakes, whether these
  are simple or even rather complex syntactical errors.

- Mistakes in spelling and grammar should be comprehensively
  avoided when an article or other pieces of writing is sent out
  for publication or to be read – this is because such errors can
  impact the perception or integrity of a publication, or simply
  just spoil the experience for the reader.

- Full-time, professional proofreaders are members of an
  editorial publishing team as much as anybody else and all will
  try to make sure that the documents are well written before
  sending them out for printing.

- Proofreaders can also be self-employed or on the books of an
  agency, while other members of editorial teams such as
  journalists, editors, sub-editors and copy-editors can also be
  proofreaders as part of their job description or work within
  that team.

- However, if it can be avoided, you shouldn’t ever be the final
  proofreader of copy you have written yourself – you will make
  mistakes!

- Along with the ability to identify errors in written text,
  proofreaders must also know how to mark-up documents properly
  for correction.

- This usually involves the use of standardised symbols so that
  anyone making the corrections after you have marked a document
  up can understand and use the proofreader’s instructions.
  Inevitably, sometimes proofreaders will have to correct the
  same document a number of times, as the job is not finished
  until the text is as completely error-free as is humanly
  possible.

- Other parts of a proofreader’s job can include measuring the
  spacing and margins to ensure that they meet the standard
  criteria of a publication before the work is sent out to be
  printed. Proofreaders employed by the likes of newspapers and
  magazines can also be responsible for the positioning of
  headlines, articles, and photos.

- Many professional proofreaders and copyeditors work directly
  ‘on-screen’ using programs such as InDesign and QuarkXPress to
  ensure that the finished copy on the page can be swiftly sent
  out to be printed when there is a tight deadline.

- Proofreaders must also be able to work closely with writers,
  journalists and other proofreaders to make sure every word,
  sentence and paragraph is formatted correctly and free of error
  before the copy makes its way to the next stage of the
  publication process.

### - Where does a proofreader do his or her work?

- The proofreading role is one of many kinds of publishing roles
  which takes place behind the scenes at all publishing companies
  – and even many of those which only publish online.

- They are often part of an in-house team along with other
  proofreaders, writers, sub-editors, line-editors, copy-editors
  and editors, and most work under a supervisor or line manager
  in an office along with other members of staff.

- Proofreaders can also work from home if they are self-employed,
  sometimes checking academic and scholarly work, or pursuing
  privately-funded projects – these may also work temporarily
  under contract for publishing house offices, at their own
  offices or from home.

- Other proofreaders who do private work may specialise in
  a particular field and only proofread documents for particular
  kinds of organisations, such as grant applications for
  non-profits, or in a particular subject such as news journalism
  or science writing – a subject-specific qualification is often
  useful if you wish to concentrate on a particular area.

- There is a wide variety of places in which proofreaders can
  work; this could be a favourite coffee shop or café if
  freelance, or in a busy national or local newspaper or magazine
  office, potentially proofreading tens or hundreds of items and
  articles every week.

### A PROOFREADER’S PERSONAL QUALITIES

- A professional proofreader should have a number of abilities,
  qualifications and qualities to ensure that they can perform
  their work to an excellent standard and pursue their career
  effectively.

- The most important of these are:

### - 1. Excellent computer skills

- It is very important nowadays for a proofreader to have good
  and effective computer skills as, in this era of technological
  advancement, most proofreading will be performed on-screen or
  for a copy that will later be posted or uploaded onto websites.

- It is, therefore, essential for a proofreader to have an
  excellent understanding of numerous computer apps, programs and
  tools that relate to his or her task, such as Track Changes and
  Spellcheck in Word.

- There are many different types of online proofreading tool
  available that can help you review a text for mistakes and
  errors – these include the likes of SlickWrite, Grammarly,
  Ginger, Proof Read My File and many others. To employ these
  tools, you will need to have a detailed knowledge of computer
  programs and good up-to-date internet skills.

### - 2. Patience and diligence

- Proofreading is often considered a mundane task because it
  requires you to make a detailed analysis of a given text to
  identify grammar and spelling mistakes, bad choices of wording
  and meaning, poor sentence structure and punctuation errors. To
  perform such an in-depth analysis of content, you will need to
  have good reserves of concentration and patience.

- If you are being hired as a proofreader to review a piece of
  writing or document, you must have the precision and
  persistence to review a lengthy text with a consistently
  critical eye. Proofreaders should also be able to grasp what
  a writer is trying to say, however ambiguous the actual writing
  may be.

- Many professional proofreaders recommend double reading – that
  is, getting two people to read a text – along with reading
  aloud and backward reading to find both major and minor
  mistakes. Consequently, a professional proofreader should have
  the patience and skills to do a near-forensic review of any
  manuscript or text assigned to him or her.

### - 3. Be an attentive and passionate reader

- Every proofreader should be a good – even voracious – reader;
  that is, he or she should enjoy reading all the different forms
  of writing there are, including fiction and non-fiction books,
  academic journals, printed and published essays, newspapers,
  magazines, web content and blogs, articles and features, and
  even business letters.

- Having reading skills is probably the most essential
  characteristic that any proofreader should be in possession of,
  right from the start. This quality will give you that extra
  ability to competently find errors in grammar, spelling and
  sentence structure.

### - 4. Language awareness

- For locating spelling mistakes, wrong use of participles, verbs
  and tenses, incorrect word choice and typographic errors in any
  given text, it is imperative that a proofreader has expert
  command over his or her language or languages.

- A professional proofreader must be able to correct such
  mistakes accurately, appropriately and effectively. A full
  understanding of language is necessary for a proofreader to
  shape any text into something that is clear, well-structured
  and flows naturally when read.

- Awareness of various style guides, sheets and books

- There are many different kinds of standard style guide in the
  UK (and, indeed, US) English – these include the likes of 
    - The Oxford Style Manual by R M Ritter, 
    - The Guardian and Observer Style Guide
        - (www.theguardian.com/guardian-observer-style-guide-a), 
    - The MHRA Style Guide
    - (www.mhra.org.uk/Publications/Books/StyleGuide/download.shtml)
  and Copy-Editing: 
    - The Cambridge Handbook for Editors, Judith Butcher.

- A proofreader should be familiar with most of these popular
  style guides in order to review every detail of a text
  carefully and to ensure it conforms to the appropriate style of
  writing. Understanding the various style guides also helps to
  identify citational or referencing errors and, therefore, to
  ensure that the text is correct and accurate in all aspects.

- Please note, however, that many publications and organisations
  have their own particular ‘house style’ guides, which are often
  updated continuously as exceptions and new usages and rules are
  discovered and used. A client should let you know if you will
  be expected to conform to a house style; however, this will
  almost certainly be partially based on one of the standard
  guides, in any case.

### - 5. Formal training

- Every proofreader (or, indeed, copy-editor) needs some kind of
  formal training, though there is no standard set qualification
  for the profession.

- However, appropriate training should include a good GCSE grade
  in English language and literature (and preferably an ‘A’
  Level) – this will give you a solid foundation in the basic
  grammar and uses of language on which you can build your
  skills.

- In addition, there are a large number of college and agency
  courses which can be taken on their own or combined with other
  courses such as those in marketing, human resources, finance or
  many other subjects. The present course is a good start!

- Any work experience gained in publishing (whether while still
  at school or in a work environment) will also be an advantage.

- It is also very helpful for any proofreader to be widely read
  and have knowledge of a wide range of subjects in order to
  analyse the written word effectively – all-round general
  knowledge is not just an advantage in pub quizzes!

### - 6. Great observational skills and consistency

- Even small mistakes such as the inappropriate usage of
  exclamation marks or misplacing of commas can make a big
  difference to the finished quality of a document. Poor usage of
  full stops, ‘n-dashes’, colons and semi-colons can completely
  change the emphasis or even the meaning of a sentence.

- If a word is omitted or a double space is left between the
  beginning and end of two sentences, a proofreader should have
  the ability to catch it. 

- Proofreaders need to be detail-oriented so that he or she can
  scan and pick up all the minor mistakes in any given text. It
  is certainly important for a proofreader to be a good observer
  and notice details so that he or she can effectively review
  every text they work on, as well as making sure it is
  accessible and precise.

- In addition, he or she must be punctual in completing the
  required proofreading before any deadline specified by a client
  or publisher.

### - 7. Effective communication skills

- Appropriate and quick communication is essential for any
  proofreader, particularly when there is doubt about the use of
  any word, sentence or content of any sort. A proofreader should
  be able to communicate freely and make any query clear to
  a client or editor. Even inadvertent miscommunications by the
  proofreader can affect the resulting text a great deal.

### - 8. Allow adequate time to proofread

- Proofreading is often a very challenging task which requires
  allowing enough time to read and print out a text, analyse its
  content, mark-up any errors using the standard symbols and then
  perform the corrections; you may then have to check a document
  again in the same way to make sure that you haven’t missed
  anything else.

- A proofreader should allow themselves plenty of time to carry
  out all these tasks. Just reviewing a text online or on-screen
  is never enough to make it wholly accurate and error-free – you
  will have to perform multiple readings to check for mistakes
  and this is best done from a printed paper (or ‘hard’) copy.
  This technique is usually of paramount importance for effective
  writing, proofing and editing.

- A good proofreader is not afraid to use a dictionary,
  thesaurus, grammar reference book or style guide to review the
  content of a text and correct it. Furthermore, he or she should
  not feel shy when consulting others for appropriate advice.

### PROOFREADING TERMINOLOGY

### - Definition of proofreading:

- Proofreading is part of the editing process, but it is not
  actually ‘editing’ – it is the careful examination of the final
  draft of a document or text after it has been properly edited
  to ensure there are no errors or mistakes left.

- A proofreader will review the copy for errors and the incorrect
  use of regional English – that is, making sure that either
  American or UK English is being used consistently when
  necessary – and will often error check the page ‘furniture’
  such as:
    - captions, 
    - ‘pull quotes’, 
    - ‘standfirsts’, 
    - headlines, 
    - headings and straplines, 
    - images and illustrations 

- During the editorial and publication process, proofreading
  usually happens after the stages of actual editing have been
  completed. A final copy of the manuscript, known as a ‘final
  proof’, is then examined and analysed by a professional
  proofreader.

- A proofreaders’ job is to check for quality before the
  magazine, paper, book or text goes into mass production or
  circulation. 

- Often, he or she then takes the original edited copy and
  compares it to the proof side-by-side to ensure that there are
  no omissions or missing pages, or awkward words or page breaks
  (and displeasing artefacts such as ‘_orphans_’ – see below).

### - What a proofreader doesn’t do

- Though a proofreader sometimes does a little light editing (for
  example, correcting inconsistent spelling or non-adjectival
  hyphenation), he or she has a subtly different job to
  a copy-editor.

- In fact, if too many errors are noted, he or she may return the
  proof for further copy-editing before going over it again.

- Professional proofreading is required by all traditional
  paper-based publishers as an essential quality-assurance
  measure, before printing off any quantity of books, newspapers,
  magazines or publicity materials, whether small or large.

- However, many self-publishing authors who have had their
  manuscript or text professionally copy-edited may skip the
  proofreading – this is becoming very prevalent online, with
  a parallel decline in standards of the published text. It is
  worth writing to such authors and pointing out the mistakes,
  thus fishing out some extra work for yourself.

- A struggling author may be tempted to proofread their own work
  to save time and money. However, a thorough professional
  proofreading is always the best way to go.

### - What do proofreaders do?

- After all the other changes have been done, a final ‘proofing’
  checks for any remaining mistakes in the layout, spelling,
  grammar, punctuation and usage of bold, italics and
  underlining. There is never any re-writing performed at this
  stage – your job is just making sure that the content is
  correct.

- The Society of Proofreaders and Copy Editors (SfEP) states
  that: 
    > “The proofreader reads the copy for consistency in usage
    > and layout, for accuracy in the text and references and for
    > typesetting errors. 
    > The proofreader, however, is only acting as a quality
    > check, making sure that the copy-editor or typesetter has
    > not missed something. He or she is not responsible for
    > overall consistency and accuracy.”

### - The standard proofreading symbols

- So that corrections can be understood at a later date, there
  are standard international proofreading symbols which should be
  known and deployed by all professional proofreaders.

- The use of proofreading symbols almost always follows the same
  pattern: a mark is placed in the margin in pen or pencil by the
  proofreader, along with specific (usually abbreviated) details
  about the change that should be made during a correction, and
  a mark in the text showing where those instructions should be
  carried out.

- The complete standard set of proofreading marks is defined by
  British Standard BS 5261C and will be presented in full in
  Module 4.

- There is a large amount of jargon used in the world of
  publishing, as you might expect from such a creative and
  technology-driven industry, but not all mean what you might
  think they do.

- For example, a ‘sans serif’ is not a holiday camp in Spain,
  ‘curly quotes’ isn’t a hairstyle, an ‘orphan’ isn’t
  a parentless child and a ‘bastard title’ has nothing to do with
  whether your aristocratic parents are married. 
    - ‘Overmatter’ is not a physical state, while ‘daggers’,
      ‘bullet points’ and ‘backslashes’ are rarely fatal … and
      finally – ‘dead copy’ can often be revived.

### - What is copy-editing?

- Copy-editing (sometimes written as two words or one word,
  depending on style) describes all the work that an editor does
  to improve the text of a manuscript and make it ready for
  publication.

- Here, we set out the jargon used in the copy-editing trade.
  These are the terms and abbreviations used by copy-editors as
  they try to produce copy that is clear, correct, consistent and
  concise.

- You will need to know these terms and how to use them when you
  are preparing any piece of writing for publication, and you
  will also potentially need to use and understand them when
  consulting.

- A comprehensive glossary (or alphabetical list) of proofreading
  terms now follows, including their basic definitions. Try to
  learn them ‘by heart’ now and as the course progresses – you
  will need to comprehend what they are as well as what they are
  instructing you to do.

### PUBLISHING PROCESS (Proofreading Perspective)

### - The production process

- Once a piece of text, a manuscript or document has been
  accepted for publication by whomsoever is employing you to
  proofread it, it will go through several essential stages
  before it is ready for printing or digital publishing and
  distribution.

- These stages can be summarised in the following ways, though
  they sometimes vary according to a client’s instructions or
  a company’s style:

    - Copy-editing
    - Correction
    - Typesetting or, more likely these days, pasting into
    - a program such as InDesign
    - Sub-editing
    - Correction
    - Proofreading
    - Correction
    - Publication

- Most books, newspapers or magazines are taken through this
  process by specialist project managers, which can be either
  in-house or freelance (as any employees on the publishing line can be)

- The time this takes varies, but often the work takes place to
  a strict deadline, particularly when a publication is daily,
  weekly or monthly, or when a release date has been set. Your
  job in any editorial position is to meet that deadline.

- The typical publishing schedule for a book from the handover of
  the finished manuscript to the production department to
  publication and printing is often as follows:

    - Copyediting and correction 
        - (4 weeks, with the author being queried in the 3rd week)
    - Typesetting or pasting onto the page with illustrations or diagrams 
        - (two weeks)
    - Proofreading, correction and collation 
        - (three weeks)
    - Revision 
        - (one week)
    - Revision checks and indexing 
        - (one week)
    - Final correction 
        - (one week)
    - Digital bundle creation 
        - (one week)
    - Printing 
        - (five weeks)
    - Delivery of bound copies to the warehouse and distribution
        - (four weeks)

- This is an idealistic turnaround for a book.

- In the case of a monthly magazine, there can often be
  a 6-to-8-week run-up to printing, where the first three weeks
  are spent editing, correcting, designing and collating to style
  and templates, and then a week is spent readying every page for
  the press on office computers. 
    - Often the finished magazine will be printed from PDF files
      created online or on an internal server.

### - Copy-editing

- Typically, an experienced, professional copy-editor will be the
  first person to look at a piece of writing.

- The job of copy-editor is often substitutable for a sub-editor
  in the magazine and newspaper trades, but such staff can also
  perform several more complex tasks (for which, see below), as
  well as other, similar ones. The level of editing to be
  performed can vary according to the requirements of a project.

- Generally, a copy-editor will perform the following:

    - The correction of grammar, spelling and syntax
    - Checking references and bibliographies for omissions and
      inconsistencies in style
    - Bringing any suspected errors, omissions or duplications to
      the attention of the journalist, author or writer
    - The enforcement of house style and tone of voice
    - The undertaking of any rewriting that may be necessary
    - The incorporation of the answers to author queries into the
      electronic text (generally a Word document)

- It is unusual for the author to see the finished text before
  publication, though a few can be contractually obliged to do
  this and you will have to comply accordingly; you will
  certainly be told whether such clauses exist, however.

### - Sub-editing

- While copy-editors are largely employed by book publishers,
  news and periodical organisations usually employ _sub-editors_
  to deal with similar tasks.

- In reality, sub-editors – or ‘subs’ – perform the same tasks as
  a copy-editor, such as checking grammar and spelling, but also
  have a number of other important duties.

- These can include some or all of the following:

    - Large-scale rewriting and restructuring of articles
    - Large-scale cutting of articles
    - Writing photo and illustration captions, crossheads,
    - ‘standfirsts’ and headlines
    - Laying out newspaper and magazine pages (usually onto the
    - page in desktop publishing programs such as InDesign or
    - QuarkXPress)
    - Checking for and correcting problems involving libel and
    - copyright, and other legal issues
    - Fact-checking and correcting
    - Making sure journalists and reporters meet their deadlines
    - Styling text and inserting HTML links to other relevant
    - stories or articles
    - Making online text SEO (Search Engine Optimisation)
    -  compliant so that search engines can easily find it

### - Proofreading

- A typical part of book production will be when the edited files
  are tagged to create XML from which the digital versions of
  a book or publication will be produced, and the print version
  of your book is set in Indesign, often personally by the
  copy-editor.

- Page ‘proofs’ are then created in electronic PDF formats, which
  are then ready to be checked online or on printed-off versions,
  which can be either in colour or black-and-white. These need to
  be sifted very carefully for mistakes, and sometimes more than
  once by more than one person since this will be the last chance
  to make any amendments before the publication is printed.

- The proofreader only makes essential corrections at this stage,
  as all the major editing should have been done in detail and
  there should be next to no typographical errors remaining.

- Once the final corrections have been made, the editor or
  Project Manager usually supplies revised final proofs in
  electronic format for the proofreader to check. At this point
  in book production, indexing may be done – this is sometimes
  allotted to the proofreader job list, too, or to a professional
  indexer if the budget will stretch to that.

### - Indexing

- If you are compiling a personal index, your editor or Project
  Manager will supply you with the publisher’s detailed
  guidelines, which will usually comply with or be derived from
  a well-known standard or standards, which is often academic in
  origin.

- A list of major words (aka ‘headwords’) and subheadings may be
  drawn up at ‘first-proof’ stage, but page numbers are usually
  not put in until the final, revised proof stage, as this is
  generally when the pagination of the book is being set.

- If the company is using the services of a professional indexer,
  he or she will be asked to compile the index at the first-proof
  stage, and the author will be sent the index to check at the
  revised proof stage before it is finalised by the proofreader.

### - Printing

- Once the revised book proofs have been approved, the editor or
  Project Manager will supply digital print-ready files to the
  publisher, usually no later than six weeks before the
  publication date of bound sellable copies. Only then is the
  final cover artwork submitted by the designers. Finally, the
  Production Editor checks every single file and sends the whole
  bundle off to be printed.

### - Publication

- The process is still not over, and advance copies of a book are
  checked over by the Production Editor. Once approved, the stock
  is delivered to the publisher’s warehouse and arrangements are
  made to ship the finished printed book to our distributors
  around the country, and even the world.

- As a proofreader this is probably the most complex publishing
  process you are likely to be involved in, hence the detail
  provided here. However, whether you are proofing a concert
  flyer or a book, the basic tasks are the same – you will need
  to make the text completely sound in terms of grammar, spelling
  and punctuation.

### Summary

-     A proofreader’s job is almost always to find mistakes in
      the spelling, grammar and punctuation of a piece of writing
      and correct them, almost always at the final stage of the
      publication process
-     A copy-editor’s job is to do all the above at an earlier
      stage, plus making sure that the text is well-structured
      and conveys its ideas clearly, that it is well-formatted
      and that any factual problems and legal issues are also
      addressed
-     The most important terms used in the two jobs, and where
      the jobs sit in the production processes of books and
      periodicals, were presented in full.
