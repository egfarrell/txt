### - Introduction
    - In books and longer reports, it is not just the main body
      of the text which gets published.
    - There are also several pages at the beginning and end of
      a volume which can comprise of:
        - title pages,
        - prefaces,
        - introductions,
        - copyright pages,
        - lists of figures and plates,
        - contents,
        - addenda,
        - appendices,
        - bibliographies,
        - endnotes and indexes.
    - Together this material comprises of the front and end matter,
      and it also needs careful copy-editing.

    - Editing the front and end matter in books, academic papers
      and corporate reports includes the likes of prefaces,
      footnotes, title pages, contents pages, lists of tables,
      appendices, references, postscripts and indexes.
    - Each of these sections requires specialised techniques and
      must incoporate the style and tone of the main text. They
      must also follow the client’s brief.

### - Become a sourcing expert
    - The key parts of a text requiring the most research are
      potentially the footnotes, endnotes, reference sections and
      bibliography, along with any appendices containing
      additional information.
    - The main pitfall in compiling these sections is the
      inclusion of too much information.
    - Footnotes and endnotes are used by authors when they want
      to signal there is more data or information available, but
      also that it isn’t essential for the main body of the text.
      It is only relevant for those who want to dig deeper. These
      extra notes can also add to the veracity or gravitas of
      a text.
    - Footnotes and endnotes enable the author to continue the
      narrative uninterrupted without having to insert the
      sources of every quote or fact into the main body – thus
      hindering the flow of the text.
    - Such a system is also essential in providing extra support
      for the author’s assertions, as well as for providing the
      direct sources of quotes.
    - If you are copy-editing academic texts, ‘real-life’ stories
      or other factual works, it is a valuable skill to know how
      to set out footnotes, endnotes, reference sections and
      bibliographies.

### - How to use reference marks?
    - Serious factual texts,will need to reference items which
      appear in the main body of your text and that need further
      explanation. For this you must use the note system.
    - Notes may be in the form of footnotes or endnotes, and
      a style guide may indicate which to use.
    - Footnotes or endnotes work as follows:
        - A reference mark is placed at the appropriate point in
          the text, usually using a standard symbol such as an
          asterisk, dagger or consecutive numeral
        - An explanation corresponding with the mark is inserted
          at the bottom of the page, in the case of a footnote,
          or at the end of a chapter or manuscript in the case of
          an endnote

### - What is a reference mark?
    - The term ‘reference mark’ refers to a symbol or numeral
      which is placed in the main text  and informs the reader
      there is more detailed information to be found elsewhere in
      the text.
    - A reference mark is created by inserting the symbol, just
      after the sentence (or phrase) containing the information
      to be referenced.
    - The mark is usually formatted in superscript form. e.g.
    - “The first use of the term ‘skiffle’ on record was in 1925
      in the name of Jimmy Bryant and his Chicago Skifflers,
      though other musical historians dispute this.^3”
    - If a document contains a number of notes, the usual
      protocol to number them sequentially, and re-start the
      number with each new chapter
    - If there aren’t many footnotes, their numbering can be
      consecutive throughout the text or standard symbols should
      be used.
    - The most frequent of these are as follows:
        - `(*)` Asterisk
        - `(†)` Dagger
        - `(‡)` Double dagger
    - It is also best to use these symbols if the author has
      allotted superscript numbers to other features of the text,
      such as in algebraic equations.
    - Sometimes both footnotes and endnotes are used, and in this
      instance, endnotes should be referenced with numbers and
      footnotes with symbols.
    - If a manuscript has too many footnotes or endnotes, perhaps
      suggest to the author that they are consolidated.

### - Where to place reference marks?
    - You should always place the reference marks directly after
      the information needing an explanation, preferably at the
      end of a sentence or clause, _after the punctuation_ but
      before a dash, if there is one.
    - e.g.
    - “Atlanta’s efforts to curb downtown traffic during the 1996
      Summer Olympics dramatically improved the health of
      children with asthma and people of all ages with chronic
      obstructive pulmonary disease.^2 ”
    - In this case, the numerical superscript will identify
      a footnote or endnote referencing the source of the quote.
    - Reference marks placed before (after?) a closing bracket
      usually refer to information contained within the brackets.
    - e.g.
        - “The Essex Chronicle reported that Paul Walbridge had
          definitely seen a black panther on the neighbouring
          farmland despite there being no photo available
          (sightings of black panthers in Britain are almost
          invariably found to be domestic cats, if good quality
          images are taken)^3”.

### - Reasons to use reference notes
    - Footnotes and endnotes can be used to reference anything
      that the author does not think is important enough to
      include in the main text.
    - Their are typically used to:
        - To alert readers that material has been taken from an
          external source
        - To indicate the original source of a text, statement or
          quotation
        - To present background material that would be excessive
          in the main text or to quantitatively support
          statements in the text
        - To reference other sections of the same work

### - When to use footnotes instead of endnotes?
    - Footnotes are placed beside the progression of the text
      simply because they are quicker to read, instead of turning
      pages to other locations in the text.
    - However, many publishers prefer endnotes, purely for the
      aesthetic reason that they don’t interrupt the text flow or
      page layout. Publishers may think it off-putting for
      customers if they see significant amounts of text at the
      bottom of a page.

### - Footnotes
    - Footnotes are usually placed at the bottom of the same page
      where the reference mark appears.
    - They are typically inserted in a smaller font smaller than
      the running text and are inserted as a superscript.
    - The note should always begin at the bottom of the page on
      which the relevant reference marks appear but may continue
      onto the next page if needed.
        - If continuing onto the next page, the break in the note
          should always be in the middle of a phrase or sentence
          so the reader is aware the footnote continues on the
          following page.

### - Endnotes
    - Endnotes are compiled in page order into their own section
      towards the end of a manuscript. They are usually placed
      after the main text and appendices but before
      a bibliography.
    - Endnotes frequently use a smaller typeface than the main
      body. The section is generally has the heading ‘Endnotes’
      or ‘Notes’ to differentiate it from other ‘end matter’.
    - If an endnote is removed or added, all numbering must be
      updated to reflect the new numerical order.
    - In addition ‘endnotes’ and ‘footnotes’, there are two types
      of reference:

### - "Content notes"
    - This term indicates further information that an author
      wishes to share about the text to which a reference mark is
      appended

### - "Source notes"
    - This is a citation given to indicate the source where
      a statement or fact originated.
    - Content and source notes are not generally treated any
      differently in a given text, but it is helpful to know this
      jargon.
    - Another way of presenting citational material is to use
      ‘parenthetical notes’. These occur when references or
      supporting information is not inserted as a footnote or
      endnote but instead placed within brackets after
      a statement.
    - e.g.
        - “Cannibalism in the Papuan Tree Frog is strictly
          limited to the dry season after the species has bred
          (Dracula, C and Frankenstein, M. 1993. Protein and
          energy conservation in Indonesian arboreal anurans.
          Amphibian Conservation 37: 221-232).”
    - If there is a reference section at the end of the book, an
      entry such as this may be rendered as “(Dracula and
      Frankenstein, 1993)”, with the full reference listed
      alphabetically or consecutively in that section.

### - Which style should be used for note citations?
    - Each industry and discipline has many reference and
      citation styles.
    - As a copy-editor, you are likely to encounter a large
      number of style guides and manuals and you need have access
      to the latest editions
    - You must check which edition a publisher or client expects
      before accepting a job or project.
    - Different editions may contain significant revisions and
      copy-editors need to keep up-to-date.

### - Summary
    - This module covered front and end matter. You learnt that:
    - The sections at the beginning and end of a manuscript are
      just as important as the main text body
    - _Reference marks_ such as asterisks, daggers and numerals
      are placed in the text to indicate footnotes and endnotes
    - References are usually placed at the end of a manuscript,
      but can also be referred to in the text using several
      methods, including parenthetical notes.
