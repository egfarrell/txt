### INTRODUCTION
    - A key task for copy editors is to ensure a text is
      consistent and accurate. A key facet of this nuanced task
      is the correct use of language.
    - You will have to be aware of the meaning and effect of
      every word as it is deployed in a text, and be able to hone
      the words into a fluid and compelling whole.
    - It must be appropriate and accessible for its intended
      audience.
    - Topics in this module are:
        - A summary of editing techniques
        - Editing for fluency, plainness and simplicity
        - Removing inappropriate material
        - Editing for multicultural audiences

### - Summary of editing roles
    - Some publishers employ both 'line-editors' and
      'copy-editors'. However, in this course, 'copy-editing' is
      taken to cover both job descriptions, as both pay detailed
      attention to language use and use the same mark-up
      techniques.
    - Nowadays, a copy-editor is often expected to cover both
      jobs but this is not always the case and some publishers
      still maintain the traditional roles.
    - Each role uses different processes and skill sets and often
      occur at separate phases in the editing process.
    -  Line-editing is a specific task which concentrates on the
       creative content, writing style and language use at the
       level of sentences and paragraphs.
        - It's aim is not to sift the text for errors but to
          focus on the way the language is used to communicate
          with the reader.
    - A line-editor will ensure that the language used is fluid,
      clear and pleasant to read, and will also check it conveys
      an appropriate sense of atmosphere, feeling and tone.
    - Line-editors ensure the chosen words convey a specific
      meaning, rather than a series of generalisations and
      clichés.

### - Line-editing
    - A line-editor will identify:
    - Unnecessary or repeated words / sentences
    - Run-on sentences
    - Redundancies from repeated information
    - Dialogue or overwritten paragraphs to be tightened
    - Fictional scenes where the action / dialogue is confusing,
      or the author’s intentions are unclear
    - Shifts in tone and clumsy, awkward or unnatural-sounding
      phrasing
    - Blandly written passages to be ‘livened up’
    - Confusing narrative or descriptive digressions
    - Changes to make the overall narrative thrust better
    - words or phrases clarify or enhance the meaning
    - In this way, an editor doesn’t just only improve the
      manuscript but gives the writer the creative tools to
      become better in future projects.

### - Differences: copy-editing & line-editing
    - In contrast with line-editing, copy-editing aims to address
      a manuscript’s flaws at a more technical level, ensuring
      the writing meets industry and stylistic standards.
    - It is more akin to high-end proofreading than actual
      editing, if split away from the job of line-editor, as it
      was historically.
    - In a strict interpretation of the role, a copy-editor will:
        - Correct spelling, grammar, punctuation and syntax
        - Ensure standards of consistency are upheld in spelling,
          hyphenation, numerals, fonts and capitalisation. That
          is, the style is maintained.
        - Flag statements which are ambiguous or factually
          incorrect, particularly for non-fiction or news writing
        - Track internal consistency. E.g.: ensure there are no
          discrepancies in the plot, setting and characters

    - There has always been an element of overlap between the
      work of a line-editor and a copy-editor.
    - All editors will highlight technical errors or logical
      inconsistencies. This is because their overarching aim is
      to make the writing better and they tend to be
      perfectionists by disposition.
    - However, it is not the specific purpose of a line-editor to
      comb the text with the intention of fixing the grammar,
      spelling, capitalisation or change all spellings of
      'colour' to 'colour' because the is presented in UK
      English; that is the job of a copy-editor.
    - Copy-editing, strictly speaking, requires a deep,
      rule-based understanding of standard English usage that
      many traditional editors don’t have or need.
    - The main reason that line-editing and copy-editing was
      traditionally kept separate is that copy-editing always
      came after a line edit.
    - The content of your manuscript should be completely
      finalised on the page before being fine-tuned at the level
      of a copy-edit.
    - Logically, there is no point in expending time proofreading
      parts of an early draft that will end up being
      significantly changed by the time the final draft stage is
      reached.
    - Other than the proofreader, the copy-editor is usually the
      last person to make major changes before the text goes into
      production.
    - Therefore, the job of a line-editor is to help the author
      tell a better story, while a copy-editor ensures the
      grammar and spelling are completely correct.

### EDITING FOR FLUENT & SIMPLE COPY
    - Content editing is the essential job of copy-editing. It
      requires close liaising with the author, project manager or
      managing editor.
    - A copy-editor should ask themselves:
        - Is the author’s tone and level of complexity suitable
          for the intended audience?
        - Is the text organised logically or appropriately?
        - Is the layout conducive to the text?
        - Are quotes or extracts properly referenced?
    - You must carefully work through these points as until the
      answer to all questions is ‘yes’, while ensuring the
      author’s aims or voice isn't disrupted.
    - Imagine copy-editor as a 'gardener' who is pruning and
      cutting each plant with the goal of making the existing
      plants in a garden look as good as possible, while staying
      true to the original!

    - What will this 'pruning' entail? The most important tasks
      are:
        - Clarifying ambiguous phrases or assertions
        - Fixing structural, organisational or conceptual issues
        - Identifying inconsistencies, anomalies & discrepancies
        - Revising figures of speech, redundancies, hyperbole,
          metaphors and euphemisms
        - Suggesting fixes to anything which interferes with
          direct and streamlined communication with the reader

    - A copy-editor can also help smooth awkward transitions,
      look out for biased or imprecise language or stereotyping,
      and highlight libellous or sensationalist statements that
      could expose the author or publisher to legal action.

    - For the most contentious issues, you should merely point
      out potential problems and suggest possible fixes. It is
      not your responsibility to actually implement these
      changes. You can enable this by querying it, a process
      discussed later.

    - Remember: all changes must retain the authorial voice. Do
      not put words into the author’s mouth.

    - It is helpful when the author provides his or her own notes
      or even style sheet containing essential information on
      slang, jargon, figures of speech, euphemisms or acronyms
      which are essential to understanding. This is particularly
      useful in genre fiction or scientific writing.

    - When editing fiction, a list of characters and their
      personality types may be provided, as well as environment
      details or backstory. Always ask whether such documentation
      exists before starting a specialised job.

### - Cutting the text down
    - Simplicity and fluency almost always involve pruning the
      text. Many  writers and authors have a tendency to
      overwrite rather than not provide enough.
    - However, the rougher the initial condition of  the writing,
      the more overwhelming it may feel. Don’t panic or despair.
      Rougher projects improve your abilities and skills.
    - Ideas for trimming and honing unwieldy or overwrought
      English are provided below. The most frequent ‘word crimes’
      are also included.

### - Clichés
    - A cliché is only a cliché because it’s true.
    - Consequently, your aim is not to get rid of every one but
      to re-work parts where cliché and convoluted metaphors
      cloud the author's intent. Or where the cliche is plainly
      unsuitable. In most contexts, one idiom is usually enough.
    - E.g.: “Going forward, Brian thought he’d think outside the
      box and then run it up the flagpole and see who saluted
      it.”
    - Better: “In future, Brian decided that he would try to be
      more original with his ideas and test them out on his
      colleagues afterwards.”
    - Bear in mind that if the language sounds trite or hackneyed
      you will need to freshen it.

### - Euphemisms, business talk and jargon
    - We also need to consider clunky phrases and jargon. Even
      the best writers sometimes use phrases that makes the text
      uneccessarily complicated.
    - Business talk and jargon should be replaced with plain
      English. E.g., neologisms such as ‘utilise’ and
      ‘effectuate’ could be replaced with ‘use’ and ‘cause’, or
      even ‘employ’ and ‘result in’.
    - Unless it’s a key element in the writing, you should not
      use chat-room and texting abbreviations. They will only be
      understood by a certain number of people and are not
      standard English.
    - Banned examples include: ‘lol’ = ‘laugh out loud’ or
      abbreviations such as ‘C U L8R’

### - Redundancies
    - It is easy for a writer to habitually use phrases that are
      ‘normal’ when spoken but verbose when printed.
    - As a copy-editor, be aware of redundant words.
    - Examples:
    - ‘Past history’
    - ‘To plan in advance'
    - ‘Major breakthrough’
    - “I, personally, think”
    - “Armed gunmen”

### - Inconsistency
    - This is a very important issue, and an authoritative style
      guide is very useful. When working with book manuscripts,
      the best copy-editors keep running lists of facts to which
      they will refer as the edit is progressing.
    - e.g., if editing a work of fiction, keep notes on:
        - The various characters (such as: page 7: Jo Watson,
          blonde hair, muscular),
        - Locations (page 107: Romford, Essex – location of
          cousin’s home),
        - Backstory and plot information (page 99: Jo has purse
          stolen in shopping precinct – is it replaced later?)
        - Date and weather consistency (page 13: Jo is in Spain
          in July)
        - Jo suddenly becomes Joe on page 159 etc
    - Inconsistencies also occur in factual, academic or
      corporate documents, especially when terminology has
      recently been updated or historical names are still being
      used.
    - If you are editing a local history of the 1980s and the
      text mentions a 'Snickers' bar, checking will confirm that
      it would have been called a 'Marathon' at that time.

### - The passive voice
    - Even the grammar-checking software will suggest that The
      active voice is more direct in most situations.
    - e.g.: consider the sentence: “It was discovered that Jo had
      eaten a Snickers bar”.
    - The author hasn’t told you who found this out – he or she
      is merely pointing out that Jo ate a Snickers. Changing to
      the active voice may introduce more information: “Jerry
      discovered that Jo had eaten a Snickers.”
    - Not all sentences need an active voice, however. You should
      use your editorial judgment to decide when it works better.
      The passive voice may be suitable in some cases.
    - E.g.: “Jo was invited to the office party”. It may be
      irrelevant who issued the invitation.
    - Ensure you read everything in context (include the house
      style as well) and use your judgement and experience.

### KNOWING THE READERSHIP OR AUDIENCE
    - Note that language which may be appropriate for one
      readership may not suit another.
        - e.g., if editing a guide to web content management,
          a definition of HTML should be supplied early in the
          text. However, putting such a definition in
          a newsletter for programmers would be patronising.
    - A copy-editor will constantly have to make decisions based
      judgement and the potential reader should always be the key
      factor behind these decisions.
    - Situation where the readership is key:
        - Deciding on spelling out abbreviations such as PC
          (whether ‘personal computer’ or ‘political
          correctness’), RSPB or mph etc
        - Hyphenating the most common `open compound` words is
          also a judgement call;
            - if ambiguity could result, you may not need to.
              e.g., inserting hyphens in the phrase
              ‘small-business owner’ is grammatically correct but
              could well be seen as overkill when addressing
              self-employed people
        - When editing books in a series which feature recurring
          characters, you can often assume readers are following
          the sequence and already know the backstory from
          previous instalments.
        - However, many novels in a series with recurring
          characters are also meant to be read as stand-alone
          tales, so this may not always work.
        - Despite official school teaching, contractions and
          informal language are valid in much writing, especially
          fictional work.
        - The readers of scientific journals or school textbooks
          are less likely to be put off by technical terms and
          jargon. In fact, a dryer tone is positively required
          – flippancy would detract from the textual authority.
        - Whenever you have doubts regarding appropriateness for
          the potential audience, query the author.

### - Keeping the writer’s voice
    - A good copy-editor carries out numerous style and usage
      corrections and also carefully considers how the
      information will be understood by its intended audience.
    - However, a genuinely good copy-editor has another trick:
      a cloak of invisibility in the final work. This knack of
      retaining the author’s voice involves a fine-tuned set of
      judgement calls as well as the requisite skills of
      fact-checking, grammar and spelling.
    - To ensure you retain the unique characteristics of an
      author’s writing the following methods of channelling the
      author may be useful:

### - Pruning skills
    - It will often be necessary for you to rewrite copy or add
      a sentence for clarity. You will need to ensure that your
      own written inserts flawlessly maintain the tonal integrity
      and consistency of the author’s own voice.
    - To perhaps over-extend a metaphor: prune the dead and
      overgrown parts of the flower but keep it whole and looking
      better.

### - Leave comments
    - Sometimes you will be unsure as to the precise wording an
      author would use. In this situation, insert a comment box
      or Author Query asking the writer to consider certain
      changes.
    - A good writer always appreciates assistance but ensure you
      offer that advice politely.

### - Do not ‘over-edit’
    - A good copy-editor shouldn’t change text arbitrarily
      because they would have written the copy themselves. It is
      not your work, even if you shape some of it.
    - A copy-editor shouldn’t rewrite or add whole chunks of text
      and shouldn’t re-organise the structure or change faulty
      _plot development_. These are areas the author should do
      (perhaps after a polite query form yourself or consultation
      with a line-editor). Your job is to ask polite questions,
      and the protocol is to make suggestions using queries.
    - "If it ain’t broke": Sometimes an author’s sentences are
      simple, clear and well woven together – if this is the
      case, leave them be.

### - Ask what is expected of you
    - Before starting a longer project, discuss the requirements
      in detail with the author, publisher and/or managing
      editor.
    - You will likely want to understand the kinds of projects
      the person has worked on before, and you will need to find
      out what the client expects of you. Drilling down, you will
      need to ask how much editing is required.
    - A `heavy edit` when the author has expected a lighter touch
      could ruin a good working relationship; remember that it is
      someone’s creativity you are dealing with.
    - Gauging the balance between levels of editing is partly
      intuitive and partly understanding the client.

    - ‘baseline edit’: (light edit)
        - Correct mechanical inconsistencies and mistakes in the
          body of the text: these will include spelling,
          capitalisation, punctuation, abbreviations and
          acronyms, hyphenation and dash use, font type and size,
          among many other issues.
        - Correct inconsistencies and mistakes in all other parts
          of the document: these will include the footnotes and
          endnotes, tables of content, page numbers and their
          placement, headers and footers, as well as images,
          captions, annotations, charts, graphs & histograms.
        - Check and correct grammar and usage errors, but not so
          far as to change anything that isn’t an outright error.
        - Flag awkward or confusing language, but don’t revise
          them – make a comment or query.
        - Query unusual or potentially misused words that might
          not be easily understandable by the readership.
        - Flag information that may be incorrect (opinion pieces
          are particularly nuanced in this regard, though
          fact-checking these is a lost art and worth reviving).
        - Flag information or quotes that require permission
        - Query statements that may leave the writer or
          publisher open to legal action.

    - 'heavy edit'
        - includes the items above for a 'baseline edit'
        - Correct all errors and inconsistencies in grammar,
          syntax and usage
        - Rewrite sections that are too overwrought, ‘wordy’,
          awkwardly constructed or otherwise confusing
        - Flag, comment on and query overused, inappropriate
          figures of speech, jargon or sentiment.
        - Revise incorrect or non-factual information
        - Query discrepancies, anomalies and conflicts in
          content. You can also fix these if you are certain. In
          works of fiction, the content includes details in the
          plot, setting and characters.
        - Suggest changes in language that appears to be biased
          or promote stereotyping or could be insensitive to the
          readership; however, context is important.
        - Suggest changes to layout or structure in terms of
          clarity or readability, or suggest a more logical way
          of putting forward an argument.

### EDITING TO REMOVE INAPPROPRIATE MATERIAL
    - So-called ‘political correctness’ (PC), serves a necessary
      purpose as it is "the avoidance of forms of expression or
      action that are perceived to exclude, marginalise or insult
      groups of people who are socially disadvantaged or
      discriminated against."
    - A writer will want their work to appeal to a broad
      readership, and be widely read and understood. Even if
      their work is intended for a specialised audience, it is
      still necessary not to exclude sections of society.
        - This makes business and creative sense, but also has
          a moral point: no group in society should be treated as
          superior to any other.
    - A copy-editor must point out when an author strays too far
      over the line between being wisely cautious and being
      silly; it is also to point out that certain words, phrases,
      sentences and intents could be interpreted as biased or
      insensitive towards socially disadvantaged groups.
    - Writing does not have to offend or incite people to get its
      point across, but it shouldn’t 'mollycoddle' them, either.
    - A copy-editor should flag or change language that promotes
      bias, hatred or stereotyping based on gender, sexuality,
      religion, race or ethnic background, age or mental or
      physical disability.
    - However, a good copy-editor must judge how to negotiate
      that precarious balance between eliminating language which
      is potentially unacceptable and allowing authors to express
      themselves.
    - Your real task is not to censor the author’s mode of
      expression, but to highlight transgressions they may not be
      aware of.
    - "It’s political correctness gone mad” is a commonly heard,
      phrase which describes how the possibility of offending
      someone is a hindrance to free speech, personal expression
      and offering an honest opinion.
    - In reality, there is little truth to this and PC aims for
      a  better, more caring society. It doesn’t mean that
      contentious issues can’t be discussed, only that it is
      right to treat others fairly, in print and in person. What
      is wrong with that?

- Below are issues that are worth querying:

### - Gender
    - Should a writer refer to something as ‘he’ or ‘she’, when
      ‘it’ would suffice?
    - Is it necessary to describe professionals as a ‘male nurse’
      or a ‘female doctor’?
    - Is the descriptor ‘he’or ‘his’ appropriate? (e.g.: "The
      line-manager just invited you to a meeting his office”)
    - Can words like 'chairman' and 'fireman' be changed to
      'chairperson’ and 'firefighter'?
    - For guidance on acceptable terms for gender roles, see:
        - https://www.gires.org.uk/resources/terminology/
    - For guidance on acceptable LGBTQ+ terminology see:
        - https://www.itspronouncedmetrosexual.com/2013/01/a-comprehensive-list-of-lgbtq-term-definitions/

### - Race
    - Is it necessary or important to point out racial
      differences between characters or people? e.g. "The board
      comprises eight men and two African-American women”
    - Is there any reason to use arcane terms such as ‘oriental’
      when referring to an Asian or East Asian person?
    - Guidance on racial and cultural language:
      - www.covwarkpt.nhs.uk/download.cfm?doc=docm93jijm4n468.pdf&ver=580
      - https://www.lawsociety.org.uk/en/topics/ethnic-minority-lawyers/a-guide-to-race-and-ethnicity-terminology-and-language

### - Disabilities
    - Is it appropriate to mention a person’s disability? e.g.,
      "Despite her wheelchair she managed a team of solicitors.”
    - Consider changing negative references such as ‘confined to
      a wheelchair’? Referring to the same person as
      a ‘wheelchair-user’ is perfectly adequate.
    - For an up-to-date reference acceptable terms for
      disabilities, see:
      - https://ncdj.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/10/NCDJ-styleguide-2018.pdf
    - Using straightforward PC language. Eg a term such as
      ‘differently-abled’ – while being well-meant, seems
      awkwardly euphemistic. Perhaps ‘person with a disability’
      is better.

### - Political correctness in fiction
    - Fiction is largely governed by its own rules.
    - It is generally desirable in journalism and corporate
      communications to maintain an impartial and objective tone,
      but this is not the case in fiction, where people’s speech
      may be offensive or aired in the context of a story.
      - language which would be unacceptable in a real-life work
        contexts is often contained within fictional writing.
    - e.g., there is no need to excise every single potentially
      offensive comment in a novel. e.g. where country people are
      referred to as ‘yokels’ – and it is mostly unnecessary to
      change dialogue which contains controversial language or
      subject matters.
    - Prejudiced language may be a defining facet of a fictional
      character or an integral part of a story and shouldn’t be
      altered by the editor.
    - If you are bothered by a term or idea in a fictional text,
      query the author but let them make changes if necessary.

### - Editing for multicultural audiences
    - Nowadays many societies are multicultural and most factual
      writing must appeal to an inclusive readership.
    - This means you will inevitably copy-edit the work of
      someone from a different class, cultural background,
      gender, sexual orientation or community. You will need to
      be sensitive to these recurring aspects of writing.
    - One way to ensure writing is representative or authentic
      with regard to different communities is to engage
      a `sensitivity reader`. This is a person who reviews
      manuscripts and checks for for misrepresentation, bias,
      insensitive language and cultural inaccuracy, and makes
      suggestions to editors and authors.
    - A sensitivity reader is not a censor but an essential
      component to ensure diversity and inclusion.
    - To edit for a multicultural audience:
        - Leaving your assumptions at the door – not all beliefs
          and cultural references are universal and
          generalisations about a particular group in a text can
          dsiengage or insult members of that group. e.g.,
          assuming all Muslims follow one branch of their
          religion can put off those that don’t (and secular and
          non-religious people from the same background)
        - Learn about the communities mentioned in the text.
          e.g., despite news reports, only a small number of
          birdwatchers are referred to as ‘twitchers’ – those who
          chase after rare birds.
        - If you have time, try to meet representatives of
          a community or attend an event to gain a broader
          understanding of its issues.
        - Perhaps involve a member of the community themselves.
          e.g., ask a Rabbi for advice if details of the Torah
          are mentioned or engage a sensitivity reader (above)
        - Demonstrate a casual empathy with any group included in
          the text. If it is likely that you would be offended in
          their shoes, then they will be, too.
            - However, even this situation can be nuanced or even
              highly charged; e.g., try not to leave the text
              appearing as if it is explaining prejudice from
              a more knowledgeable position than the people who
              have experienced it. This will seem patronising at
              best.
