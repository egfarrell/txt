# Finding Work

### Introduction
    - Copy-editing and proofreading are very competitive fields
      and work can come in many forms, from one-off proofreading
      jobs to editing a BBC website or proofing an entire novel.
    - Jobs can be full-time, part-time, contractual, freelance or
      involve a permanent position. It is partly up to you where
      you decide to specialise in, but whichever path you choose,
      you will need to gain experience and build up a portfolio
      of work.
    - There are many factors to consider when entering this field
      but the following sections will give a taster as to what is
      available.

### HOW TO FIND PROOFREADING AND COPY-EDITING CLIENTS
    - It could be said that anyone can be a proofreader however
      the devil is in the detail.
    - You will need qualifications, ideally including ‘A’ Level
      English, as well as a number of essential personal
      qualities:
        - Constant attention to detail
        - The ability to communicate well
        - An excellent standard of literacy, with expert
          knowledge of grammar, punctuation and spelling
        - Excellent personal organisation
        - Excellent time-management
        - Good word-processing skills, with an expert knowledge
          Word and Adobe Acrobat

    - The following tips could prove useful:
        - Try to gain experience at your current job or college,
          or from among acquaintances.
        - Is there a company report you could offer to proofread
          or a memo which needs double-checking?
        - Perhaps help fellow students who need a second pair of
          eyes on their essays or theses. The people you know may
          not pay well, or at all, but through them, you can gain
          the experience which could lead to better work
        - Use your personal contacts and networks to generate
          leads.
        - Some publishers will even give a trial piece of work
          for proofreading (though often at a lower rate)
        - Write speculative emails to potential clients. Always
          ensure you have found the correct person to contact.
          Not only will this ensure that you are given due
          consideration, but it also demonstrates perseverance
          and ‘detective skills’
        - Join the CIEP. The society has 38 local groups and
          contact with local peers may yield future work.
        - Network at local business events to seek leads. Prepare
          a description / business cards of your services.
        - Add your details to various organisational lists of
          editors and proofreaders and keep your entries
          up-to-date.
        - Join as many forums and social media groups as possible
          – some may eventually yield work.
        - Join proofreading and editing job boards and mailing
          lists. E.g.
            - https://findaproofreader.com
            - https://www.peopleperhour.com/
            - https://worksome.co.uk/
            - https://freelancersintheuk.co.uk/
            - https://www.upwork.com
            - https://www.constant-content.com/
            - https://www.fiverr.com/

    - However, a word of warning: many of these sites are poorly
      monitored, and some tasks are lowly paid. Even so, these
      sites can help build your client base.

### COPY-EDITING WORK
    - These days there are fewer full-time employers offering
      specific proofreading positions (though _finance companies_
      can be an exception) but there are often jobs available for
      copy-editors which involve proofreading.

### - Research the job
    - Your search will begin with research.
    - Perhaps contact a managing editor or current copy-editor at
      an accessible publishing company to request an
      ‘informational interview’
    - Don’t be shy – most people are happy to share knowledge,
      tips and advice with would-be peers and colleagues.
    - Try not to ask too many uninformed questions during your
      time with an industry professional. Do background research.
    - Perhaps you’re already lucky enough to be employed in some
      aspect of the publishing, journalism or marketing. It may
      well be worth you checking in with managerial staff for any
      internal copy-editing opportunities.
    - Copy-editing is frequently performed under other job
      titles. If you find out what those jobs are, you can apply
      for them when you see in-house advertisements.

### - Learn by experience
    - A copy editor’s job is to be an arbiter of excellent
      grammar.
    - However, just getting an English or journalism degree isn't
      neccessarily enough.
    - A great copy-editor is intimately familiar with several
      style manuals as well as the in-house style. Continuous
      learning is also needed.

### - Develop your skills & be patient
    - As with many careers, be prepared to ‘pay your dues’. It is
      a competitive profession and will take several years to
      become excellent.
    - You will almost certainly have to look for job openings on
      small newspapers or websites, apply for temporary
      copy-editing jobs and work freelance to gain experience and
      references.
    - Potentially take an internship or editorial assistant’s
      position at a publishing house.
    - You will need to show keenness and intelligence, put
      yourself forward for any copy-editing assignments which
      come up.
    - A beginning copy-editor should be open to any proofreading
      opportunities as well. Though a simpler skill, many
      copy-editors need to excel in this area too and
      proofreading is a traditional ‘gateway’ into copy-editing.
    - You will need to be flexible and accommodating when
      starting out as a copy-editor, even if you may have
      a preferred specialisation in mind.
    - The best copy-editors never stop learning. Not only is the
      technology constantly changing, but your skills must be
      continuously honed.
    - Ideally be a ‘magpie’ reader and fact-gatherer, keen to
      expand on your general knowledge about many subjects.
    - This indiscriminate accumulation of knowledge can help you
      spot mistakes and inconsistencies in any piece of writing.

### FREELANCING AND BUILDING YOUR OWN BUSINESS
    - You may covet the independence and variety of being
      self-employed as a freelancer. This is especially true in
      the era of the 'gig economy'.
    - Freelancing can be a rewarding way of earning a living, but
      isn’t partciularly easy. You may have to work long hours to
      meet deadlines, while time taken off for illness or
      holidays may appear as a missed opportunity for work.
    - Freelancing has a steep learning curve, as you work out how
      to price your labour – are you pricing yourself
      competitively or are there hidden costs or unpredicted
      tasks that are uneconomic?
    - Have you the self-discipline to keep looking for work even
      as you panic about a looming deadline?
    - Working for yourself can be rewarding; it often provides
      daily variety and the constant opportunity to learn and
      improve your skills.
    - Each person's method of gaining work will vary and
      specialisations will send people in different
      directions. However the following sections may offer useful
      tips:

### - Reality checks...
    - The field is highly competitive.
    - You will need time and patience to build up your business
      and it is difficult at the beginning.
    - It can be very hard work.
    - Proofreading is a specialised skill your output needs to be
      perfect in order to gain recommendations & a portfolio.
    - There are no set rates – you’ll have to have a strong grasp
      of what your skills are worth in the marketplace and price
      your work accordingly;
    - You need an eye for detail and excellent language skills.
      You will also need to be aware of the mark-ups, various
      types of software and understand the industry jargon.
    - Training is an investment in your future; Consider honing
      your skills with further qualifications.
    - You may find plentiful _marketing work_ – both for yourself
      and for clients. This requires creative thinking and
      planning.
    - Customers have different expectations – be flexible and
      adaptable to meet your clients expectations.
    - Don’t be a ‘jack of all trades and master of none’. Try
      specialising in the proofing and editing you are most
      interested in.

### - Freelancing ‘to-do’ list
    - Your priority is to make a business plan – as technically
      you are a business owner. Budget carefully and
      realistically gauge how long your business will take to get
      up and running.
    - Do not neglect advertising. It is vital.
    - Other freelancers can provide useful information, contacts,
      networks etc. Joining the CIEP is recommended.
    - Continually _research your field_ so you gain an
      ever-deeper understanding of your chosen profession and
      your customers’ needs and expectations.
    - Create a professional online presence.
    - Complete the appropriate training and qualifications.
      Contact the CIEP and remember that all experience counts.
    - Properly equip your workspace and make sure you hold the
      latest software. (e.g. "inDesign")
    - Act professionally – you are a highly skilled business
      owner, so every email, letter and phone call must be
      handled appropriately. Brand your letterheads and websites,
      and make sure any promotional material and publicly
      viewable content is properly edited and proofread. Ensure
      you keep accurate and auditable accounts.
    - _Specialisation_ can be a real asset, particularly when
      starting out. You will need to target the relevant
      customers and businesses after you’ve identified
      a potential gap in the market – capitalise on any education
      and work experience you already have.
