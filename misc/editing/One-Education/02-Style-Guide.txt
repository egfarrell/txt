# Style Guide

### Introduction

- For the sake of grammatical and spelling consistency, most
  professional publications have a set of rigid and detailed
  guidelines which are usually kept in as a style guide, style
  manual or style sheet.

- In publishing, the style guide and style manual are synonymous
  and describe the document containing the company’s or
  publication’s standards for the writing, formatting and design
  of the various documents used in the production process. This
  is often kept as a Word document on an internal server and
  issued to staff and freelance workers as a reference.

- A style sheet usually consists of a summary of the main
  editorial decisions regarding these matters. Whether you are
  copy-editing or proofreading, you should keep a copy close to
  hand and even commit the most important parts to memory.

- Style guides should be religiously adhered to when being used,
  even if there are some grammatical, spelling or editing quirks
  that you aren’t used to.
    - Most editors are familiar with the house style in forensic
      detail and your work will be expected to match those
      standards. In fact, many of your corrections will be
      adapting and changing a writer’s work to the house style.

- However, style guides are not set in stone. The nature of
  language means that writers and editors will often come across
  exceptions to rules and new exceptions to rules and innovations
  in slang, grammar and terminology that will have to be
  incorporated into the house style guide. These are often the
  subject of spirited debate among editorial staff before the
  changes are included.

- Usually, as a proofreader or copy-editor you'll follow the
  style guide you are given, but if exceptions occur you should
  raise them as queries – they may be later included in a revised
  version, or you may be asked to provide an alternative.

- Knowing how to use a style guide is a very important part of
  any editorial freelancer or staff member’s job and,
  consequently, we will dedicate this unit to these guidelines.

- The major topics to be covered in this module are:
    - What a style guide is used for
    - How to use a style guide
    - Applying the style guide to different kinds of copy

### What a style guide is used for

    - CONSISTENCY:
    - copy-editors have to make numerous judgment calls on the
      work they are correcting because every document or piece of
      writing is unique and whatever choices are made regarding
      each word’s spelling or punctuation symbol must be
      implemented throughout.
    - Centrally documenting each stylistic decision as it is made
      is the best way for a copy-editor to remain consistent and
      for a proofreader to check his or her work against that
      consistency.
    - For instance, on these slides, whenever there are
      paragraphs which are presented as bullet points (like the
      one above), the last sentence of the bullet-pointed section
      will have no full stop (or ‘period’ in US English) – this
      is a stylistic idiosyncrasy that has to be maintained for
      consistency’s sake and would have to be put into the
      course’s style guide if it was to be edited and published

    - EFFICIENCY:
    - when it comes to saving time, the style sheet pays off
      exponentially for everyone involved. Whether you’re the
      copy-editor or proofreader, you won’t have to look up the
      same spelling or rule multiple times and neither will the
      author, in-house editor, caption writer, indexer, designer
      or anyone else who uses the style sheet

    - CLARITY:
    - immediately after a manuscript or text has been copy-edited
      or proofread, you will probably be clear on how to handle
      certain stylistic conventions. For example, all English
      county names may have to be abbreviated instead of spelt
      out – for example, Oxon for Oxfordshire, Hants for
      Hampshire and so forth. However, when you’re reviewing the
      document again after the author has made some final
      revisions a couple of weeks later, you may have to ask
      yourself if you’re likely to remember each convention.
      Making sure that you have the discipline to maintain an
      up-to-date style sheet will mean that you will have
      a record of even the most obscure, spur-of-the-moment
      editorial decisions.

- Therefore a style guide establishes the standard style
  requirements of a company’s printed word output and ensures
  consistency both within a document and across multiple
  documents or publications.

- Practices vary, but a style guide will usually set out
  standards to be used in areas such as punctuation,
  capitalisation, citing sources, the formatting of numbers and
  dates and the appearance of tables and figures, among other
  factors.

- The style guide can also indicate best practices in usage,
  language and visual composition, orthography (that is,
  conventional spelling) and typography.

- In academic or technical documents, a guide can also delineate
  best practice in
    - ethics
        - (such as citation of authorship, ethics in research and
          disclosure),
    - pedagogy
        - (that is, the methods and practices of teaching)
    - and technical and regulatory compliance.

- Style guides can be specialised in several ways, depending on
  who is deemed likely to be reading a text; this could be the
  general public or specialists and experts ranging from the
  students and scholars of various academic disciplines,
  scientists, doctors and nurses, journalists, lawyers and
  solicitors, business people and those familiar with the jargon
  of various specific industries. The individual style guide of
  a publisher or organisation will dictate the ‘house style’.

- This variety in scope, detail and length is provided by the
  _‘cascading’_ of each style over other styles, in a way which
  could be seen as analogous to how styles cascade in web
  development and desktop publishing
    - for example, online HTML styles cascade over those of CSS.

- Short style guides or summaries of the house style are usually
  called _style sheets_. A longer, more comprehensive guide is
  often known as a style guide, style manual or manual of style
  and often abbreviated as ‘MOS’.

- Publications such as academic journals typically have short
  style sheets that will _cascade_ over the more comprehensive
  ‘house style’ guide of the whole department, company or
  organisation.
    - In turn, most house styles will cascade over an even more
      comprehensive standard style manual that will govern an
      entire industry or profession.

- The main reference works cascade over the grammatical and
  spelling (orthographic) norms of the form of language which is
  being used. For example, UK English orthography for British
  publications.

- Some style guides will be tailored to specific professional
  areas such as graphic design, which will often include
  typography.

- Style guides are also available for publications that are only
  available online and will govern the visual and technical
  aspects of the webpage, along with the text it contains.

- Style guides don’t just cover orthography, but also correct and
  ethical usage. The style guide might indicate ways of
  describing people that avoid the pitfalls of xenophobia,
  racism, sexism and homophobia.

- Guides in scientific and technical fields cover orthodoxies in
  nomenclature, specifying preferred or generally accepted names
  or classifications that are clear, standardised and
  ontologically sound – these might include biological taxonomy,
  chemistry or physics nomenclature as well as molecular genetics
  nomenclature.

- Most professional style guides are revised regularly to
  accommodate any accepted official changes in style, conventions
  and usage.

- It is the evolution of the subject matter itself which
  determines the frequency of updates and revisions; this might
  be as infrequently as every 20 years for style manuals in
  a reference-work format.
    - However, it can be much more frequent - for example, the
      bird names used in the nomenclature and taxonomy of the
      International Ornithologists’ Congress are often updated
      every quarter.
    - Many houses and individual project styles change more
      frequently, then – with many being in constant flux
      – though most retain their older conventions rather than
      completely replacing them.

### How to use a style guide

- Whether you’re the copy-editor who created the style sheet or
  the proofreader benefiting from it, never underestimate the
  power of _proximity_.

- The closer you are to your style sheet, the quicker you can use
  it. If you’re copy-editing or proofreading electronically, have
  your style sheet file open and adjacent to the document. 

- If you’re working on hard copy (aka printed matter), always
  keep the style sheet within arm’s reach.

- As a proofreader, reviewing the style sheet should be your
  _first step_ as soon as you get a project.

- Become familiar with what’s on it so you know what to look for
  from the minute you start the job.
    - If the length of the document is substantial (eg. a book
      manuscript), review the style sheet periodically (between
      chapters, perhaps).
    - Don’t let yourself get to the end of a proofreading job and
      discover you’ve missed something from the style sheet.

- It is possible that you will have the house style mostly
  memorised, but you will still have to make sure that the
  copy-editor hasn’t highlighted _exceptions_ to the rules.
    - For example, if an author doesn’t want to use the Oxford
      comma, you will hate to discover that after you’ve already
      marked corrections throughout the document.

- While proofreading, you may identify terms or other items that
  you think should be added to the style sheet for the benefit of
  anyone who might see the document after you.
    - You can certainly make those additions, and you will need
      to identify them, so the copy-editor or editor knows to use
      the updated version of the style sheet in future.

### Applying the style guide to different kinds of copy

- Modern style guides don’t just cover printed matter – most now
  also cover online content, which not only includes text but
  guidelines for branding, graphics, fonts, formatting and
  colours, as well as many other considerations.

- This is because a style guide doesn’t only cover grammar and
  spelling, or even _tone of voice_, but is often considered to
  be at the heart of a company’s or website’s _identity_.

- However, in this module, we are mostly concerned with
  proofreading and copy-editing straightforward text, which will
  be inserted into website templates or uploaded to a Web
  Management System.

- The ‘take-home message’ is that you should strictly follow the
  style guide for a given job and not to be afraid of asking for
  guidance and pointing out inconsistencies.

- A style guide can be applied to many different forms of writing
  and will have been adapted accordingly.
    - For example, a fiction style guide is likely to have much
      more leeway for a writer’s creative impulses and
      predilections than one for non-fiction. The same goes for
      a more niche specialist manuscript or a website.

- Even so, whether you’re the actual copy-editor who composed the
  style sheet or the proofreader who will be using it, you will
  need to keep it close to hand, whether in a physical form or as
  an open computer document.

- The closer you are to the style sheet as you work, the more
  rapidly you can deploy it to solve any problems that might
  arise.
    - In the case of electronic copy-editing or proofreading, the
      style sheet file should be open and adjacent to the
      document you are working on, so that you can refer to it as
      an onscreen split or quickly click on an open tab.
      Obviously, if you’re working on a hard copy of the text,
      the style sheet should be kept next to your text document
      or computer or, at least, within arm’s reach, at all times.

- A professional proofreader will review the appropriate style
  sheet as soon as he or she is offered a new project. You will
  need to become very familiar with its contents so that you know
  what to keep an eye out for from the second you begin work.

- Of course, a text document can be very long in the case of
  a conference report or a book manuscript, and in this instance,
  you will need to review the style sheet frequently.
    - You don’t want to get to the end of a job and then realise
      you’ve missed something that was mentioned in the style
      sheet or, even worse, that you’ve been inserting a common
      usage which is contradicted by the style guide.

- Even when you have a house style memorised, you will still need
  to make sure that exceptions haven’t been itemised in the
  ‘small print’.

- For example, if an author doesn’t want to use the Oxford comma
  (where a comma is inserted before the last ‘and’ in a list
  within a sentence – usually an Americanism), you won’t want to
  discover that you’ve inserted them throughout the document.

- In a similar way, you won’t want to include m-dashes in
  a document, only to find that they aren’t allowed.

- As you proofread a text, you may spot terms or punctuation that
  you consider needs be added to the style sheet, so that
  consistency can be maintained by other editors and proofreaders
  who will use the same guidelines.
    - Ensure that you make those additions to all copies of the
      style sheets – usually after okaying them with the
      copy-editor – and indicate them in the master document so
      that the editorial staff will know they must use the
      updated version in future.
