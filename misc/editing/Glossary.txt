### PTC GLOSSARY at bottom

### Backslash:
    - a typographic term for the following symbol: ‘ \ ’

### Baseline edit:
    - the simplest form of editing performed by a copy-editor;
      a lighter edit which involves basic checking of spelling,
      punctuation, grammar and similar issues, but also the
      raising of queries – it is essentially a first-run
      proofread but with more commentary

### Bastard title:
    - the first printed page of some books, which occurs before
      the main title page and contains only the title of the book
      itself

### Bold (verb form: embolden):
    - a thicker style of type, as exemplified by the way the term
      itself is written here

### Bullets or bullet points:
    - a list with each item preceded by a symbol, usually a dot

### Capital letter:
    - your name begins with a capital letter as does the city of
      Winchester’s. Words can also be spelt out in larger capital
      letters LIKE THIS in certain circumstances

### Closed up:
    - this is punctuation with no space on either side, as in
      this way of writing the time: ‘3-4 pm’; as you can see, in
      this case, there is no space allowed between the numbers
      and the dash

### Curly quotes:
    - the standard double quotation marks, which replace the
      straight versions often put in place when using unformatted
      text

### Dagger:
    - a typographic symbol usually used in the place of an
      asterisk to indicate the presence of a footnote, if the
      latter has already been used; this symbol can also appear
      in the form of a ‘double dagger’ for a second footnote.
      Generally, set out as a superscript above the line of the
      printed sentence

### Em dash:
    - this is the very long dash you sometimes see in a text,
      which is often twice as long as an ‘en dash’.
    - They are always used closed up, usually in the place of
      commas or brackets (aka parentheses):
        - for example: “it was—all in all—a great party”:
        - or to indicate speech that has been interrupted, as in
          the case of “What the—”, he gasped. Em dashes are
          stylistic quirks which aren’t always used in commercial
          publishing

### En dash:
    - this is the medium-length dash, as used here, just before
      this first phrase of its definition; it is often used
      ‘closed up’ to indicate a range between figures, as in
      ‘2–3.30 pm’ or in place of a colon, semi-colon, parentheses
      or commas

### Fixed space:
    - this is a space used to keep two words or groups of letters
      or digits together that would otherwise be separated by
      a character space;
    - for example, the case of that used between numbers and
      units in ‘100 ml’ so that neither gets separated by a line
      break

### Font or typeface:
    - the name of a specially-designed type to give a consistent,
      neat look to the letters on the printed page; Times New
      Roman is commonly used in Word documents, but there are
      many thousands of others

### Furniture:
    - in printing, these are the pieces of metal or wood which
      separate the blocks of type; on a layout (perhaps in
      InDesign), furniture refers to the in-built graphic lines
      and placement indicators such as folios and rules, to which
      type and images must be arranged

### Hard return:
    - this is the computer key which starts a new line and is
      otherwise known as the ‘Enter’ key, ‘Return’ key or
      linefeed

### House style:
    - this term always refers to the company- or client-specific
      guidelines for spelling, terminology, punctuation and all
      the other parts of a piece of text that must be adhered to
      before a piece can be said to have reached publication
      standard.
    - The major points are frequently collated as a ‘style sheet’
      which copywriters, editors of all stripes and proofreaders
      use to maintain a consistent style in all written texts
      used by a company

### Hyphen:
    - this is the most familiar and shortest dash used in a text
      and can be used to form compound words (for example
      ‘long-form’ or ‘pick-me-up’; this is known as a ‘hard
      hyphen’. It can also indicate the separation of parts of
      a word at the end of a line of text, in which case it is
      called a ‘soft hyphen’. It must never be used in place of
      punctuation like en-dashes and m-dashes

### Indenting:
    - when a line or block of text is placed further away from
      the margin than most of the main text or paragraphs, it is
      referred to as ‘indented’

### Italic:
    - this is the kind of type which slants to the right and
      appears to be lighter in form than the main body of the
      text.
    - It often indicates the stressing or emphasis of a word as
      it would be uttered in speech but can also be used for the
      scientific names of animals and plants or sometimes less
      familiar foreign words and phrases being used within
      otherwise English text

### Justified:
    - text is considered ‘justified’ if the margins on both sides
      of it are evenly spaced from the edges of the page and all
      the complete lines of text have the same length.
      Justification is what makes most newspaper columns and
      printed text look neat

### Kerning:
    - the lateral distance between the letters in lines of type

### Leading:
    - the vertical distance between the two baselines of lines of
      type

### Ligature:
    - this describes the situation where two or more letters are
      joined to form a single character, such as ‘æ’ or ‘ﬀ’. It
      isn’t used very often these days

### Line break:
    - a line break is when the text is split into two lines,
      generally at the right-hand end of a line of text

### Linefeed or ‘line spacing’:
    - when you start a new line by hitting the ‘Return’ or
      ‘Enter’ key, you create a distance between the two lines of
      text: this is line spacing

### Lower case:
    - these are the uncapitalised letters in a sentence or line
      of text. The term originated from the times when
      uncapitalised letters used to be kept in the lower of two
      boxes by typesetters when old-fashioned printing presses
      were still used in newspaper production

### MOS
    - Manual of Style

### Orphan:
    - this is the situation when the first line of a new
      paragraph is isolated at the foot of a page and should
      ideally be moved to the following page (or the following
      column in a magazine or newspaper) to become part of the
      rest of the paragraph to which it belongs; this is often
      done by adjusting the leading

### Orthography
    - the conventional spelling system of a language

### Overmatter or overset:
    - a printed matter which has been typeset but for which there
      is not enough space on the page

### Parentheses or brackets:
    - parentheses are the collective nouns for the standard pair
      of round brackets used to add relevant but extraneous
      phrases or asides to a sentence (like this); the singular
      active form is ‘in parenthesis’, and can also be used to
      describe phrases placed between commas or dashes

### Pull quote:
    - a short, attention-grabbing quote extracted from the main
      text of a piece of writing to be used as a subheading or
      layout feature

### Roman:
    - this is the usual state of type on the page; that is, the
      regular upright font style (like the one you’re reading
      now) as opposed to bold or italic type

### Run-on:
    - a continuation in the text where two lines or paragraphs
      are consecutively connected that were separated before; for
      instance, in the case where a sentence had been split
      unintentionally by a hard return

### Spaced:
    - this describes a word or phrase with a space on either side
      in which some form of punctuation is used, also with
      a space on either side - typically like the en dash in this
      sentence

### Standfirst:
    - a brief, introductory summary of an article’s contents
      which is usually placed just by or underneath the headline,
      particularly in periodicals

### Stet:
    - from the Latin for ‘let it stand, this is usually written
      in the margin on the same line as a few dots or subscript
      dashes underneath a correction the proofreader has made in
      error.
    - It means that you should leave the text as it is and not
      make the corrections originally indicated. When working
      on-screen, you can simply delete a correction if it is
      wrong, though the term can still be used on the print-offs
      which you are well-advised to use at some point during the
      proofreading process

### Style sheet:
    - this is a summary list of instructions about grammar or
      spelling preferences that relate to the house style of the
      publication or text you are correcting, usually with regard
      to hyphenation, capitalisation or Americanised word endings
      (that is, -ise or -ize).
    - It must always be strictly adhered to unless you are told
      otherwise by the client or editor-in-chief. It is
      a shorter, more practical version of the style guide or
      style manual which serves the same purpose

### Take back or take over:
    - this describes the process of correcting a bad word break
      such as ‘toxifi- cation’ at the end of a line and the
      beginning of the next, where you should either ‘take back’
      the last part to make the word complete in the first line
      or ‘take over’ the first part of the word and move it to
      the next line

### Tone of voice:
    - this is a nuanced description of the public or trade
      perception of your business or publication and can be
      ‘felt’ through the way a text or piece is written.
    - It is reliant on the impression given to the reader and to
      some degree can’t really be expressed as a definition: as
      a proofreader, you’ll get to understand the tone quite
      quickly.


### Transpose:
    - the swapping of words or letters around that are in the
      wrong order. For example, in the common typo of the word
      ‘and’, which is often mistakenly typed ‘adn’, you need to
      transpose the letters ‘n’ and ‘d’ to make it read correctly

### Type:
    - this is the same thing as a character or letter, and is
      a term which originated in the printing industry to
      describe a piece of metal with a raised letter or character
      similar to those which used to be found on an old-fashioned
      typewriter

### Typographical error or ‘typo’:
    - also known more prosaically as a misprint, this is
      a mistake in spelling which must be removed by the
      copy-editor. However, copy-editors are only human and your
      job as a proofreader is also partly to pick out any
      remaining typos and correct them

### Unjustified:
    - this word describes the situation when the margin on the
      left side of the text is aligned but the right margin is
      ragged or uneven. While the spaces between the words are
      all the same size, the lines have different lengths. This
      can be used in some books but is normally used for online
      text and emails because it is generally easier to read on
      a computer screen

### Upper case:
    - describes capital letters and, like ‘lower case’, derives
      from the wooden typesetting cases where the letters were
      kept in the traditional printing trade

### Widow:
    - the opposite of orphan (see above); describes a short last
      line of a paragraph (or just one word) which has edged over
      to the top of the next page or column; such a word or
      phrase should be ‘taken back’ to the paragraph it belongs
      to, on the previous page or column, perhaps by adjusting
      the leading or kerning


### PTC GLOSSARY
- ascender The part of a lower-case letter that rises above the height of the letter x; d, h and k are examples of letters with ascenders. See also descender; non-lining ﬁgures; x-height and Figure 5.
- attribute Typographic styles that can be applied to characters in a roman font, such as italics and bold. See also typestyle.
- author’s voice A writer’s distinctive writing style; for example, how they use words, syntax, metaphors and similes, and emphasis.
- baseline The imaginary line on which the type sits. See Figure 5.
- binding The style of covering and the way in which the pages are attached to it in
- printed works. Binding styles include: hardback (or cased); paperback, limp and B
- format (all of which describe soft covers); spiral; looseleaf; sewn; wire- or saddle- stitched; perfect (glued).
- bleed When an illustration spreads beyond the text area right to the fore-edge, gutter, head or foot on the trimmed page.
- blurb Description of a book on the jacket or cover.
- bold Heavy type. Bold can be applied as an attribute (emboldening), but there are also typefaces that have a variation that is bold, in which case the degree of bold is part of the name, e.g. Stone, Stone Semi-bold, Stone Bold.
- book block See running sheets.
- brief A set of instructions relating to a particular job or task.
- case The binding of a hardback book, consisting of boards and cloth. See also cover.
- cast-off The calculation of the number of lines or pages a piece of text will make when it is typeset. It is usually based on a word count in relation to the chosen page design, with allowance made for tables and illustrations, if relevant. centre Position in the column or on the page equidistant from both margins.
- club line See orphan.
- chapter opening The first page of a chapter.
- CMYK The abbreviation for the four-colour process inks used in printing: cyan, magenta, yellow and black – the K stands for ‘key’ and is used to avoid mistaking B for blue.
- colophon An organization’s identifying graphic icon, e.g. the penguin in an oval. See also logo.
- content management system (CMS) A program to store and feed digital content into a software application or website. Content may be stored as separate ‘fields’ within the CMS and reused in multiple products, making updating them relatively easy.

### More copy-editing terms
- AA                                 : Stands for author’s alteration and indicates changes made by the author him- or herself on a set of proofs.
- Abstract                           : The synopsis of an academic paper which usually appears before the main text.
- Air                                : The white space on a printed page.
- All Cap                            : This abbreviation means that the text is all in capital letters.
- Ampersand                          : The full name of the ‘&’ (‘and’) character.
- Art                                : Encompasses all the illustrations, maps, graphs, photographs and drawings to be included in a text.
- ‘At’ sign                          : The full name of the ‘@’ character, usually prefiguring an email, Twitter or Instagram address.
- Bibliography                       : The list of sources cited or consulted in the writing of a book or paper, which are usually published as part of the end matter.
- Blockquote                         : An extract or quoted passage set out of, or next to, the running text without being enclosed by quotation marks.
- Boilerplate                        : This is text or copy that is reused without any changes.
- Box-out                            : This is text or type that is framed by a border to make it stand out from the rest of the text or to highlight discrete blocks of information.
- Character                          : This describes an individual letter, number, symbol or even a space between actual characters in the case of online text.
- Citation                           : An entry – usually numbered or alphabetised – which directs the reader to other texts that serve as source materials or factual support.
- Clean up                           : The action of incorporating the author’s responses to the copyediting into the final hard copy or computer file (usually before it is proofread).
- Copy                               : A manuscript which is to be typeset.
- Corrigendum or addendum            : A printer’s error which has been discovered too late to be corrected in a document or published product and has had to be included in a separately printed list.
- Cross-reference (aka x-ref)        : A phrase which mentions another part of the same document and is usually referred to in a standard, often abbreviated, way.
- Curly quotes or smart quotes       : A way of saying double inverted commas which are curved and are usually placed around the quoted text.
- Dead copy                          : Describes a manuscript that has been finished, typeset and proofread.
- Dingbat                            : An ornamental character, such as a smiley face, which is used on printed matter (sometimes in a similar way to emoticons online).
- Display type                       : This is the large type used for chapter titles, headings and crossheads.
- Ellipsis                           : The correct name for the three dots (‘. . .’) character which indicates a pause or absent material.
- Em dash                            : The name for the longest dash (‘—‘) character, which is often rendered in manuscripts as two hyphens ( ‘–‘ ).
- En dash                            : Name of the medium-length dash (‘–‘) character.
- End matter                         : The material found at the end of a manuscript or book, including the appendices, endnotes, glossary, bibliography and indexes.
- Figure                             : An illustration or graph printed within and as part of the continuing text.
- Folio                              : Describes the page number in a type-set text. Further to this, a ‘drop folio’ is a page number found at the bottom of a page while a ‘blind folio’ has no page number, even though that page is included in the numbering of the text.
- Font                               : Describes the characters which are part of a given style and size of a typeface.
- Footer or running foot             : Describes one or two lines of copy which are set at the bottom of every page of a document.
- Frontmatter or prelims             : The usual material to be found at the front of a manuscript or book, which can include the title and copyright pages, the preface, the table of contents, lists of maps and illustrations, the acknowledgements and the introduction.
- Galley                             : The initial printed version or proof of a document.
- Gutter                             : Describes the space or margin between facing pages; at the printers, this often has colour tests on it before the pages are trimmed.
- Hard copy                          : Any text that appears printed on paper at any stage of the proofing process.
- House style                        : The editorial style preferences of a publisher which cover almost every aspect of the final published page.
- Justify                            : Items of typeset so that the margin is aligned to them. Book pages are generally justified to the left and right, while other documents can often be justified only to the left (a condition called ‘ragged right’)
- Kill                               : To instruct the deletion of text or illustrations or to edit out a story (perhaps you remember the old comedy series set in a press room called Drop the Dead Donkey?).
- Lead or lede                       : The journalists’ term to describe the first few sentences or paragraph of a story.
- Leading or linespacing             : Describes the spacing of lines in a text.
- Legend or caption                  : This is the explanation that accompanies an illustration or a figure.
- Manuscript                         : The initial text of an author’s work which has been submitted for editing and publication.
- Mark up                            : To insert composition, proofreading or editing instructions into text or on copy or layouts.
- MS                                 : The abbreviation for a manuscript.
- Page proof                         : The printed version or proof of a document in the page form in which it will appear when published.
- Pass                               : The read-through of a manuscript by a copy-editor.
- PE                                 : The abbreviation for ‘printer’s error’.
- Pica                               : A printer’s unit of measure for type and letters.
- Plate                              : A page of images or illustrations separate from the text (though they can certainly have text on them).
- Point                              : A unit of measurement in typesetting which is used to indicate the various font sizes.
- Proof                              : One of potentially several trial sheets of printed material produced to be edited, checked and corrected. Hence ‘proofed’ and ‘proofreading’.
- Redline                            : The on-screen or hard-copy version of a manuscript which indicates which text has been added, deleted or edited since the previous version or proof.
- Reproduction proof                 : Describes a high-quality proof produced for the final review before printing.
- Rough                              : An initial, preliminary page layout.
- Rule                               : A vertical or horizontal line on a page, often used to position text and images or illustrations.
- Running head or header             : One or two lines of copy set at the top of each page of a document, which usually includes the title.
- Sans serif                         : A particular typeface that does not have a serif or crossline on the main strokes of the characters.
- Sentence-style or initial cap only : A capitalisation style for headlines, headings and titles in which all the words are in lower case except those that would normally be capitalised in a standard sentence.
- Serial comma or Oxford comma       : A comma which precedes the word ‘and’ or ‘or’ in a sentence involving a list of items (for example: “one, two, and three”). This is usually only used in US English.
- Sidebar                            : A short piece of writing, article or news story that complements or amplifies the point of a major feature or story.
- Sink                               : Describes the distance from the top of a printed page to any element placed on that page.
- Specs                              : Short for specifications, this indicates the typeface used, point size, spacing and margins, or other technical details.
- Stet                               : This term indicates that text marked for deletion should be restored and derives from the Latin for “let it stand.”
- Style sheet or style guide         : A form or document created by an editor or copy-editor to record editorial decisions applied to the text used in a particular company or publication.
- Trim or boil                       : To reduce the length of a story or piece of writing.
- ‘Typo’ or typographical error      : A misprint.
- Upper case                         : The publishing and printing industry term for capital letters.
