### Viz.
    - The abbreviation viz. (or viz without a full stop) is short
      for the Latin videlicet, which itself is a contraction of
      the Latin phrase `videre licet`, meaning "it is permitted
      to see".
    - It is used as a synonym for "namely", "that is to say", "to
      wit", "which is", or "as follows".

### OnScreen Proofreading marks
    - Use 'stamps' or Adobe annotation?
    - This is a controversial one. Proofreaders now have the
      option of using either stamps that replicate traditional
      proofreading marks or Acrobat’s built-in annotation tools.
      They can even blend the two approaches.
    - Both Aptara and SPi strongly expressed a preference for the
      exclusive use of the built-in Acrobat tools, and this is
      a preference I tend to echo. While both methods have their
      advantages and disadvantages, stamps can make the processes
      of collation and of making corrections cumbersome and
      confusing. In contrast, when the full range of Acrobat’s
      tools is used, most corrections can be made using a single
      Acrobat annotation (as opposed to two or three stamps per
      correction), and, for more complex changes, short
      explanations are often more human-friendly than stamps.

    - However, as with everything else, your client’s preference
      should be your rule of law. Different workflows will be
      appropriate to different contexts.

    - Excellent post! On proofreading, I have one publisher who
      prefers traditional markup but then scan the pages in to
      send to the typesetter. For this publisher, I use the
      stamps and send them the marked pages — saving them the
      scanning.
    - But most other of the publishers for whom I work now prefer
      the Acrobat tools and, yes, it is so much easier for the
      typesetter (or revises proofchecker) just to click from box
      to box in the comments list and find the correction
      immediately — providing the proofreader has used it
      correctly (and not used stamps and other lines/marks to
      confuse the matter, i.e., adding loads of comment boxes for
      each mark!). Although, Wiley has an excellent set of notes
      to follow.

### The copy editor's mindset
    - Butcher (2006): “The good copy-editor is a rare creature:
      an intelligent reader and a tactful and sensitive critic;
      someone who cares enough about perfection to spend time
      checking small points of consistency in someone else's work
      but has the good judgement not to waste time or antagonise
      the author with unnecessary changes.”


### What a copy-editor doesn't do
    - There are certain tasks in writing & publication that
      a copy-edtior isn't responsible for.
    - A large number of professional editors do offer some or all
      of the services listed below, but this requires separate
      fees and contractual negotiation.
    - EXTENSIVE REWRITING OR RESTRUCTURING
        - This is _substantive editing_ and should be done in the
          original writing or developmental stage
    - GHOSTWRITING
        - This is part of the writing stage and should be done by
          a paid professional ghostwriter
    - PROOFREADING
        - This comes after copy-editing and is your last bulwark
          against mistakes
    - TEXT OR COVER DESIGN
        - On small press books or magazines; this can be done by
          the whole editorial team, but generally not
          a copy-editor's job
    - INDEXING
        - Done by paid professionals and is a distinct discipline
    - RESEARCH
        - Done by actual researchers or authors (though you may
          do light research in the form of fact-checking)
    - PICTURE RESEARCH
        - A discipline in itself
    - COPYRIGHT PERMISSIONS
        - Check they have been sought, but it isn't your job to
          actually get them
    - PROJECT MANAGEMENT
        - Usually the job of an in-house departmental manager or
          publisher

### George Orwell’s six rules for writers
    - [‘Politics and the English Language’ (1946)]
    - Never use a metaphor, simile or other figure of speech that you are used to seeing in print
    - Never use a long word where a short one will do
    - If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out
    - Never use the passive where you can use the active
    - Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word or a jargon word if you can think of an everyday English equivalent
    - Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous

### Expletives
    - In grammar, _expletives_ aren’t rude words, but inessential
      words added to fill space or add to its rhythm.
    - Awkward: “`There are` many people who believe we shouldn’t
      drink"
    - Rewritten: “Many people believe we shouldn’t drink.”

### Cascading
    - Publications such as academic journals typically have short
      style sheets that will _cascade_ over the more
      comprehensive ‘house style’ guide of the whole department,
      company or organisation.
    - In turn, most house styles will cascade over an even more
      comprehensive standard style manual that will govern an
      entire industry or profession.

### Proofreading tips
    - Print a copy out
    - Change how the text looks
    - Work on one section at a time
    - Be patient
    - Check for spelling errors first
    - Check for punctuation
    - Check for missing words
    - Only concentrate on one type of error at a time
    - Identify the weaknesses in the text
    - Read aloud
    - Read the text _backwards_
    - Have a colleague check the text
    - Check your work with proofreading software

### Active passive voice
    - The _emphasis_ of the active & passive voices is different.
    - The active voice emphasizes the ‘actor’ of the action.
    - The passive voice emphasizes the ‘recipient’ of the action
      or the ‘action’ itself.
    - Depending on what the emphasis is on, the choice of using
      active or passive voice will vary.
    - Each section has a different goal and set of emphases, and
      you can adjust your use of active vs. passive accordingly.
    - Eg: the `literature review` section of a paper often seeks
      to delineate the most important contributions in the field,
      which makes actors/agents/authors important.
    - As such, the active voice is commonly used here.
    - The Methods section is often written in the passive voice.
    - You might choose to use the active voice in your conclusion
      if you want to emphasize the contributions, results, or
      accomplishments of your research.
    - Therefore, your use of active or passive may depend on the
      section of the article you are writing.
